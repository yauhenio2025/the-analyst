## 1. The dictation, cleaned

It has now become clear to me that once we get out of the Riley project, the Argument block will be super-important, but it will also be
different — flexible, emergent — based on the emerging argument I am going to make.

Bounded the way it is now, what that block represents is me trying to give the piece some structure. I would not say a logical structure,
because we do not yet know how those beats interconnect; it is an attempt to think abstractly about what kinds of things need to happen in
the piece for it to work. We need an argument; we need some logical chain; we need `[unclear: "Firedough"]`, distinctions, mechanisms, and so
forth. The point is that we should start from the assumption that there will be a repertoire of such categories. At the beginning we will
not tie them to each other rigidly or logically. We just need to be able to structure our thinking in various dimensions, and those
dimensions might overlap. We will be working in parallel on different sides of the story of the piece — a little like Rashomon. Some lanes
will be trying to specify mechanisms; some will be trying to specify the history; some will be trying to specify the reception, or the
genealogy, or whatever.

We need to be able to situate those items in the Analyst / Mastermind. Beyond that, we need to be able to understand, from the initial
dictations I make, or type, or paste from my notes, which of those headings will be most adequate for the piece I am writing, and we need to
make regular checks on whether some of them need to be reclassified under a different heading.

This will be super cool: imagine we start working on a piece. We have some notes and we want to continue working in the Stacks. So we paste
what we have already got, the system transforms it into those headings, and it is from there that all the structured work begins —
everything that happens in terms of readings, passes and so on will need to fit into one of those buckets. Once everything is clear, we can
start moving to the logical outline, the structural outline, what have you. But obviously, to work on a logical outline for a piece that is
still moving, with lots of moving parts, we need something much looser, and this loose structure is what we want.

So maybe even calling it "the argument" is a misnomer, because it is not really an argument. It is more like working lanes: the roads that
arrive at the logical argument of the piece, which will then be transformed into a rhetorical argument, because we will need to know how to
present it. We really need to be clear and to differentiate between these three things. I did not have it right before.

These loose lanes will differ from project to project, but they will nonetheless be super important for the whole enterprise, because this
is where most of my thinking happens. We just need to make them explicit, make them visible inside the Analyst, and differentiate them from
whatever is going to make a logical argument out of them.

In Hollywood they say that the quality of a film is judged by the quality of what is left on the cutting-room floor. These lanes allow us to
gather the widest and broadest possible material, most of which will remain on the floor. Before we get from the gathering of that material
to the final film, we pass through proper logical testing — what storytelling tries to systematise in all sorts of schemas: whether the theme
of the film is explicit, whether it is visible. The film business has its own logical analysis, and from it a script emerges; the script is
supposed to communicate all of those themes, characterisations and intrigues in a narrative way. So it is essentially: research thinking;
then, down from there, some kind of logical, structural vision; and from there the narrative. In our case it will be exactly like this,
because that is how I have been working on the Riley piece: thinking about mechanisms, genealogies, alternative interpretations of facts,
the reception, the way you can think of political Marxism as a history-of-science paradigm. Those are all lanes on which my thinking
travels, and it is from those lanes that a logical outline of some kind eventually emerges, implicit or explicit.

And in parallel there is the fourth dimension, which we also need to make very explicit: I have also been gathering empirical support — the
cards I mark for the Stacks, which now land on the separate page called Landed — and other interesting cases: arguments, critiques of
Brenner, which are not only logical but also quotes coming from `[unclear: "AD"]` and others. So there are really four things happening in
parallel. As I work on this piece I am thinking about all four: about the lanes; about collecting empirical materials, loading them and
sending them here (they land on Landed, and I am also putting them in my Google Doc); about the logical structure; and about the rhetorical
structure — what kind of parts to build and where. In my mind there is modelling happening in all four dimensions, and we need to preserve
that.

I am not saying there is a sequence — that we only arrive at the logical part after everything else is done, and only at the narrative part
after everything has been cleared. They clearly happen in parallel. It is just that I am not paying much attention to narrative while I am
still working inside the lanes, because I know the argument is still too raw to be packaged into a logical, let alone a narrative, outline.
But there is already a basic model in my mind of what that narrative might look like, and our approach has to carry that through as well.
So we need to parallelise: assume that I will be working on all four together, and keep measuring them against each other. If more material
lines up in my empirical part that would warrant opening another lane, we should open another lane.

Where we currently show the argument, we should actually show four tabs. In each of them a model of the final product is already being
developed: there will be some temporary logical outline, some temporary narrative outline, and I will be able to go and comment on each. We
absolutely need to cross-feed them one to another: if we add more materials to the lanes, we should be refining and updating the logical
outline; and the same happens if I add something to the empirical part, which then reflects on the lanes.

This is basically how I think we should change the ontology of the project. Right now the argument was the loose bit, but ultimately this
is the brain of the entire operation, because everything else — our bench, our experiments, all the incoming stuff — revolves around those
four dimensions, or those four components. We need to make them much more prominent, and we need to understand where in our system we need
to make changes to make them prominent. That would require an entirely new commission: the examination of the code, the examination of what
would need to change in the front end, and so forth. But right now we really need to solve this at the level of the ontology and at the
level of the structure.

And this has to work for much bigger products too: we are going to be writing a book, and a book has chapters. But chapters are something
that step in only at the level of narration; for the logical purposes, the dimensional purposes and the empirical purposes, chapters do not
matter, and the parts in which chapters live do not matter either. As long as we can disaggregate those four, we will be in extremely good
shape.

So please start putting this into action; clean up this dictation and save it prominently; send it to the Analyst and the Mastermind as a
memo and have them save it somewhere, preferably as Markdown, and put it on GitHub. We need this very prominent, because I think it is the
clearest articulation of what I actually want to build that I have had in the last few months.

## 2. The ontology, in the Stacks' words

A piece (a Brief) is modelled in **four dimensions at once**. Each dimension is a model of the final product, kept current in parallel with the
others, shown as its own tab where the argument column stands today, and open to his comment. Nothing waits for another dimension to finish.

| Dimension | What it is | Its objects | Who writes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. The lanes** | The working headings on which his thinking travels for *this* piece: mechanisms · genealogy / history · reception · distinctions · alternative interpretations of the facts · the paradigm (political Marxism as a history-of-science object) · periodisation · the stakes … A lane is a heading, not a claim; lanes overlap; the set differs by piece and grows. Where most of his thinking happens; the widest gathering; what stays on the cutting-room floor is kept here. | A lane (name · one-line scope · kind from the repertoire · opened when · by whom); what is filed under it: his dictations, notes and pastes (as turns), hunches, runs and their memos, challenges, distinctions, reference sets, questions, units. | He names or confirms them; the desk proposes them from his first dictations, notes and pastes, files every arrival into one or more lanes at once, re-examines the filing on a regular check, and proposes a new lane when material lines up that fits none. Every reclassification is a reaction he sees, never silent. |
| **2. The materials** | The empirical dimension: the cases, quotations, cards and critiques that support or trouble the piece — what he marks in the Curation App (Landed), pastes from his Google Doc, or the desk finds (exhibits, evidence-layer claims, passages). | A material (source · quotation or card · where it came from · filed under lanes · bears on propositions). | He gathers; the desk files each material under lanes and marks what it bears on in dimension 3; an accumulation that fits no lane proposes one. |
| **3. The logical outline** | The argument proper — the structural vision: propositions and what must hold for them (dependencies, tests, what would refute), derived from the lanes and the materials; temporary, versioned, never chaptered. This is where "logical testing" happens (is the thesis explicit; what carries what). | A proposition (text · version · the lanes it draws on · the materials it rests on · depends-on · tested-by · status: draft / his / contested). | The desk drafts and re-derives; he accepts, rewrites, drops (as the premise review does today). |
| **4. The narrative outline** | The rhetorical argument — how it is told: the order, the parts, the sections; for a book, the chapters and the parts they live in. Chapters exist only here. | A section (title · order · level · the propositions it carries · the paragraphs of the position it owns · drafts). | The desk proposes an order from dimension 3; he arranges; sections are drafted alone (the section-draft lane). |

**The three distinctions he insists on**: research thinking (the lanes) ≠ the structural vision (the logical outline) ≠ the telling (the
narrative outline). The materials are the fourth dimension and cut across all three. "The argument", as one column, conflated the first three.

**The cross-feed — the core of the vision (§0.2): the four are interconnected and modelled continuously; a change in one is assessed for what it changes in the other three, by a cheap model, as a proposed revision** (the rules the desk keeps). *Corrected 12:20 against his words of 11:20 and the July dictations (Study 4 §c 28, §d 6): the first draft of these rules said "none applied silently", which would reproduce the ballot pile. The rule is: the assessment runs by itself and is silent when nothing moves; a filing proceeds; a re-filing proceeds unless it moves a proposition he approved; a proposed revision reaches him only when the change moves something, once, with its severity, when a burst of his closes; nothing cascades — his click applies it; the desk's own decisions go to the pile.* The rules:
1. Anything that arrives is filed into lanes at once (dictation · note · paste · a pass · a reading · a card). The filing is re-checked on a
   cadence and whenever a lane's scope changes; a re-filing is proposed, with why.
2. A material filed under a lane is read against the propositions that draw on that lane: bears on · supports · contradicts — the reading is
   what the drain and the exchange readers already do, now with a lane and a proposition to land on.
3. When a lane's material moves (a new memo, a settled distinction, a batch of cards), the propositions drawing on it are re-derived as a
   *proposed* revision of the logical outline — he accepts or not.
4. When a proposition moves (his revision, an accepted edit), the sections carrying it are marked for a rewrite and the narrative order is
   re-proposed — propagation as it exists today, one dimension further.
5. When the materials in a lane outgrow it, or an accumulation fits no lane, a new lane is proposed. Lanes can be merged, split, closed;
   nothing filed is lost — it moves.
6. His comment on any tab is a turn read against all four (effects on lanes, materials, propositions, sections), with the trace drawn.
7. *(added 12:20, from his 11:20 words "changes anything in the narrative or the logic or the headings … affects everything else" and the
   July dictations, Study 4 §c 18–19)* The arrows run back from the telling: a chosen order, or a concept accepted for clarity, is read back
   against the propositions as a proposed revision (4 → 3); a section's stated need — a case of a kind, a quotation of a kind — is a want
   the desk goes to fetch (4 → 2); a proposition's test commissions its fetch (3 → 2).
8. *(added 12:20, the law that bounds the core; his words of 22 Jul: "the structure, the dichotomies, the order of what I present … I would
   never want to delegate to the LLM"; "if it is predictable, then who needs me?")* The desk derives and proposes; a dichotomy, a
   periodisation, a name and the order are his, or shown as the machine's; a coined term is flagged for his renaming.

**Where the categories live.** The repertoire of lane kinds is a vocabulary record in the Mastermind (owner the-stacks, like `reaction_kinds`),
so that other organs read it and an engine can propose a new kind by editing a record. A Brief's lanes are rows under the Brief in the Stacks
(the accepted state stays here, as with questions and inquiries); the classifier that files an arrival into lanes and the deriver that
re-proposes the logical outline are Mastermind engines with their practices, called by the Stacks' jobs. The July dictations on distinctions,
groundings and questions (`the-analyst/communications/vision_2026-07/INDEX.md`) describe engines that run *inside* lanes.

**Scale.** A journal reply and a book are the same four dimensions; the book differs only in dimension 4 (levels: parts · chapters · sections)
and in the number of lanes. Nothing in dimensions 1–3 knows a chapter.
