# Shared inquiry and position map

The collection asks how states reshape market-organized transnational systems when security objectives, industrial policy, and private capital become intertwined. Both texts reject accounts in which states simply command economic structures, but they examine different objects and offer distinct explanatory concepts.

- **`aukus` — “AUKUS ‘behind the scenes’: through the lens of militarised neoliberalism.”** Published online in 2024 and appearing in a 2025 journal issue, its object is AUKUS—especially Pillar II—across the member states and engagement by Japan and South Korea. It argues that AUKUS is both a security partnership and a mutation of neoliberalism emerging through bipartisanship [F1]. Its distinctive role is a critical political-economy account: it treats exclusive geopolitical analysis as “geopolitical fetishism” that obscures social and class relations [F2], and emphasizes selective economic reconnection, regulatory market creation, and incorporation of a new business class [F3] [F4] [F5]. Its principal qualifications are that it does not assess whether AUKUS will push the Indo-Pacific toward conflict [F6], and that Pillar I faces serious delivery uncertainty [F7].
- **`subsea` — “Private Network Realignment: State Strategies Versus Market-Driven Globalization in the Subsea Cable Network.”** Appearing in a 2026 journal issue after receipt and acceptance in 2025, its object is the predominantly privately owned global subsea-cable network, examined through the SeaMeWe-6 conflict and France’s 2024 nationalization of Alcatel Submarine Networks. It introduces **private network realignment** to explain ex post political attempts to redirect market-driven infrastructure [F8] [F9]. Its distinctive role is an intermediate account between statecraft- and capital-centric approaches, distinguishing industrial, regulatory, and coercive strategies [F10]. Its principal qualification is that state influence remains uneven: France can pursue an industrial strategy through ASN, but its balanced position is constrained by US pressure [F11] [F12].

The publication and composition metadata do not establish intellectual succession. Neither text cites the other, and no claim of inheritance, revision, or innovation relative to the other is warranted. Their relationship is therefore one of independently argued continuity and divergence.

# Strongest substantive agreement

The strongest common position is that contemporary security intervention reorganizes inherited market structures rather than simply superseding them. `aukus` says AUKUS “reconnects, rather than deconstructs, the global economy,” while `subsea` examines political intervention in “predominantly privately owned global networks” [F13]. The alignment is at the level of the contemporary economic-security nexus: both reject an opposition between an autonomous market and an all-commanding state.

This agreement has limits. `aukus` calls AUKUS “a mutation of neoliberalism” [F1] and explains it through bipartisanship, state transformation, class coalitions, and selective accumulation. `subsea` instead describes state action as an attempt to “correct” market-driven infrastructure development [F9], with strategic choice conditioned by the positions of states and corporations. The first is a broader interpretation of a security partnership and its social relations; the second is a framework for varieties of intervention in predominantly private networks. They independently reargue a related proposition, but neither presupposes or revises the other.

The texts also resist uncomplicated deglobalization, although their answers are not fully commensurable. `aukus` identifies selective economic reconnection [F3], whereas `subsea` finds continued expansion through parallel and overlapping cable systems [F17]. Physical expansion can coexist with exclusion, duplication, and selective decoupling. The collection therefore does not warrant the stronger claim that geopolitical rivalry leaves globalization unchanged.

# Strongest qualification and unresolved disagreement

The sharpest qualification concerns state capacity. `aukus` argues that state transformation “reconnects business, politics, and military networks in new ways.” `subsea` argues at a different analytical level that “not even the United States or China can simply engineer infrastructure development or fully capitalize on their control” [F14]. The apparent tension does not survive alignment as a contradiction. Institutional power to reorganize coalitions is not equivalent to comprehensive command over a transnational network.

The empirical cases make this distinction concrete. In late 2021, the SeaMeWe-6 consortium voted to award the cable contract to SubCom [F18]. That is evidence of an outcome at the level of a particular contract. It is not evidence that the United States achieved complete network control. France also retained room for industrial action through ASN’s acquisition [F11], although its balanced stance is “increasingly undermined by growing US pressure” [F12].

`aukus` preserves an analogous outcome distinction. Regulatory controls can create market opportunities for selected firms [F4] while Pillar I simultaneously faces “severe delays” and uncertainty about submarine delivery [F7]. Accordingly, an industrial-beneficiary finding cannot establish operational capability.

There is no direct substantive contradiction between the texts on their aligned questions. The unresolved issue is instead the reach of each framework. The collection does not decide when coalition-building and market creation yield durable sovereign capability, when corporate or network constraints defeat state projects, or whether these interventions increase the likelihood of military conflict.

# Collection-level judgment

Jointly, the collection licenses three conclusions.

First, market organization persists within the contemporary turn toward security-driven state intervention. Second, states act through industrial, regulatory, ownership, and sometimes coercive measures rather than exercising frictionless command. Third, outcomes are selective and level-specific: intervention can redirect a contract, preserve a national firm, or create opportunities for favored sectors without delivering comprehensive control or operational capability.

The collection does **not** license a single integrated theory. Militarised neoliberalism explains state transformation and favored coalitions around AUKUS; private network realignment explains strategies conditioned by corporate and network position. The proposition that security competition reorganizes private capital through position-dependent state intervention is a defensible **analyst inference**, not an identical claim made by both texts.

The exact remaining questions are whether these transformations systematically increase military-conflict risk, how durable their beneficiary coalitions are, and when announced market creation becomes sovereign operational capacity. The first would require a conflict-risk design connecting interventions to escalation or war incidence. The second would require comparative evidence tracing corporate influence, state support, and returns over time. The third would require outcome evidence separating announced funding and contracts from completed infrastructure, delivered capability, and sustained operation.
