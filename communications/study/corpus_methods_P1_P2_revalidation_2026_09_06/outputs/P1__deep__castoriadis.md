# Comparative reading

## Comparison question and position map

**Comparison question:** Across the three essays, how are technique, democracy, and capitalism understood as social-historical institutions; what organizes each, how do formal claims differ from effective operation, what role does internal contestation play, and how far can their categories travel across periods and cultures?

These criteria are justified because each text asks how an apparently self-contained domain—technology, political form, or economy—is constituted within a wider society. All three distinguish official or reductive descriptions from effective social relations, and all address historical variation and contestation. They are not, however, equivalent empirical case studies: the principal cases are conceptual approaches and broad regimes, supplemented by historical and national examples.

## Main similarities, differences, and the strongest exception

### From autonomous technique to socially instituted worlds

The 1984 essay’s central intervention is to reject both poles of the familiar antithesis: technique as a neutral tool and technique as an autonomous destiny. Its statement that “Every society creates its world” and that technique is “neither an instrument nor a cause” places technical activity within society’s institution of reality [F1]. The additional claim that “The most important of all ‘techniques’ is social organisation itself” expands the object beyond artifacts and machines [F2]. The approach therefore does not deny technical effects; it denies that technique can be isolated as a univocal determinant.

The 1997 capitalism essay follows an independent but compatible route. Rather than starting from technical objects, it identifies capitalism through the “positing of the economy” as central to social life [F7]. This is a contrast in organizing object: technique is an everywhere-embedded dimension, while capitalism institutionally elevates economic calculation to a supreme criterion. The relation is not that the later text replaces “technique” with “economy.” Instead, it specifies a regime in which technical transformation is oriented toward increasing output and reducing cost [F14].

The strongest shared conclusion is therefore **anti-reductionist but not anti-institutional**. Neither technical objects nor economic mechanisms operate outside a social world. Yet the texts do not dissolve those mechanisms into vague context: the technique essay identifies concrete technical ensembles and class struggle, while the capitalism essay identifies maximization, cost reduction, state formation, and changing capital controls.

### Democracy supplies the normative counter-position

“What Democracy?” introduces a different primary object: who exercises collective power and how that power limits itself. Democracy is defined not by inherited norms or periodic voting but as “the regime of selflimitation,” autonomy, and self-institution [F4]. Equality accordingly becomes the effective equal possibility of participation in power [F5]. This converts the general concern with social self-creation into a political criterion: institutions are autonomous only when those subject to them can participate in their formation.

The 1984 proposed post-revolutionary society and the 1990 democratic economy are normative constructions, not reported outcomes. The former proposes conscious technological transformation under workers’ management; the latter proposes consumer sovereignty, producer self-management, and explicit collective decisions about costs and growth. The supplied anchors support the broader democratic mechanism more securely than every detailed policy clause, so the matrix does not present these proposals as empirically achieved cases.

The contrast with contemporary Western societies is explicit. The 1990 text reports that the public/public sphere is effectively private and classifies the resulting regimes as liberal oligarchies, despite their defensive liberties [F12]. Thus “democracy” as an official label is not commensurable with democracy as effective collective power. This parallels the 1997 distinction between capitalism’s claim to rationality and its effective operation: narrow calculation omits environmental destruction and degraded human life [F9]. The two essays examine different objects—political power and economic calculation—but share a method of testing formal or internally declared criteria against actual institutional relations [F16].

### Contestation is constitutive, not external noise

The strongest corpus-wide continuity is the role assigned to contestation. In the technique essay, choosing among available technologies is both an instrument and a stake of class struggle. In “What Democracy?”, political struggles establish rights and relatively open institutions without fully realizing autonomy. In the capitalism essay, working-class struggles force wage and working-time changes that create mass consumer markets and modify the regime’s operation [F10].

These are not interchangeable causal claims. Only the 1997 text specifically attributes capitalism’s resilience through expanded consumer markets to working-class struggles. The 1984 text attributes to class struggle a role in selecting and applying technology. The 1990 text attributes relatively open arrangements to emancipatory struggles. The common pattern is narrower: **effective institutions are shaped through internal conflict rather than unfolding solely from formal rules, technical imperatives, or economic laws.**

This also distinguishes intended policy from reported effects. Democratic self-management and conscious technological reconstruction are proposed aims. By contrast, wage increases, reduced working time, privatized political decision-making, and technology’s role as a stake of workplace conflict are presented as interpretations of historical or contemporary operation. None should be converted into an independently measured outcome.

### Exception test: shared forms do not guarantee shared institutions

The strongest common pattern might tempt a universal conclusion that every society is constituted through the same relation among technology, economy, and political contestation. The texts themselves block that inference.

The clearest exception comes from the technique essay: “Extremely similar ‘technical ensembles’” may appear in “a limitless variety of cultural and historical contexts” [F3]. Technical resemblance therefore cannot carry social chronology or causation. The adaptation of European wood-chisels into traditional adzes by Eskimos and Laplanders further shows that an imported object can acquire a different technical status through local incorporation [F11].

The democracy essay supplies an analogous scale exception. Athens demonstrates that certain democratic forms are possible, but it cannot be treated as a template for France or other mass societies; the shift from tens of thousands to tens of millions changes the institutional problem [F6]. Tocqueville’s “United States” is also temporally qualified because the text argues that the Jeffersonian situation he described was already disappearing.

The capitalism essay provides the broad historical exception: most societies did not make economic efficiency or output maximization their central orientation [F15]. Thus the capitalist meaning of “rationality” cannot be projected backward or outward as a universal evaluative standard. Together these exceptions support contextual comparison, not historical particularism: comparison remains possible, but only after object, scale, time, institutional ensemble, and local meaning are aligned.

No ranking among technique, democracy, and capitalism is warranted: they are different kinds of cases. ## Supported judgment and most useful missing evidence

The collection supports a coherent position: technique, political power, and economic rationality must be understood as dimensions of historically instituted societies, not as autonomous mechanisms or neutral forms. Official labels and narrow internal criteria—“democracy,” technical efficiency, economic rationality—are inadequate unless tested against effective control, social conflict, excluded costs, and the society’s own instituted ends. Across all three essays, contestation is constitutive of institutional operation [F10], while autonomy is the strongest explicit normative alternative [F4] [F5].

The position is not a universal causal law. Similar technologies can accompany different cultures; ancient democratic forms cannot simply be scaled up; and capitalist rationality cannot be imposed as a transhistorical measure. The supplied texts therefore support a contextual, institution-centered comparison, not a claim that every society follows one technical, democratic, or capitalist path.

The most useful missing evidence would be: bounded comparative studies of how the same technology operates under different workplace and political institutions; evidence on participation, information access, and delegation across polities of different sizes; independently measured social and environmental costs alongside capitalist output; and empirical evaluations of worker-managed production or democratic economic decision-making. Translation research comparing *techne*, “technique,” “technology,” *oikos*, *agora*, and *ekklēsia* would also clarify where conceptual equivalence fails. No conclusion should be generalized beyond the historical and conceptual coverage of these three supplied essays.

### Counter-evidence

- Similarity at the corpus level is limited by the technique essay’s explicit observation that similar technical ensembles coexist with varied cultural and historical contexts: “Extremely similar ‘technical ensembles’ are to be met with in a limitless variety of cultural and historical contexts.” [F3]
- Athens does not establish that identical democratic forms are feasible at modern scale: “What can happen when we are dealing with thirty-million citizens {as in France} or three-billion citizens?” [F6]
- Capitalism’s productive effectiveness is not denied; the objection is that its own accounting and market models do not establish social optimality [F9].

### Open questions

- How would democratic self-institution operate at the scale of modern mass societies?
- Which institutional arrangements would permit workers and citizens to transform technology consciously?
- How should environmental, social, and anthropological costs be measured without reducing them to the capitalist criterion under criticism?
- Do worker-managed or democratically planned production systems achieve the proposed autonomy in practice?
- How do the meanings of *techne*, technique, and technology vary across translations and non-Western institutional settings?
- Which claims remain valid outside the Western European and rich-country coverage emphasized in the supplied essays?
