# Reading the collection

## Shared inquiry and position map

The collection asks whether capitalism can be interpreted **as** a religion rather than merely as an economic order influenced by religion, and what such an interpretation explains about money, growth, labour, innovation, and modern society. All three texts concern modern capitalism, but they operate at different analytical levels. Because the first two were both published in 2001, their order here does not establish an intellectual chronology.

- **`deutschmann2001_capitalism_as_religion`** treats money and capital in modern society from an explicitly sociological perspective. Its central claim is that “capital and the capital form of money” possess “*intrinsically* religious qualities” and take religion’s place in modern societies [F1]. Its distinctive role is to join Simmel’s *Vermögen*, Marx’s capital dynamic, and a theory of entrepreneurial myths into a processual account of growth. The important qualification is that the analogy is exploratory and sociological, not a settled general definition of religion or money: the text says its “aim is to explore the possibility” of the proposed integration [F10].

- **`deutschmann2001_promise_of_absolute_wealth`** analyzes modern capitalist money as a promise of “*absolute wealth, over the totality of human possibilities*” [F2]. It gives a fuller formulation of capitalism’s promissory, cultic, and indebted character and extends the account of innovation to technological, organizational, and consumption myths. Its distinctive role is to explain the capitalist religious form as a dynamically this-worldly transcendence rather than a fixed sacred–mundane division. It nonetheless presents the framework conditionally—an interpretation that “could be capable” of synthesizing relevant research—and leaves open whether the cycle of myths can continue indefinitely [F11] [F12].

- **`deutschmann2022_interpretation_of_capitalism_as_religion`** shifts the defining object from money’s paradox and entrepreneurial myth cycles to **unbounded markets as a global social system**. It argues that such markets “form the core of the capitalist social system itself” [F3]. Its distinctive contribution is epistemic: religion and market liberalism are compared as responses to society’s inability to observe itself as a whole, while money assumes a representational function formerly attributed to God. The chapter also ends with a practical prescription—“not new promises but sobriety and realism” about market consequences [F15]. Its comparison is narrower than a claim that capitalism simply is religion: the religious side is restricted to Abrahamic world religions, and capitalism cannot answer fundamental questions about the world’s beginning, end, or human finitude [F13].

Across the collection, the position is therefore continuous but not static. The two 2001 articles foreground money as *Vermögen*, capital’s growth imperative, creative labour, and myth-mediated innovation. The 2022 chapter retains the religious comparison and the importance of money, creativity, and growth, but reconstructs them through market unbounding, social self-description, contingency, and the conflict between market freedom and rational self-limitation. This is a comparison of argumentative emphases, not a demonstrated chronology between the same-year texts.

## Strongest agreement and strongest qualification

### Strongest agreement: creative labour is indispensable

The strongest substantive agreement concerns the difference between human labour and noncreative productive inputs. The first 2001 text states that “*only* labour is capable of producing surplus value” because it is “the only genuinely creative factor of production.” The second says almost the same: labour is “the only factor of production that is capable of genuinely creative performances.” The 2022 chapter retains the premise in a different formulation: “unlike machines, humans can conceive and practically bring forth something new” [F7].

The alignment is close in object and time: all three discuss human labour within industrial or modern capitalist development, contrasted with machines and other resources. Their warrant is causal: innovation and growth require capacities for novelty that fixed inputs do not possess. The qualification is important, however. The first two texts explicitly connect creativity to surplus value, while the 2022 chapter connects it to the exploitation and marketing of new technologies and products. Thus the collection strongly supports the creative-capacity claim, but the full Marxian conclusion that only labour produces surplus value is directly restated only in the 2001 texts.

This agreement also preserves a condition sometimes lost in a compressed labour-theory reading. The *Promise* article rejects the idea that time spent working automatically secures profitability: “labour must be employed in a *creatively destructive* way in order to be profitable” [F6]. Labour is therefore neither a homogeneous quantity nor a self-sufficient cause. It must be organized through entrepreneurship, cooperation, institutions, and socially resonant visions.

### Strongest qualification: the religious analogy has several nonidentical objects

The strongest qualification concerns what “capitalism as religion” means. In the first article, the object is principally capital and the capital form of money: they have “*intrinsically* religious qualities” [F1]. In the second, the focal object is money’s utopian promise: “Individual command over *absolute wealth, over the totality of human possibilities*” [F2]. In 2022, the focal mechanism is representational: “money as the universal medium of unbounded markets unobtrusively takes [God’s] place” [F4].

These are mutually supportive but nonidentical answers. Intrinsic paradox, utopian promise, and representation of social unity operate at different conceptual levels. The 2022 formulation therefore should not be read as merely repeating the earlier one. It supplies an independent epistemic route through Luhmannian self-description and Hayekian dispersed knowledge. Conversely, shared reference to Simmel, Marx, religion, and money is not enough to establish a simple inheritance relation.

The analogy is also bounded on both sides. The 2001 *Promise* article says: “capitalism differs from traditional religion in that it is no longer based on a fixed distinction between the mundane and the transcendent world” [F9]. The 2022 chapter adds: “capitalism is far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” [F13]. The apparent conflict between calling capitalism religious and denying it religion’s existential answers disappears once the object is aligned. Capitalism can assume some social functions of religion—promise, unity, contingency management, practical miracles—without becoming a comprehensive theology.

## Joint judgment and remaining questions

The collection jointly licenses four conclusions.

First, capitalist money cannot be adequately understood as a neutral exchange device. Across the texts it is generalized power, a promise of open-ended possibilities, and a medium through which global market society represents its own unity [F1] [F2] [F4].

Second, the religious analogy is substantive but bounded. Capitalism occupies functions once associated with religion and produces a this-worldly transcendence through growth, innovation, and promises of mastery. Yet it lacks traditional religion’s fixed mundane–transcendent distinction and cannot answer its fundamental existential questions [F9] [F13].

Third, capitalist growth is presented as structurally compelled but socially produced. Money-capital demands renewed expansion; unbounded property rights enlarge the field of possible appropriation; and creative labour, entrepreneurial organization, and socially resonant visions turn possibilities into innovations. This integrated sequence is a defensible analyst reconstruction, not a proposition stated in full by any one document [F6] [F7].

Fourth, the critique culminates not in another comprehensive social promise but in disillusion and containment. The 2001 texts anticipate taking leave of absolute wealth; the 2022 chapter calls for sober analysis and practical containment of social and ecological consequences [F14] [F15].

The principal unanswered empirical question is whether capitalist myth-making and creative destruction can continue indefinitely. The *Promise* article asks, “Can the cycle of myths go on infinitely?” without settling it [F12]. The 2022 chapter supplies reasons for doubt—ecological damage, social disruption, and conflict between global markets and democratic self-determination—but not a decisive account of where or how the process stops [F16]. Deciding this would require passages or evidence specifying measurable stopping conditions: whether ecological limits, profitability crises, political regulation, failures of social cooperation, or exhaustion of investable innovations can interrupt the growth imperative.

A second unresolved question is how the two 2001 causal emphases—creative labour and cyclical myths—relate exactly to the 2022 mechanism of unbounded private-property rights. Evidence would need to show whether unbounding is treated as a prior institutional condition for myth-driven innovation, an alternative explanation, or one component of a multilevel causal model. The supplied texts support compatibility, not a definitive hierarchy.

### Counter-evidence

- The analogy is not unrestricted: “capitalism is far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” [F13].
- Capitalist market freedom is internally qualified by the statement that it “collides with the enlightened idea of freedom as rational self-limitation and thus ultimately destroys freedom.” This limits any reading of market universalism as realized emancipation.
- Marx’s labour theory is retained only after rejecting the proposition that “the mere temporal expenditure of average productive labour would secure the profitability of capital.” Creativity and social organization are necessary conditions, not optional additions.

### Open questions

- Can capitalist myth cycles and creative destruction continue indefinitely, and what empirical condition would stop them [F12]?
- Is the unbounding of private-property rights a prior cause of creative labour and myth-mediated innovation, or a differently scoped description of the same process?
- How should the conflict between global market logic and republican-democratic self-determination be institutionally contained [F16]?
- Does the 2022 chapter retain the full cyclical model of economic myths, or only the broader role of imaginations and visions? The retained material is insufficient to decide.
- What precise criterion distinguishes a religious “function,” “analogy,” “replacement,” and “competition” in the collection? The texts supply overlapping examples but no single settled taxonomy.
