# Comparison question and source position map

**Comparison question:** How do states redirect market-organized security and infrastructure systems toward strategic objectives, through which instruments and corporate actors, and with what demonstrated limits?

The criteria—market–state relationship, intervention instruments, corporate role, and reported result or limit—are justified because both documents address them directly. The comparison is nevertheless cross-sectoral and approach-level: AUKUS is a multi-country defence and technology partnership, while the subsea study develops a conceptual approach through two bounded infrastructure cases. The criteria support structured contrast, not an overall ranking.

## Compact source-to-case map

- **`aukus` — AUKUS political economy.** Covering the partnership announced in 2021, especially Pillar II and its links to Japan and South Korea, the article argues that AUKUS is both a security partnership and “a mutation of neoliberalism emerging in the context of bipartisanship” [F1]. Its distinctive role is to explain broad changes in state, business, military, and financial relations. It expressly does not determine whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific toward conflict [F2].
- **`subsea` — private network realignment.** The article examines two nested empirical cases: the US–China conflict over SeaMeWe-6 and France’s nationalization of Alcatel Submarine Networks (ASN) [F3]. Its distinctive conceptual claim is that private network realignment consists of political interventions that reshape infrastructures and related markets through corporate decisions [F4]. It qualifies this argument by describing state interventions as uneven and incomplete rather than comprehensive control [F5].

The different publication years do not establish argumentative succession or inheritance. The documents instead provide independent routes to a related conclusion: security strategy works through markets and firms rather than simply displacing them [F6].

# Consequential similarities and differences

## Strategic intervention works through markets

The strongest common finding is not simply that governments have “returned,” but that strategic action operates through market institutions. The AUKUS article states that “AUKUS exists within a system of market coordination that is now linked to security institutions and specific alliances.” The subsea article similarly observes that important networks remain “to a large degree privatized,” even as states seek to reconcile network development with governmental interests [F6].

What persists is corporate and market organization. What changes across the documents is the object and analytical language. `aukus` treats the defence partnership as a mutation of neoliberalism and emphasizes bipartisan coalitions, state transformation, and accumulation. `subsea` treats intervention as retrospective realignment of privately organized infrastructure. Shared attention to states and markets therefore establishes convergence on a problem, not identity between the two theories.

## Instruments vary with institutional and network position

The documents reject a uniform model of state intervention. The AUKUS account calls the partnership an “enmeshment of regulatory control, industrial and defence policy, and wider social relations.” The subsea account instead emphasizes “heterogeneous network realignment strategies” combining industrial, regulatory, and coercive elements in different proportions [F19].

The empirical cases make this differentiation concrete:

- AUKUS uses partner-country regulatory coordination, including legislation permitting controlled goods and technologies to move without ordinary export licences [F21].
- China’s cable-sector strategy relies on state subsidies and incentives for projects to connect to Chinese territory [F23].
- The United States uses commercial incentives alongside threats and exclusionary intervention against HMN Tech [F12] [F22].
- France relies on direct ownership, agreeing to acquire 80% of ASN for €350 million [F16].

These instruments cannot be ranked without an evaluative standard. If the reader’s criterion is immediate influence over a private procurement decision, coercive-regulatory leverage appears consequential in SeaMeWe-6. If the criterion is retention of domestic production and deployment capacity, France’s ownership strategy is more directly relevant. Neither conditional comparison establishes overall superiority.

## Firms are beneficiaries, decision-makers, and constraints

Corporate importance also persists across both accounts, but in different forms. The AUKUS article says that “Venture capital and start-ups have been among the main beneficiaries.” The subsea article stresses the other side of corporate power: firms controlling infrastructure may have incentives to circumvent political interference [F20].

SeaMeWe-6 provides the clearest bounded mechanism. The multinational consortium did not become irrelevant when governments intervened. Instead, its members recalculated the commercial consequences of choosing HMN Tech once sanctions became more credible [F13]. The subsequent withdrawal of China Telecom and China Mobile, with approximately 20% of investment, shows that state pressure can reorganize financing as well as procurement [F14].

France follows a different route. Rather than pressure ASN from outside, the state moved to acquire it [F16]. That decision matters because ASN possesses identifiable productive capacity: a Calais plant, seven cable ships, and more than 800,000 km of deployed optical fiber [F17]. By contrast, the AUKUS evidence is broader and more interpretive, identifying the incorporation and benefit of start-ups and venture capital without supplying a matched project-level effectiveness measure [F9].

## Intended policy is not the same as demonstrated result

The documents differ materially in their evidence of outcomes. `subsea` reports bounded decisions: sanction-sensitive supplier reconsideration, investor withdrawal, and agreed acquisition terms [F13] [F14] [F16]. The AUKUS account combines enacted instruments and observed political-economic relations with announced funding, projected opportunities, and unresolved delivery.

Consequently, AUKUS policy intention should not be converted into proof of industrial or security success. Bipartisanship permits regulatory coordination [F8], and the Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act establishes a concrete facilitation mechanism [F21], but neither finding proves that all projected Pillar II capabilities have been delivered. Pillar I supplies the strongest internal warning: bipartisan support has not eliminated production and congressional risks [F10].

# Strongest pattern tested against exceptions

The strongest pattern is that geopolitical competition generates market-shaping intervention while leaving firms structurally important [F6] [F20]. France is the clearest exception to a stronger claim that such competition necessarily divides infrastructure into two neatly separated US and Chinese blocs. Its policy selectively engages projects associated with both powers [F15].

France nevertheless also limits the autonomy thesis. Its hub position and ownership strategy do not eliminate resource asymmetry: French firms lack the financial scale of US and Chinese competitors [F18]. Similarly, state intervention does not imply comprehensive control in the subsea sector; the article expressly describes its effects as uneven and incomplete [F5].

AUKUS supplies a parallel exception to the proposition that political consensus ensures implementation. Its bipartisan foundation supports regulatory action, but submarine-production delays and possible US non-delivery leave major capability outcomes unsettled [F10]. Across the collection, state activism is common; reliable delivery and complete control are not.

# Comparative judgment

The supplied collection supports a qualified judgment: **contemporary security and infrastructure competition proceeds through selective reorganization of markets, corporate decisions, finance, regulation, and industrial capacity rather than through an uncomplicated return to sovereign state planning.**

AUKUS provides the broader account of bipartisan state transformation, regulatory coordination, and the incorporation of new business interests. The subsea study provides a differentiated conceptual mechanism and more bounded evidence of corporate response, investment withdrawal, and state acquisition. Together they show that states may facilitate, subsidize, pressure, exclude, or acquire firms, but the form and effectiveness of intervention depend on institutional position, network centrality, corporate capacity, and available financial or coercive leverage.

The evidence does not establish that militarised neoliberalism and private network realignment are interchangeable, that all strategic sectors follow the same pattern, or that intervention reliably produces security outcomes. The most useful missing evidence would be matched measures of public expenditure, corporate gains, delivery, resilience, and counterfactual firm behavior; completed implementation evidence for AUKUS Pillar II; and comparable cases from less-connected states. Generalization should remain limited to the supplied defence-partnership and subsea-cable settings.

### Counter-evidence

- Bipartisan support for AUKUS does not ensure submarine delivery; production delays and possible US congressional denial may leave few concrete results [F10].
- State intervention in private cable networks remains uneven and incomplete [F5].
- France’s selective engagement does not eliminate its financial and technological disadvantage relative to US and Chinese firms [F18].

### Open questions

- Whether AUKUS Pillar II deliverables will materialize as projected.
- How corporate gains and public costs compare across the three cases.
- What firms would have done absent subsidies, sanctions, regulatory exemptions, or nationalization.
- Whether Chinese cable firms will remain excluded outside regions subject to strong US leverage.
- Whether less-connected states can pursue autonomous network strategies comparable to France’s.
