# Citation-fidelity reading

## Audit result

The supplied index contains **28 checked pair candidates** drawn from **27 citing texts** and routed to **five held Weber source keys**. Ten pairs can be settled against a page-resolved Weber window: **RW0001, RW0016, RW0023, RW0029, RW0042, RW0045, RW0048, RW0064, RW0085, RW0088, and RW0096**—eleven rather than ten when the undated reuse of pp. 965–66 is counted as its own citation event. Of these, four are accurate verbatim or terminological attributions, five are fair adaptations or paraphrases, one is selective, and one is stretched. No checked pair is contradicted strongly enough to warrant “misattributed.”

The remaining **17 pair candidates are unresolved** because the retrieved material is a search candidate, a different edition or collection, an editorial introduction, only part of a composite citation, or does not provide a sufficiently specific A-side attribution. The index’s other **70 name windows** are outside the checked-pair sample; six of those are note links rather than independent citation events. This audit therefore supports no conclusion about Dylan Riley’s complete citation practice or Max Weber’s entire reception.

### Practice within the checked sample

The settled citations show a consistent pattern of **explicit signalling when Riley adapts Weber**, especially in *Faultlines*, and generally accurate direct quotation. The main fidelity risk is not fabricated wording but **conceptual enlargement**: a specific Weberian mechanism—legitimate violence, imperialist profit opportunities, status monopolization, tax-farming—is made to support a broader account of infrastructural state power, declining profitability, contemporary estates, or asset-price capitalism. That conclusion applies only to the eleven settled pairs, not to Riley’s work as a whole.

## Source-specific reading

### Estates, status honour and closure

The 2003 argument starts from Weber’s differentiation of economic class, social status and political parties. Riley turns that differentiation into a theory of political society as a filter affecting class formation [F1]. This is a reargument, not something merely presupposed later.

The 2025 estate chapter changes the immediate problem. It no longer asks principally how political organization blocks a property-owning group from forming as a class. It asks why wage workers may rationally defend the market value of labour power through race, ethnicity or credentials. Weber’s A180 formulation permits this overlap: “Ständische Lage kann auf Klassenlage … ruhen,” but it immediately adds that estate position is not determined by class position alone [F9]. Riley’s addition is to interpret such estate organization as a defensive response to political capitalism.

The sources therefore do not support a simple “fusion” of market closure and status honour. Weber’s estate definition combines social estimation, lifestyle, education, prestige and monopolized acquisition opportunities, while preserving nonidentity with class. Riley makes material defence more prominent, but the held 2025 passage still identifies cultural marking and market interest as jointly operative rather than identical.

### Political capitalism, imperialism and tax-farming

The 2013 essay begins with imperialism. Its exact Weber quotation predicts the revival of imperialist capitalism [F2], while Riley adds a stronger claim: that imperialism answered declining returns in rational capitalism. The held window clearly compares imperialist and pacifist profit opportunities, but it does not state a general law of declining profit rates. That part is Riley’s Marx–Weber synthesis.

*Faultlines* (2020) is more cautious about derivation: “Adapting Weber’s concept” openly marks a conceptual transfer [F6]. The object changes from Roman expansion and politically privileged profit opportunities to contemporary American returns secured through political power.

The 2025 Riley–Brenner reply then revises the contemporary concept in response to criticism. It does not simply repeat the 2020 formulation. It distinguishes historical tax-farming from asset-price support and defines a new M–A–M′ circuit alongside, not in place of, M–C–M′. Weber’s pages explain the private capitalist tax-farmer, fiscal predictability and exploitative risk [F10]; the contemporary circuit is independently argued by Riley and Brenner. The undated *Thesis of Political Capitalism* generalizes this route further, defining politically engineered asset inflation as internal to value expansion [F11]. Its date must remain unknown, so it cannot be placed before or after the 2025 reply as an argumentative development.

### Patrimonialism, bureaucracy and political organization

*What Is Trump?* accurately quotes patrimonial office as lacking bureaucratic separation between private and official spheres [F5]. Riley’s decisive claim is nevertheless not that the United States has become a patrimonial order. It is that a patrimonial leadership network has been inserted “like a foreign body” into a legal-rational state. The continuing resistance of bureaucracy is therefore evidence of incomplete patrimonialism, not an exception the essay suppresses.

The 2024 relational-power chapter takes a different route. Rather than applying one Weberian type, Emigh and Riley distribute power across material, organizational, associational, structural, coalitional and ideational mechanisms. Its composite Weber citations often route several propositions to several pages, so many individual pair judgments remain unresolved. Substantively, however, the chapter is best read as an independent typology assembled from Weber, Marx, Gramsci and later theory, not as an exposition of Weber alone.

### Meaning, standpoints and alternatives

The 2005 essay uses “suppressed alternatives” to move beyond Millian comparison, but the English Weber locus is not held in an edition-equivalent form. The methodological inheritance therefore remains unresolved rather than absent.

The 2026 *Angles of Vision* develops a stronger standpoint argument: Marx approaches capitalism from wage labour, Weber from the firm, and standpoints are “politicoepistemological achievements.” This is explicitly Riley’s Lukácsian reconstruction. It connects to the institutional arguments by making perspective constitutive of what social relations become visible, but no indexed primary pair tests it.

### Science, values and responsibility

The 2011 Judt essay emphasizes both the contrast and complementarity of conviction and responsibility. Weber’s held text indeed calls them “supplements,” but also calls their maxims fundamentally different and stresses foreseeable consequences. Riley’s criticism of Judt therefore rests on the full tension, not on the final formula alone.

The 2023 Brown review goes further by arguing that Weber generalized a specifically nationalist problem into a universal separation of fact and value. The cited p. 126 supports the conflict between salvation and political violence and the example of Florentine civic greatness [F8]. It does not by itself establish the historical thesis about German capitalism, socialism and geopolitical rivalry. That thesis is Riley’s contextual addition and must be evaluated against wider Weber materials than the retained page.

## Scope and evidence limits

For the **27 citing texts**, attribution kind, attributed claim, source position, source context and edition/locus were not comprehensively assessed across their full bodies. The supplied material consists of citation witnesses, not complete inventories. Positive attribution findings are therefore confined to the passages listed in the audit. An inconclusive result means the relevant criterion—an actual Riley clause paired with Weber’s own words at an edition-accounted locus—was not met; it does not mean the document contains no Weber attribution.

For the **five Weber source documents**, attribution-kind and attributed-claim assessments are outside their role: they are primary windows, not citing-author texts. They receive no positive attribution-kind row. *Political Writings* is additionally unusable for RW0074 because the retrieved words belong to its introduction. MWG I/7 is only a search proxy for the English *Methodology* citation. MWG I/23 positively settles A180 but not the unrelated search-routed composite citation RW0080.

For **paired fidelity**, the inspected basis was the supplied citation witnesses, primary windows and retrieval metadata. Coverage is partial: 28 pair candidates were eligible, 11 are settled here, and 17 remain unresolved. The criterion required both a substantive A-side predicate and Weber’s own words at the cited or edition-accounted place. The principal limitations are substituted editions, search-only retrievals, composite citations and missing exact loci.

For **sample practice**, the same partial basis applies. Linked notes were not counted as independent events, and unchecked name windows were not converted into negative evidence. No conclusion extends beyond the settled sample.

### Counter-evidence

- Weber states that status position “ist nicht durch” class position “allein bestimmt,” which limits any reading that reduces estates to market interest alone [F9].
- Weber’s state definition names legitimate physical force, not the entire package of bureaucratic and infrastructural capacities attributed by Emigh, Riley and Ahmed [F3].
- The tax-farming pages describe historical fiscal administration; they do not contain M–A–M′ or a theory of politically engineered asset prices [F10–F11].
