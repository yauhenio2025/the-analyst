# Dylan Riley’s Uses of Max Weber across the Supplied Texts

## Position map of the supplied collection

The collection contains twenty-seven citing texts, including four coauthored works and one undated essay. Weber’s place varies sharply: he can supply a methodological device, a conceptual distinction, evidence against an interlocutor, an object of historical criticism, or merely an example in a discussion of sociology’s canon.

## The reception spine

### Political mediation before “estate politics”

The earliest supplied use is not a Weberian theory of capitalism but a Weber-and-Gramsci genealogy of political mediation. In **Privilege and Property**, Weber’s class-status-party essay helps Riley refuse a binary state/society model: “political society is reducible neither to interests in civil society, nor to the state.” Riley then converts that opening distinction into an institutional claim about the filters through which interests become claims on public power [F1]. Weber is therefore conceptual evidence and genealogy, not the paper’s empirical evidence about Lombardy.

The extension matters. Riley’s central claim is that residence-based privilege prevented agrarian owners from organizing politically as a class. His own programme studies “the political structures that affect how material and ideal interests become politically effective” [F2]. That is more specific than merely invoking classes, statuses and parties. It anticipates the later concern with how materially grounded groups are politically constituted, but it does not yet deploy Weber’s estate category as the principal explanatory object.

The 2005 article changes the task. Weber no longer supplies a substantive theory of political society. *The Methodology of the Social Sciences*, p. 172, is cited alongside Moore for “suppressed alternatives embedded in historical sequences” [F3]. The move is methodological evidence for counterfactual-sensitive causal analysis. Riley’s authoritarianism argument itself—strong associations enable fascist organization in Italy, while weaker and regionally fragmented associations constrain the Spanish case—remains Gramscian. Shared historical vocabulary therefore should not be mistaken for substantive Weberian inheritance.

In **Waves of Historical Sociology**, Weber becomes an object within intellectual history. Riley’s simplified periodization makes the “relative influence of Marx and Weber” a principal axis: the first wave is Weberian, the second combines Marxian and Weberian concerns, and the third both breaks with and returns to those problems [F4]. The return is productive rather than devotional. Through Gorski, Weber’s religious sociology supports accounts of historically constituted rational agency and large-scale historical transition; because these are Gorski’s readings, they are dialogue rather than direct adoption.

### Political responsibility: evidence against moralism

In **Tony Judt: A Cooler Look**, Weber moves from disciplinary genealogy to argumentative evidence. Judt’s “Weberian calculus” allows responsibility without partisan commitment. Riley answers that “the ethic of responsibility was embodied by the political actor who took responsibility for the use of ‘legitimate violence’” [F5]. Weber here is a foil to Judt and a partner in Riley’s anti-moralizing argument.

The crucial point is not that responsibility cancels conviction. Riley quotes the two ethics as “not ‘absolute contrasts, but rather supplements’” [F6]. Politics still requires commitment to a cause; responsibility consists in accepting the consequences of political means. Riley then aligns Weber with Sartre: both reject a detached morality that obscures conflict, violence and collective action. This is a substantive adoption, though restricted to the supplied passage from *Politics as a Vocation*.

The 2023 review of Wendy Brown reopens the same problem at greater depth. Riley still values Weber’s responsible politician as one who “held himself accountable for condemnable acts and not ‘history’, ‘nation’, or ‘party’” [F7]. But Weber is no longer merely evidence against another reader. Riley historicizes Weber’s fact/value distinction, arguing that its real problem was reconciling knowledge and German nationalism [F8]. Thus the later text does not abandon the 2011 reading; it retains responsibility while revising the claimed generality of Weber’s framework.

That change is established by the paired passages, not by chronology alone. In 2011, Weber authoritatively corrects Judt; in 2023, Weber’s own political situation becomes the object of criticism [F9]. Riley’s positive addition is that values may possess a cognitive component: they can arise from diagnoses of what exists and programmes for what should exist. He borrows the Weberian phrase “objective possibilities” for this reconstruction, making Weber a dialogue partner whose conceptual resources survive the criticism [F10].

### Imperialism and political capitalism

**Routes or Rivals?** brings Weber into the explanation of imperialism. Against Michael Mann’s reliance on militarism and prestige, Riley writes that Weber also identified “a strong material dynamic to imperialism” [F11]. Weber’s *Economy and Society*, p. 919, supplies direct textual evidence: imperialist capitalism is associated with capitalist influence on politics and drives to expansion. Riley interprets this as close to the Hobson-Lenin view that declining returns make politically secured investments, arms and state loans attractive.

The stance must remain qualified. Riley says Weber “never fully developed” the argument and “seems in some places” to hold it. Weber is thus evidence and dialogue, while Mann is the immediate foil. The 2013 passage establishes the germ of a later concern: profit seeking can proceed through political power rather than ordinary competitive investment.

In **Faultlines** (2020), this becomes explicit conceptual adaptation. Riley writes: “Adapting Weber’s concept of Roman ‘imperialist capitalism’, this could perhaps be termed ‘political capitalism’” [F12]. The modal phrase matters. Riley does not simply repeat Weber; he uses *Economy and Society*, p. 917, to name a contemporary US formation in which returns increasingly arise from direct political power.

The 2025 coauthored reply with Robert Brenner gives the concept a historical model and a contemporary difference. Weber is one source in the genealogy of political capitalism; tax-farming is its archetype, since entrepreneurs advance money for politically granted rights of extraction. But contemporary political capitalism is not direct fiscal privatization: its mechanism is “politically engineered support for high asset prices” [F13]. This is a revision through analogy. What persists is politically constituted rent; what changes is the mechanism.

The undated **Thesis of Political Capitalism** makes the authorship of the new formulation clearest. After reporting Weber’s definition, Riley states: “I use the term to refer to a specific sort of property relationship in which political power enters the process of value expansion” [F14]. The new M–A–M′ circuit—money into assets and political influence, producing appreciated assets—is Riley’s addition. Because this essay’s year is missing, it cannot be securely inserted before or after the dated 2025 formulations.

### Patrimonialism as a hybrid diagnostic

**What Is Trump?** offers the most sustained direct application of Weber’s domination typology. Riley turns from the fascism analogy to “Weber’s three forms of rule”: charismatic, patrimonial and bureaucratic [F15]. *Economy and Society*, pp. 1028–31, supplies two predicates: patrimonial office lacks bureaucratic separation of private and official spheres, and staff allegiance rests on personal rather than office-bound loyalty.

Yet Weber is not a template into which Trump fits cleanly. Riley’s key reading is hybridization. Trump practices patrimonial rule “at the head of a modern capitalist state,” an “inherently paradoxical combination” [F16]. Legal-rational officials resist the household network; patrimonialism therefore “cannot be complete.” Nor can its legitimacy rest on tradition. It requires media-mediated charisma. Weber here supplies the types, while Riley’s account of their unstable combination is the innovation.

The move resembles the later tax-farming analysis. In both instances Riley preserves the historical type but refuses identity between old and new. Patrimonial administration inside a bureaucratic-capitalist state in 2018 and politically constituted rent through asset prices rather than fiscal privatization in 2025 are two instances of the same method: Weber diagnoses mixed formations when his concepts are historically qualified [F17].

### Estates, status closure and class formation

The 2022–26 texts return to a problem already latent in the Lombardy article: why common economic situations do not automatically produce class politics. In **Seven Theses**, coauthored with Robert Brenner, workers who organize to protect labour-market value “coalesce as what Weber termed ‘status groups’” [F18]. Credentialling, racial exclusion and anti-immigrant politics are not dismissed as immaterial identity. They are materially grounded strategies of closure.

**The Rise of Political Capitalism** (2025) sharpens “status group” into “estate.” Workers organized through credentials, race, ethnicity or citizenship “do not form a class”; they form what Weber called an estate [F19]. The cited locus is Weber 1922, p. 180—marginal A180 in the held MWG I/23, pp. 598–99. Riley’s addition is to connect estate formation to political capitalism: in a stagnant economy, wage earners fight over a fixed distributive pool, and defensive sectional organization becomes more rational than difficult class-wide coordination.

This does not establish the planning memo’s stronger fusion thesis. Riley does not collapse market situation, status honour, estate and class into one category. He explicitly distinguishes class politics from estate-type politics and says estates are culturally marked but materially founded. “Closure” is therefore the operative bridge, not conceptual identity.

**First Principles** (2026) generalizes the insight without a specific Weber citation. The class is “fundamentally fractured by the material interests deriving from the market position of its component parts” [F20]. Weber here diagnoses the obstacle; Riley’s proposed answer is Marxian and political: a programme must join specific market interests to the common condition of wage labour. The later texts therefore presuppose and redeploy the status/class distinction rather than replacing class analysis with Weberian stratification.

### Standpoint, explanation and Weber’s limits

Across the methodological and metatheoretical essays, Riley repeatedly uses Weber as a benchmark whose power is inseparable from limitation.

In **The New Durkheim**, Weber is the classical theorist who, unlike Marx and Durkheim, grasped “the deep affinity between modern industrial capitalism and mass suffrage” and saw representative government as strengthening the state [F21]. This is substantive evidence against Bourdieu’s neglect of democracy. Riley immediately qualifies it with Weber’s diagnosis of Caesarist tendencies. Weber’s value lies in holding together mass democracy, strengthened administration and authoritarian risk.

In **Bourdieu’s Class Theory**, Weber’s sociology of domination is a macrosociological benchmark, and Weber joins Marx as an authority for treating labour as reflexive, consequence-monitoring activity rather than as a field game. Yet Riley rejects Bourdieu’s attribution of an unproblematic “law” of differentiation to Weber and Marx. Weber is thus simultaneously evidence against Bourdieu and a thinker whose name cannot substitute for causal explanation.

**Science and Politics: A Response** and **Hidden Dogmatism** intensify the criticism. The former says Weber’s denial of general historical laws is “contradicted by his own theory of rationalization” [F22]. The latter attacks the Weberian preference for partial theories without a grand narrative as potentially dogmatic. Neither essay abandons Weberian explanation; both argue that its implicit historical commitments must be made explicit.

**Angles of Vision** (2026) supplies the fullest synthesis. Riley relocates the Marx–Weber difference from rival causal claims to standpoint: “Marx approached capitalism from the standpoint of wage labour, Weber from that of the owners of capital” [F23]. Weber asks when owners can calculate expected profits; Marx asks when use values generally become commodities. Both converge on wage labour as a precondition, but for different reasons.

Through Lukács, Riley preserves Weber as an account of capitalism’s immediate appearance to its agents while subordinating that account to Marxist mediation and class history. “Bourgeois” is expressly conceptual-epistemological, not biographical. The final “Einkesselung of Weber” is therefore neither dismissal nor synthesis on equal terms: Weber is enclosed within a Marxist explanation that retains his calculative insight [F24].

### Secondary readers and indirect uses

Several supplied texts use Weber through other readers:

- Gorski’s Weberian account of ascetic Protestantism and post-Axial change is reported in **Waves of Historical Sociology**.
- Judt’s supposed Weberian calculus is reconstructed and rejected in **Tony Judt**.
- Biernacki’s “return to Weber’s ideal types” is assessed in **Back to Weber!**; Riley grants that interpretation may preserve meaning but argues that procedures remain underspecified.
- Runciman’s pairing of charismatic authority and institutional responsibility is only reported in **Politics as Theatre?**
- Brown’s reading of the vocation lectures occasions Riley’s own extensive historical reinterpretation.
- Connell’s treatment of Weber as a canonical author is a foil in **Notes on the Curriculum**, not substantive Weber reception.
- The four-author **Sociology of Sociological Interventions** reports an Emigh–Riley–Ahmed synthesis of Weber and Gramsci; its passage-reading scope did not support an independent positive interpretation of Weber.

These reader-mediated passages matter, but they do not carry the same evidential weight as direct citations to *Economy and Society* or *Politics as a Vocation*.

The defensible trajectory is not a simple conversion “back to Weber.” Weber’s role broadens from political mediation and historical method to imperialism, domination, political capitalism, estates and standpoint. This is development in argumentative use, not a claim about Riley’s intentions or complete oeuvre.

## Scope and evidence qualifications

The supplied citation index is routing metadata only. Its 27 texts, 98 candidate name windows, 28 checked pair candidates and 70 unchecked or linked windows do not constitute substantive evidence, and duplicated links do not create new citation events. Unresolved windows are not evidence of silence.

For **Privilege and Property**, **Civic Associations**, **Waves**, **Back to Weber!**, **Third Reich**, **New Durkheim**, **Politics as Theatre?**, **Bourdieu’s Class Theory**, **What Is Trump?**, **Science and Politics**, **Faultlines**, **Real Utopia**, **Social Foundations of Positivism**, **Notes on the Curriculum**, **Seven Theses**, **Hidden Dogmatism**, **Reflections on Brown**, **Sociological Interventions**, **Relational Power Theory**, **Rise of Political Capitalism**, **Long Downturn**, **Angles of Vision**, **First Principles** and the undated **Thesis of Political Capitalism**, the recorded outcomes for the relevant document-level dimensions remain **inconclusive**, even where positive findings were retained. The criterion was an explicit, predicate-bearing passage identifying Weber’s place, object, argumentative move, proposition-level stance or local accomplishment. The listed body sections or complete supplied texts were inspected, but review records were generally marked unchecked, malformed, partial, or unable by themselves to establish completeness. “Inconclusive” therefore limits certification; it does not erase the anchored positive passages reported above.

The principal recorded exceptions are:

- **Tony Judt**: citation stance is **findings present** and supported within stated scope; place, object, move and reading remain recorded as inconclusive because the evidence handoff did not certify a complete reception beyond this article.
- **Routes or Rivals?**: the direct cited object in *Economy and Society* is **findings present**; citation stance is **no relevant instance** under the strict criterion because rejected anchors merely reported Weber rather than showing Riley explicitly adopt or dispute the attributed proposition. This is not a claim that Riley has no discernible attitude in the broader passage.
- **The Racialization of Legal Categories** and **Sociological Interventions**: coauthorship must be retained. Their Weber uses cannot be assigned to Riley alone.
- **Seven Theses** and **Long Downturn** likewise remain Riley–Brenner arguments.
- **Relational Power Theory** remains Emigh–Riley.
- **The Social Foundations of Positivism** remains Riley–Emigh–Ahmed.

Some assessments covered complete supplied articles; others were explicitly partial and concentrated on Weber-citing sections. A partial or malformed assessment cannot be converted into evidence of absence. The collection itself is not claimed to be Riley’s complete bibliography, and it cannot establish Weber’s entire reception.

Edition limits also matter. The held Gerth–Mills copy is a 1948 impression while citing texts sometimes reference 1946 or 1958. The methodological citation in the 2005 article is to the 1949 English *Methodology*, while the held German collection is not an established page-equivalent. Weber 1922 p. 180 in the 2025 estates chapter corresponds to marginal A180 at MWG I/23 pp. 598–99. The undated political-capitalism essay remains undated.

## Read first

1. **“What Is Trump?”, pp. 25–28** — the clearest sustained example of Riley applying, combining and qualifying Weberian types rather than treating them as labels.
2. **“Routes or Rivals?”, pp. 491–92** — the crucial bridge from prestige to material political capitalism and the best passage for assessing Weber against Mann.
3. **“Science and Politics: Reflections on Wendy Brown,” pp. 449–53** — Riley’s most explicit critical reconstruction of Weber’s politics, fact/value distinction and responsibility.
4. **“The Rise of Political Capitalism,” pp. 97–102** — the mature use of estate formation to explain materially grounded but non-class politics.
5. **“Angles of Vision,” complete two-page essay** — the most concise statement of Riley’s final supplied position: Weber’s owner-side standpoint is powerful but must be mediated on Marxist terms.

## Judgment and unsettled question

Across this collection, Weber is neither Riley’s master theorist nor a disposable foil. He is a recurring supplier of distinctions that Marxian analysis needs but cannot simply absorb unchanged: political mediation, interpretive counterfactuals, legitimate domination, responsibility, imperialist profit, patrimonial administration, status closure, estates, legitimacy and the calculative standpoint of the firm. Riley’s characteristic operation is **appropriation through historical qualification**. Weber identifies a mechanism or angle of vision; Riley relocates it in relations of exploitation, geopolitical conflict, institutional hybridity or class formation.

The unsettled question is whether this procedure yields a stable synthesis. The later texts argue that Weber’s estate and market-position categories are indispensable for understanding working-class fragmentation, while Marx supplies exploitation, totality and historical agency. What remains unresolved is the precise mechanism by which estate-type, materially grounded divisions can be politically “sutured” into class formation without either dismissing their real interests or allowing closure to replace universal politics.
