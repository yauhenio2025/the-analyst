## Compact map of the complete supplied collection

### Riley’s citing texts

1. **_Privilege and Property_ (2003)** argues that residence-based privilege prevented capitalist landowners from becoming a political class; its distinctive role is to introduce political society as the “filter” translating interests into claims. It is a single Lombard negative case, not a general proof. [F1]
2. **_Civic Associations and Authoritarian Regimes_ (2005)** argues that strong associations can supply authoritarian parties with organizational resources rather than necessarily protect democracy. Its Weber citation is methodological and parenthetical, so the article does not provide a developed Weber interpretation.
3. **_Waves of Historical Sociology_ (2006)** periodizes the field through changing Marx–Weber relations and argues that its advance includes returns to earlier problems. Its historiographical “waves” do not establish a linear intellectual development. [F2]
4. **_Tony Judt: A Cooler Look_ (2011)** criticizes Judt’s moralized historiography and reconstructs Weberian responsibility as accepting the consequences of political violence. Its Weber discussion occupies a bounded part of a much wider critique of Judt. [F3]
5. **_Routes or Rivals?_ (2013)** challenges Michael Mann’s benign separation of capitalism from war and reconstructs Weberian imperialism as materially connected to political capitalism and interstate competition. It is a review essay, not Riley’s final theory of political capitalism. [F4]
6. **_Back to Weber!_ (2014)** presents Biernacki’s return to Weberian ideal types as an alternative to coding while arguing that Biernacki under-specifies interpretive procedure. Its main object is Biernacki, not Weber. [F5]
7. **_The Third Reich as Rogue Regime_ (2014)** calls Tooze’s separation of instrumental and substantive rationality “deeply Weberian,” then criticizes its failure to connect Nazi aims to class and uneven development. “Weberian” here classifies Tooze’s explanatory structure; it is not a direct reconstruction of Weber’s writings. [F6]
8. **Emigh, Riley and Ahmed, _The Racialization of Legal Categories_ (2015)** criticizes fixed neo-Weberian state autonomy and argues that census categories succeeded only when they resonated with pre-existing social classifications. Coauthorship must be preserved; this is not Riley’s position alone. [F7]
9. **_The New Durkheim: Bourdieu and the State_ (2015)** criticizes Bourdieu’s intra-elite, symbolic-power account and credits Weber with understanding the affinity between mass suffrage, capitalism and state strength. Its central object remains Bourdieu. [F8]
10. **_Politics as Theatre?_ (2016)** criticizes Runciman’s reduction of economic and interstate crises to democratic performance. Its reference to Weber’s conviction/responsibility distinction reports Runciman’s framing rather than establishing an independent Riley interpretation.
11. **_Bourdieu’s Class Theory_ (2017)** judges Bourdieu against Marx, Weber and Durkheim as macrosociological standards and argues that Bourdieu lacks adequate theories of class, reproduction and change. Weber is principally a benchmark. [F9]
12. **_What Is Trump?_ (2018)** rejects the fascism analogy and applies Weber’s charismatic, patrimonial and bureaucratic types to Trump’s contradictory rule. The claim concerns a hybrid inserted into a legal-rational state, not complete patrimonialization. [F10]
13. **_Science and Politics: A Response_ (2018)** defends the demand for explanation against Bourdieusian reflexivity and criticizes Weber’s denial of general historical laws as conflicting with rationalization. It is a polemical response, not a systematic Weber study.
14. **_Faultlines_ (2020)** explicitly adapts Weber’s Roman “imperialist capitalism” to a contemporary system in which political power increasingly secures returns. “Could perhaps be termed” preserves the article’s provisional modality. [F19]
15. **_Real Utopia or Abstract Empiricism?_ (2020)** argues that real alternatives must be analysed as whole social orders and invokes Weber’s sensitivity to varied social types. Its brief invocation does not amount to a full reader-argument about Weber.
16. **Riley, Emigh and Ahmed, _The Social Foundations of Positivism_ (2021)** distinguishes methodological from ontological positivism and explains the latter through traditional intellectuals. Weber provides rationalization and the practical question of how to live; the article is coauthored and its publication history runs from 2020 submission to 2021 publication.
17. **_Notes on the Curriculum_ (2021)** treats Weber’s canonical elevation as institutionally mediated and insists that intellectual history requires reconstruction of ideas. It does not interpret a substantive Weberian doctrine.
18. **Riley and Brenner, _Seven Theses on American Politics_ (2022)** defines political capitalism as politically engineered upward redistribution and uses Weberian status groups to explain closure among worker fractions. It is explicitly “provisional and hastily composed” in its later description.
19. **_Hidden Dogmatism_ (2023)** argues that the Weberian preference for partial histories without a grand narrative remains paradoxical and perhaps dogmatic. It names a tradition, not a specific Weber text.
20. **_Science and Politics: Reflections on Wendy Brown_ (2023 issue; 2024 copyright)** historicizes Weber’s fact/value distinction as a problem of German nationalism and argues that political values can contain cognitive diagnoses. The dual date must not be collapsed. [F20]
21. **Ahmed, Emigh, Riley and Yuen, _The Sociology of Sociological Interventions_ (2024)** surveys theories of intellectuals and reports a combined Weber–Gramsci framework for connecting interaction to macro-political effects. It is a four-author chapter and only a bounded excerpt was supplied.
22. **Emigh and Riley, _Relational Power Theory_ (2024 metadata; 2025 copyright)** criticizes Weberian power as too agent-centred while retaining Weberian domination, bureaucracy and legitimacy as partial mechanisms. The chapter argues for relational elite/nonelite co-constitution rather than rejecting Weber wholesale. [F21]
23. **_The Rise of Political Capitalism_ (2025)** applies Weber’s “estate” to workers organized around sectional market advantages and contrasts estate redistribution with hegemonic class politics. The cited 1922 p. 180 is marginal A180 in the held MWG witness, not MWG printed p. 180. [F22]
24. **Riley and Brenner, _The Long Downturn and Its Political Results_ (2025)** revises the political-capitalism thesis in response to critics, retaining tax-farming as the historical archetype while distinguishing contemporary asset-based extraction. It is coauthored and explicitly corrective. [F23]
25. **_Angles of Vision_ (2026)** treats Marx and Weber as analytically constructed standpoints: Marx begins from wage labour, Weber from owners and the firm. It explicitly denies that either supplied a compelling general origin theory. [F24]
26. **_First Principles_ (2026)** invokes Weber to explain working-class fracture by differentiated market position and calls for a politics joining specific and class-wide interests. It gives no Weber work or locus. [F25]
27. **_The Thesis of Political Capitalism_ (date unknown)** develops the strongest formulation: Weber supplies politically oriented profit-making; Marx supplies M–C–M′; Riley adds M–A–M′, in which political influence supports asset appreciation. The missing date must remain unknown. [F26]

## From political society to neo-estates

Riley’s earliest explicit Weberian use is institutional rather than classificatory. In 2003, “Political society” is “a filter in which interests in civil society are transformed into claims on public power,” while the Lombard nobility “tended to act as a municipal patrician caste” instead of organizing around landownership. [F1] This does not fuse capitalist property, fiscal privilege and status honour. Riley distinguishes capitalist rents and contracts from politically controlled fiscal rights; his point is that residence-based institutions blocked translation between one and the other.

That distinction persists in the later estate argument. In 2025, workers organized through sectional market interests “do not form a class” but “what Max Weber called an ‘estate’.” [F22] The object has changed—from Lombard patricians to contemporary wage earners—but the inferential role is continuous: neither a common relation to production nor a shared dependence on wages automatically yields political class formation. Political organization mediates it.

Wright is consonant on the analytical distinction between class and status but adds a criticism mostly absent from Riley’s estate formulation. For Wright, Weber “did not treat this problem of extracting work effort as central to the class relations of capitalism.” [F14] Riley and Wright therefore agree that status and class cannot be collapsed; Wright insists more strongly that exploitation must remain the normative and causal centre of class analysis. This is analytical consonance with a materialist supplement, not demonstrated influence. [F27]

Rundell supplies another consonant institutional route. His Weberian city is constituted through “corporate or guild status groups,” and political development turns on closure or opening in the circulation of power. [F16] That parallels Riley’s 2003 account of political society: both make historically specific representative institutions decisive. Riley’s later “neo-estates,” however, shift from organizations such as guilds and councils to educational, racial and citizenship closure within a fragmented working class. [F28]

## Political capitalism: from imperialism to tax-farming and assets

Riley’s 2013 reconstruction of Weberian imperialism is materially expansive: “Weber’s theory of imperialism” is “quite close to the Lenin/Hobson” account. [F4] Political capitalism there names higher-return outlets—arms, state loans and imperial acquisition—under interstate competition. In 2020 Riley provisionally adapts Weber’s Roman “imperialist capitalism” to contemporary American politics, defining returns as increasingly produced by political power. [F19] The later texts no longer merely repeat the 2013 imperialism claim: they relocate political capitalism from geopolitically expansive investment to the domestic constitution of profitability.

The 2025 texts make the tax-farming genealogy explicit. Riley and Brenner call it political capitalism’s “archetypal manifestation”; the undated thesis then proposes a new property relation in which “political power enters the process of value expansion.” [F23] [F26] These are not simple restatements. Tax-farming supplies the historical comparison; the claimed innovation is the contemporary M–A–M′ circuit, where fiscal and monetary policy sustain asset values.

Ganev is the closest supplied reader, but by a different route. His criterion is that political capitalism be “embedded in patterns of governance,” particularly weak state capacity, fragmented enforcement and privilege rather than rights. [F15] Riley’s and Ganev’s accounts are consonant in refusing to equate political capitalism with any state intervention whatever. They diverge over the decisive institutional mechanism: Ganev’s postcommunist form results from de-bureaucratization; Riley’s mature form relies upon powerful states politically underwriting assets and returns. [F29]

Love supplies explicit interpretive contrast. Riley and Brenner make tax-farming the historical archetype, while Love asks whether it is capitalist under Weber’s own formal definition and answers: “Clearly, the answer is no.” [F12] [F30] This is not explicit opposition to Riley—Love’s 1991 book predates the Riley texts—but it marks the sharpest unresolved conceptual issue in the supplied reception: whether “political capitalism” names a species of capitalism or an analytically heterogeneous set of politically enabled acquisitions.

## Patrimonialism and bureaucratic organisation

In _What Is Trump?_, Riley uses Weber’s ideal types as differentiated analytical tools, not as labels to be fused. Trump’s election inserts “this patrimonial structure like a foreign body within the massive legal-rational American bureaucratic state.” [F10] The qualification is essential: Trump’s rule is hybrid and incomplete. Patrimonial loyalty to the person clashes with bureaucratic duty to the office, while media-based charisma supplies a legitimacy that inherited tradition cannot.

This differs from the 2003 argument without superseding it. _Privilege and Property_ concerns how municipal privilege transforms interests into claims; _What Is Trump?_ concerns contradictory principles of administration and loyalty. What persists is the explanatory priority of institutional form over economic interest alone.

Bendix’s broad reconstruction of domination, law and political writings is consonant with that approach. [F11] Ganev, however, shows the opposite bureaucratic pathway: political capitalism can arise not from patrimonial command inside a strong bureaucracy but from the disintegration of coherent bureaucratic command. [F15] Emigh and Riley’s later relational-power chapter revises the issue again: organizations “work through domination,” but elite authority also depends on information and cooperation below. [F21] No linear development can be inferred from these same-year or differently coauthored texts; what is established is a widening of the institutional repertoire.

## Meaning, standpoint, science and responsibility

The supplied Riley corpus contains several distinct Weberian routes here. _Back to Weber!_ reports the ideal-type strategy of “intensive interpretation of particular cultural objects,” but objects that Biernacki’s procedures leave methodologically under-specified. [F5] _Hidden Dogmatism_ then criticizes the Weberian preference for partial histories without a grand narrative. _Angles of Vision_ offers the fullest positive alternative: “Marx approached capitalism from the standpoint of wage labour, Weber from that of the owners of capital.” [F24] Standpoints are not biographies; they are constructed perspectives that disclose by blinding.

Ktenas’s reading is consonant with Riley’s resistance to abstract individualism but substantially richer on social meaning. For Ktenas, “the intended meaning of each individual action is always social.” [F18] Castoriadis likewise defines “social meaning” as “the constitutive moment” of social-scientific material. [F17] Riley’s work does not oppose these propositions, but it generally redirects meaning toward class position, political organization and historical perspective. Shared vocabulary therefore does not prove inheritance.

On values and responsibility, Riley’s position changes more clearly. In 2011, he reconstructs Weberian responsibility through the actor who accepts “the use of ‘legitimate violence’,” opposing Judt’s comfortable moralism. [F3] In the 2023 Brown review he historicizes the framework itself: Weber’s vocation essays concern “the specific value of German greatness,” and Riley argues that some values—notably socialist ones—may be cognitively linked to an analysis of social possibilities. [F20] The later text does not discard responsibility; it denies that Weber’s nationalist difficulty with fact and value can be generalized to all political values.

## Read-first route and unsettled question

Read first:

1. **_Privilege and Property_ (2003)** for political society and failed class formation.  
2. **_Routes or Rivals?_ (2013)** for Weberian imperialism and political capitalism.  
3. **_What Is Trump?_ (2018)** for patrimonialism, bureaucracy and charisma.  
4. **_The Rise of Political Capitalism_ (2025)** together with **_The Long Downturn_ (2025)** for estates, tax-farming and the mature political-capitalism thesis.  
5. **_Angles of Vision_ (2026)** for Riley’s explicit Marx–Weber standpoint argument.

The unsettled question is whether Riley’s mature political capitalism is genuinely a Weberian reconstruction or a new Marx–Weber synthesis whose asset-based circuit exceeds Weber’s category. Ganev’s governance criterion and Love’s objection about tax-farming pull in opposite directions, and the held Weber windows do not settle that larger conceptual question.

## Judgment

Among the supplied readers, Riley is neither an orthodox Weberian nor an external critic. He is closest to a **historical and materialist appropriator**: Weberian distinctions are retained where they prevent reductions—class to property, politics to economics, bureaucracy to state capacity, values to knowledge—but are repeatedly reworked through Marxian questions of exploitation, accumulation and class formation. His most distinctive contribution is to connect three problems that the other readers usually separate: **institutional mediation of class formation, estate-like closure within wage labour, and politically constituted profitability**.

- Wright’s exploitation critique resists any reading in which Weberian market position adequately exhausts class relations: “he did not treat this problem of extracting work effort as central to the class relations of capitalism”. [F14]

- Ktenas and Castoriadis make socially constituted meaning central; Riley’s supplied writings more often instrumentalize Weber for class, institutions and political economy. [F17] [F18]
