## The line of use across the collection

### Political society, class, and estates

The earliest checked attribution appears in *Privilege and Property*. Riley writes that “political society is reducible neither to interests in civil society, nor to the state,” while Weber says that parties “live in a house of ‘power.’” The objects differ: Riley theorizes political society as a filter transforming interests into claims, whereas Weber distinguishes parties from the economic order of classes and the social order of status. Weber’s distinction supports the synthesis, but Riley’s category and causal argument remain his own [F1].

The 2025 estate argument preserves this concern with political group formation while using a different Weberian resource. Riley argues that workers defending labour-power through racial, ethnic, or credential groups form estates rather than a class. Weber’s A180 formulation says that status position may rest on class position but is not determined by it alone. Riley therefore does not simply equate status honour with market closure. He uses their possible conjunction to explain materially grounded but class-fragmenting organization [F26].

This is continuity, not a demonstrated linear development. The 2003 text argues that privilege-based political organization can inhibit class formation; the 2025 text argues that culturally marked, materially grounded organization can do likewise. The later account is more explicitly organized around *Stand*, but the supplied evidence does not establish an intervening abandonment of the estate concept. Nor does A180 establish the contemporary “society of estates” diagnosis by itself.

### Imperialism and political capitalism

The collection contains three distinguishable routes from Weber to political capitalism.

First, *Routes or Rivals?* reconstructs Weber’s imperialism theory against Mann’s emphasis on militarist ideology. Weber’s cited pages support the exceptional profitability of politically protected opportunities and predict a revival of imperialist capitalism. Riley nevertheless concludes that imperialism followed from “declining rates of return on investment.” That proposition is absent from the cited window. The attribution is stretched where Weber’s comparison of profit opportunities becomes a general falling-return mechanism [F7].

Second, *Faultlines* explicitly changes the category’s scope. Riley says he is “Adapting Weber’s concept of Roman ‘imperialist capitalism’” to name profit-oriented activity whose returns depend directly on political power. Weber’s Roman discussion roots profits in tax-farming, state credit, supply contracts, privileged trade, and colonial expansion. Because Riley marks the conceptual transfer, the attribution is fair even though the contemporary category is broader [F11].

Third, the later texts retrieve tax-farming more exactly. Riley and Brenner identify it as political capitalism’s historical archetype; Weber directly treats the farming out of public tax collection. The historical use is accurate [F27]. *The Thesis of Political Capitalism* then uses tax-farming as a platform for an asset-price circuit. That remains a fair adaptation because the modern circuit is presented as Riley’s extension rather than Weber’s formulation [F28].

What persists is the claim that political power can organize capitalist acquisition directly. What changes is the proposed mechanism: imperial expansion and public contracts in 2013, generalized politically secured returns in 2020, and tax-farming recast through asset-price support later. No order can be inferred between the two 2025 texts from their supplied order, and the date of *The Thesis of Political Capitalism* remains unknown.

### Patrimonial rule and bureaucratic power

*What Is Trump?* applies an ideal-type contrast rather than claiming historical identity. Riley says Trump operated “less as a modernbureaucratic party leader than as a patrimonial household head.” Weber defines patrimonial office through the absence of bureaucratic separation between private and official spheres. Riley places that personal structure inside a legal-rational state and emphasizes the resulting resistance. The citation is accurate because it retains both the characterization and the modern-state misfit [F10, unverified].

The 2024 coauthored introduction generalizes from ideal types to a relational theory of power. Some uses stay close to Weber: the definition of power as carrying out one’s will despite resistance is accurate [F17], as is the claim that bureaucratic leaders depend on information available through administrative channels [F23]. The discussions of command-based organizational control and capitalist firms as bureaucratic hierarchies are constructive but fair syntheses [F22] [F24]. Its legitimacy claim is also exact: Weber says systems of domination seek to cultivate belief in their legitimacy [F25].

Other uses widen the inferential scope. Weber’s distinctions among rational, traditional, and charismatic authority support only part of the proposed formal/informal polarity, making that use selective [F19, unverified]. The cited windows do not formulate cooperation and conflict as a universal polarity of power, so RW0079 remains unverifiable [F20]. The retrieved German window for material-resource power concerns monetary policy rather than the attributed proposition; its irrelevance cannot be converted into Weber’s silence because other cited works were not held [F21].

### Science, values, and political responsibility

The Judt essay offers a particularly careful paired reading. Riley contrasts Judt’s “Weberian calculus” with an ethic that accepts responsibility for foreseeable consequences. Weber says that an ethic of responsibility requires an account of foreseeable results [F5]. Riley also preserves Weber’s later qualification that conviction and responsibility are “not absolute contrasts, but rather supplements” [F6]. The supplement thesis limits the first claim by preventing responsibility from becoming unprincipled opportunism.

The 2021 positivism article uses Weber in two registers. Its externalist genealogy links capitalism, rationalization, and positivism. The cited discussion establishes formal calculability, not the proposition that capitalism produces positivism, so this use is selective [F12]. The adjacent capital-accounting discussion fairly supports the narrower rationalization claim [F13]. The article also accurately quotes Weber’s question, “What shall we do, and, how shall we arrange our lives?”, while discussing the demand that intellectuals provide guidance beyond science [F14, unverified].

The 2023 Brown essay argues that Weber’s general fact/value problem should be specified as a historically situated conflict between knowledge and nationalism. Its checked citation accurately invokes the Florentine citizens who ranked civic greatness above salvation [F15]. That example supports Riley’s discussion of political commitment, but it does not independently establish the essay’s larger claims about German capitalism, nationalism, or the historical setting of Weber’s lectures.

## Checked-sample judgment

All twenty-eight eligible pair IDs were checked independently across seventeen citing works and five cited-work records. Four distinct Weber works were held; the fifth record, *Political Writings*, yielded only an editor’s introduction. Twenty-one pairs are settled: **eight accurate, eight fair, three selective, and two stretched**. Seven remain unverifiable because of irrelevant search windows, language or edition mismatch, editorial voice, or unheld component works. No checked pair is demonstrably misattributed.

Within this bounded sample, Riley and his coauthors are most reliable when citing definitions, exact distinctions, or concrete examples: power, legitimacy, patrimonial office, responsibility, science’s practical limit, status situation, and tax-farming. Explicit adaptation also produces a fair result in the checked Roman-imperialism pair [F29]. The clearest weakness is the unmarked extension from Weber’s imperialist-profit argument to declining rates of return [F7]. This conclusion applies only to the checked sample, not to Riley’s complete citation practice or Weber’s entire reception.

The ninety-eight candidate name windows include seventy unchecked or linked windows. Six of those are duplicate note links, not additional citation events. They support neither positive verdicts nor claims of silence. No scholarly citation counts were supplied.
