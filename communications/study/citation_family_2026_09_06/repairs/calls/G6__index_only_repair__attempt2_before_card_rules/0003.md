## The line of use across the collection

### Political society, class, and estates

The earliest checked attribution appears in *Privilege and Property*. Riley writes that “political society is reducible neither to interests in civil society, nor to the state,” while Weber says that parties “live in a house of ‘power.’” The objects are not identical: Riley theorizes political society as a filter transforming interests into claims, whereas Weber distinguishes parties from the economic order of classes and the social order of status. Weber’s distinction supports the synthesis, but Riley’s category and causal argument remain his own [F1].

The 2025 estate argument preserves the same concern with political group formation but uses a different Weberian resource. Riley now argues that workers defending labour-power through racial, ethnic, or credential groups form estates rather than a class. Weber’s A180 formulation says that status position may rest on class position but is not determined by it alone. It also joins social esteem to monopolized acquisition opportunities. Riley therefore does not simply identify status honour with market closure: he uses their possible conjunction to explain materially rational but class-fragmenting organization [F26].

This is continuity rather than a demonstrated linear development. The 2003 text argues that privilege-based political organization can inhibit class formation; the 2025 text argues that culturally marked, materially grounded organization can do likewise. The later account is more explicitly organized around *Stand*, but the supplied evidence does not establish that Riley abandoned and then recovered an estate concept, nor does Weber’s A180 passage establish the contemporary “society of estates” diagnosis.

### Imperialism and political capitalism

The collection contains three distinguishable routes from Weber to political capitalism.

First, *Routes or Rivals?* reconstructs Weber’s imperialism theory against Mann’s emphasis on militarist ideology. Weber’s cited pages do support the exceptional profitability of politically protected opportunities—arms contracts, monopoly concessions, loans, and occupied territories—and predict a revival of imperialist capitalism. Riley nevertheless concludes that imperialism followed from “declining rates of return on investment.” That proposition is absent from the cited window. The attribution is therefore stretched at precisely the point where Riley turns Weber’s comparison of profit opportunities into a general falling-return mechanism [F7].

Second, *Faultlines* explicitly changes the scope. Riley says he is “Adapting Weber’s concept of Roman ‘imperialist capitalism’” to name profit-oriented activity whose returns depend directly on political power. Weber’s Roman discussion roots profits in tax-farming, state credit, supply contracts, privileged trade, and colonial expansion. Because Riley signals rather than conceals the conceptual transfer, the attribution is fair even though the contemporary category is broader [F11].

Third, the later texts retrieve tax-farming more exactly. Riley and Brenner describe entrepreneurs advancing money to a ruler for collection rights and profiting from what they can extract above the payment. Weber’s discussion directly treats farming out tax collection and the farmer’s share of the surplus. The historical archetype is accurate [F27]. *The Thesis of Political Capitalism* then uses that power-based acquisition as a platform for an asset-price circuit; this remains a fair adaptation only because the modern circuit is visibly Riley’s extension rather than attributed verbatim to Weber [F28].

What persists is the claim that political power can directly organize capitalist acquisition. What changes is the proposed mechanism: imperial expansion and public contracts in 2013, a generalized category of politically generated returns in 2020, and tax-farming recast through asset-price support in the later texts. The same-year order of the two 2025 publications cannot be inferred from their order in the supplied materials, and the date of *The Thesis of Political Capitalism* is unknown.

### Patrimonial rule and bureaucratic power

*What Is Trump?* applies an ideal-type contrast rather than claiming historical identity. Riley says Trump operated “less as a modernbureaucratic party leader than as a patrimonial household head.” Weber defines the patrimonial office by the absence of a bureaucratic separation between private and official spheres. Riley then places this personal structure inside a massive legal-rational state and emphasizes the resulting resistance. The citation is accurate because it retains both the positive characterization and the modern-state misfit [F10].

The 2024 coauthored introduction generalizes from ideal types to a relational theory of power. Some uses remain close to Weber: the definition of power as carrying out one’s will despite resistance is accurate [F17], as is the claim that bureaucratic leaders depend on information available through administrative channels [F23]. The discussions of command-based organizational control and capitalist firms as bureaucratic hierarchies are constructive but fair syntheses [F22, F24]. The legitimacy claim is also exact: Weber says systems of domination seek to cultivate belief in their legitimacy [F25].

Other uses widen the inferential scope. Weber’s distinctions among rational, traditional, and charismatic authority support but do not exhaust the proposed formal/informal polarity, making that use selective [F19]. The cited windows do not formulate cooperation and conflict as a universal polarity of power, so that pair remains unverifiable [F20]. Likewise, the retrieved German window for material-resource power concerns monetary policy rather than the attributed proposition; its failure cannot be converted into Weber’s silence because other cited works were not held [F21].

### Science, values, and political responsibility

The Judt essay is a particularly careful paired reading. Riley contrasts Judt’s “Weberian calculus” with an ethic that accepts responsibility for political violence and its foreseeable consequences. Weber indeed says that an ethic of responsibility requires an account of foreseeable results [F5]. Riley also preserves Weber’s later qualification that conviction and responsibility are “not absolute contrasts, but rather supplements” [F6]. The second claim is not merely repeated vocabulary: it limits the first by preventing responsibility from becoming unprincipled opportunism.

The 2021 positivism article uses Weber in two different registers. Its externalist genealogy links capitalism, rationalization, and positivism. The cited discussion of formal rationality establishes calculability, not the proposition that capitalism produces positivism; this use is selective [F12]. The adjacent discussion of capital accounting more fairly supports the narrower rationalization claim [F13]. By contrast, the article’s quotation of “What shall we do, and, how shall we arrange our lives?” accurately places Weber’s question at the limit of scientific teaching [F14].

The 2023 Brown essay then argues that Weber’s general fact/value problem should be specified as a historically situated conflict between knowledge and nationalism. Its checked citation accurately invokes the Florentine citizens who ranked their city’s greatness above salvation [F15]. That example supports Riley’s reading of politically responsible commitment, but it does not by itself establish the essay’s broader claims about German capitalism, nationalism, or Weber’s political setting. Those are Riley’s independent historical argument.

## Checked-sample judgment

All twenty-eight eligible pair IDs were checked independently across seventeen citing works and five cited-work records. Four distinct Weber works were held; the fifth cited-work record, *Political Writings*, yielded only an editor’s introduction. Twenty-two pairs are settled: **nine accurate, ten fair, two selective, and one stretched**. Six remain unverifiable because of irrelevant search windows, language or edition mismatch, editorial voice, or unheld component works. No checked pair is demonstrably misattributed.

Within this bounded sample, Riley and his coauthors are most reliable when citing definitions, exact distinctions, or concrete examples: power, legitimacy, patrimonial office, responsibility, science’s limits, status situation, and tax-farming. Explicitly marked adaptations also tend to remain fair. The main weaknesses arise when a larger synthesis is allowed to appear as though it followed directly from Weber—most clearly the declining-profit account of imperialism, and less sharply the positivism genealogy and generalized power polarities [F29].

This judgment applies only to the checked sample. The ninety-eight candidate name windows include seventy unchecked or linked windows, six of which are duplicate note links rather than separate citation events. They were not converted into either positive verdicts or claims of silence. No scholarly citation counts were supplied.

## Read first

1. **RW0016 → RW0045 → RW0088 → RW0096**: declining-profit overreach, explicit adaptation, accurate tax-farming, and the independent asset-price extension.
2. **RW0001 → RW0085**: the continuity between political organization’s autonomy and the later class/estate distinction.
3. **RW0042 → RW0081–RW0084**: patrimonial personal rule, bureaucratic command, official information, and legitimacy.
4. **RW0010–RW0011 → RW0050 → RW0064**: foreseeable consequences, the supplement thesis, science’s limits, and political commitment.

The unsettled question is whether the unchecked Riley corpus contains direct Weber citations capable of supporting the stronger contemporary “society of estates” diagnosis. The held A180 passage makes Riley’s class/status conjunction conceptually possible but does not establish that historical thesis.
