## Paired audit

### Estates, status honour, and closure

The earliest checked use treats Weber’s parties as a distinct structure of power between state and civil society. Weber indeed separates parties from both economic class and social status, although Riley’s broader term “political society” is a synthesis with Gramsci rather than Weber’s own label [F1].

The 2025 estate argument is also substantially faithful. Weber defines status situation through social esteem, life conduct, education, ancestry or occupation, and monopolized acquisition opportunities; he expressly says that it may rest on class position without being determined by class alone [F26]. Riley therefore does not simply fuse status honour with market closure. He reargues a connection: culturally marked organization can defend material market interests. Weber supplies the conceptual possibility, while Riley supplies the contemporary political mechanism.

### Political capitalism, imperialism, and tax-farming

Three distinct uses should not be collapsed. In 2013 Riley reconstructs an imperialism theory from Weber’s discussion of politically protected profits. Weber supports the contrast between imperialist and pacifist profit opportunities and the prognosis favouring imperialist capitalism. He does **not**, in the held p. 919 window, state that rational capitalism suffers declining profit rates. That additional step makes the attribution stretched [F7].

In 2020 Riley explicitly announces an adaptation: political capitalism becomes any profit-oriented activity whose returns arise largely from direct political power. Weber’s Roman category does say that tax-farmers, state creditors, suppliers, privileged traders, and colonial capitalists profit through political expansion. The adaptation is fair because it is marked as such, though broader than Weber’s category [F11].

The later tax-farming citations are more exact. Weber says that public tax collection could be farmed out and that the private farmer’s profit was the surplus above the guaranteed payment. Riley and Brenner accurately use this as the historical archetype before developing their own asset-price account [F27–F28]. Thus the collection shows continuity in treating political power as a source of acquisition, but revision in the proposed contemporary mechanism: imperial contracts and conquest in 2013, generalized politically secured returns in 2020, and tax-farming/asset inflation in the later texts.

### Patrimonialism, bureaucracy, and political organization

The Trump analysis is closely anchored. Weber contrasts patrimonial office, where public power is treated as the ruler’s personal property, with bureaucratic office separated from private life. Riley retains both sides of that contrast and calls Trump a “patrimonial misfit,” not a fully patrimonial regime [F10].

The coauthored 2024 introduction makes wider synthetic use of Weber. Its definition of power closely reproduces Weber [F17]; its treatment of organizational command, jurisdiction, expertise, and official information is generally fair [F22–F24]. Its claim that power has a general “consensuality” polarity, however, is not settled by windows that distinguish bureaucracy, patriarchal loyalty, and charisma. That pair remains unverifiable rather than false [F20].

### Science, values, and responsibility

Riley’s critique of Judt is well supported. Weber distinguishes an ethic responsible for foreseeable consequences from an ethic of ultimate ends, but later says the two are supplements rather than absolute opposites. Riley preserves both propositions [F5–F6].

The 2021 article accurately quotes Weber’s question—“What shall we do, and, how shall we arrange our lives?”—as a question science cannot answer [F15]. The 2023 Brown essay also accurately invokes Weber’s praise of Florentines who valued civic greatness above salvation [F16]. Yet Riley’s historical interpretation of Weber’s nationalism is an independent argument; the checked p. 126 citation alone establishes the example, not the whole contextual thesis.

## Coverage and practice within the checked sample

Twenty-eight eligible pair IDs were checked across seventeen citing documents and five cited-work records. Four distinct Weber works were physically held, but the *Political Writings* retrieval was editorial rather than Weber’s voice. Twenty-one pairs receive settled fidelity judgments: eight accurate, eight fair, three selective, and two stretched. Seven are unverifiable because of search-window irrelevance, edition/language mismatch, editorial voice, or unchecked component works. None is demonstrably misattributed.

The bounded pattern is cautious adaptation rather than simple quotation. Riley and his coauthors are strongest when citing definitions and explicit contrasts—power, patrimonial office, legitimacy, science’s limits, status situation, and tax-farming. The weaker cases occur when Weber is made to support a larger historical or theoretical synthesis: declining profit rates, externalist positivism, generalized power polarities, and cross-edition methodological claims. This conclusion applies only to these twenty-eight indexed pairs, not to Riley’s complete citation practice or Weber’s entire reception.

## Read-first route

1. Read RW0016, RW0045, RW0088, and RW0096 together to distinguish imperialism, explicit adaptation, historical tax-farming, and contemporary asset capitalism.
2. Read RW0001 and RW0085 to keep party, class, status, honour, and closure analytically separate.
3. Read RW0042 beside RW0081–RW0083 for the movement from patrimonial personal rule to bureaucratic organization.
4. Read RW0010–RW0011, RW0050, and RW0064 for Riley’s changing use of Weber on science, values, and political responsibility.

The main unsettled question is whether the wider, unchecked Riley corpus supplies direct Weberian support for the claimed movement from market-position fragmentation to a contemporary “society of estates.” The held A180 passage makes that development conceptually possible but does not itself establish Riley’s historical diagnosis.
