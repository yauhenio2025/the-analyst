# Reading

## Position map

The supplied corpus contains two texts, published in 2001 and 2022. Publication dates establish their order; the sources do not separately establish composition dates.

- **“The Promise of Absolute Wealth: Capitalism AS A Religion?” (2001)** develops Benjamin’s thesis into an account of capitalist dynamics. Its central claim is that money embodies the promise of “absolute wealth,” while entrepreneurial innovation and recurrent economic myths attempt to redeem that promise. Its distinctive role is to synthesize Simmel’s account of money, Marx’s theory of capital, and Schumpeter’s theory of innovation. The qualification is fundamental: capitalism is not identical to traditional religion. Its transcendence is historically dynamized, and its myths undergo generation, institutionalization and decline rather than claiming permanent validity [F1].
- **“The Interpretation of Capitalism as Religion – Justification, Epistemic Possibilities, Critique” (2022)** recasts the comparison around the social unbounding of markets and the resulting intransparency of world society. Money now appears chiefly as a cipher for social unity and a means of managing contingency formerly represented by God. Its distinctive role is comparative and epistemological, drawing particularly on Luhmann and Hayek. It limits the comparison both to specified religious traditions and to specified functions: capitalism cannot answer religion’s ultimate questions [F2].

The main continuity is therefore not an unchanged definition of capitalism as religion. Both texts argue that money exceeds its conventional economic functions and that capitalist expansion temporalizes transcendence into an open-ended process. The major change is explanatory emphasis: the 2001 text derives this result through money’s paradox, capital, creative labour, entrepreneurship and economic myths; the 2022 text derives it through unbounded markets, world society’s intransparency, self-description and private-property claims over creative capacities.

## From a dynamic religion of capital to a comparative sociology

The 2001 article’s formulation is already qualified. Although it announces that it “takes up and elaborates Benjamin’s idea,” it does not merely repeat the proposition that capitalism is religion [F6]. Its local argument identifies a structural analogy and then prices the difference. Traditional religions rest on a fixed distinction between mundane existence and transcendence; capitalism turns transcendence into repeated construction and surpassing of limits. Economic myths accordingly have a cycle of “generation-diffusion-institutionalization and decline” [F8]. This makes instability constitutive of the capitalist form rather than evidence that the analogy has failed.

The 2022 phrase “something like a comparative perspective on capitalism and religion” makes this qualified status more explicit [F3]. That wording weakens any reading of the thesis as literal identity. The later text also restricts its religious comparator: it is “not speaking of all religions” but concentrating on Judaism, Christianity and Islam [F9]. The earlier article had discussed religion more generally while acknowledging the difficulty of constructing a universal scientific concept of it. The later formulation therefore gains comparative specificity, though at the price of applying directly to a narrower religious field.

The strongest counter-passage to an account of simple continuity is the later concession that capitalism is “far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” [F10]. Capitalism competes successfully in the provision of worldly benefits, and “to a good extent” can realize what once appeared miraculous; it cannot settle questions of human finitude or the beginning and end of the world [F10]. This does not overturn the earlier analogy, which also distinguished capitalist from traditional transcendence. It does, however, delimit the later thesis more expressly: the comparison concerns social universality, contingency management and worldly performance, not the full semantic or existential range of religion.

## Money: from paradoxical medium to cipher of world society

The 2001 argument begins from defects in functional and substantial definitions of money. Money purports to measure value even though there is no independently measurable substance common to money and goods. Hence it is “a numeraire which measures something invisible and immeasurable” [F11]. Simmel’s *Vermögen* then supplies a positive account: money gives its possessor not merely particular goods but higher-order options extending into material, social, temporal and spatial life. The force of this claim is class-qualified. Those qualities develop “only where” money exceeds daily subsistence; for the poor, money appears instead as debt and compulsion [F12].

The 2022 work preserves the proposition that money cannot be confined to exchange or scarcity regulation, but gives it a different theoretical task. The historical unbounding of markets produces a social nexus that no participant can observe as a whole. Money consequently becomes a cipher for society’s unity: “God is no longer needed to represent the unity of society or the world” because money unobtrusively assumes that place [F4]. Luhmann’s account of self-description and Hayek’s account of dispersed knowledge provide the new derivation: markets synthesize information that “in its entirety is available to no one” [F13].

This is an evidential change rather than a reversal. In 2001, the analogy rests primarily on the logical paradox and multidimensional power of money. In 2022, it rests on a historical and epistemological argument: spatially, socially, materially and temporally unbounded markets reproduce the intransparency once handled through religious cosmology. The earlier metaphors of faith and enigma remain compatible with the later argument, but the later text no longer needs indefinability by itself to carry the comparison.

A further clarification accompanies this move. “Capitalism” is not used as a harmless synonym for “market economy.” It designates “a social system based on unbounded markets,” not merely an economic subsystem [F14]. This changes the scale of the thesis: money can represent social unity because markets have penetrated the wider society, not because every use of currency is intrinsically religious.

## Growth, labour and private property

The 2001 article corrects Marx while expressly preserving a qualified Marxian result. It states that “Marx was wrong” to think that the temporal expenditure of average productive labour secures profitability [F15]. Labour must instead be deployed in a “creatively destructive way.” Schumpeter supplies entrepreneurial innovation, but his heroic entrepreneur is also corrected because innovation depends upon workers, experts, financiers and other cooperating actors. The declared solution is “a combination of the approaches of Marx and Schumpeter” [F15].

The later account retains creativity and innovation but relocates their primary structural basis. The decisive force is not instrumental reason or science treated as a religion. It is “the immense expansion of the possibility space of private property rights” produced by market unbounding [F5]. Once labour power and its immeasurable creative capacities enter the money nexus, private property becomes a claim not only over existing products but over what might be produced. Innovation, renewed desire and growth follow from this expanded claim.

The shift matters because it reduces the explanatory prominence of the entrepreneur and of discrete myth cycles. The 2022 text still refers to a continuing stream of “imaginations” and to creative destruction; it therefore does not abandon the 2001 mechanism. But the explanatory hierarchy changes. In 2001, entrepreneurial mediation and socially communicated myths explain how capital grows. In 2022, those processes actualize possibilities already opened by unbounded markets and private-property rights.

Both texts preserve a categorical pressure toward continuation. The earlier says, “Capital can only choose between growth or decline” [F16]. The later similarly argues that the search for innovations “must always go further.” Yet neither treats capitalist performance as reconciliation. The 2022 text sharpens the point: capitalism fulfils wishes while continually generating new ones, so its performance “never leads to an end and to reconciliation” [F17]. The religious analogy thus persists most strongly at the level of an inexhaustible promise whose realization reproduces the demand for further realization.

## Critique, concessions and the later conclusion

The 2022 text broadens the critique while making two important concessions. First, it rejects an account of religion as merely an obstacle to reason. Enlightenment criticism neglected monotheism’s contribution to rationalized conduct, universal ethics and a distanced scientific outlook [F18]. The consequence is not a return to religious authority. Rather, the text can describe the replacement of religion’s observer position without treating the displaced formation as wholly irrational.

Second, the later text acknowledges the market-liberal model’s historical efficacy. It generated reforms and innovations, and the “open society” may solve social problems. But these concessions are set against destructive consequences that the same order generates. Its “spectacular history of effects” is matched by extensive blind spots [F18]. This is consistent with the earlier text’s refusal to describe capitalism as merely irrational: in 2001, capitalist rationality was valid during the stabilization phase of a myth cycle, though “only” during that phase [F19].

The conclusion nevertheless changes the practical register. The 2001 article ends by anticipating the possible need to abandon the “drug of absolute wealth” if creative destruction can no longer reproduce growth. Its prediction of declining wages and unemployment is expressly conditional upon that process grinding to a halt [F20]. The 2022 work no longer centres that single hypothetical breakdown. It lists already accumulated crises and concludes: “What is needed is not new promises but sobriety and realism” in containing the consequences of unbounded markets [F7]. The later text therefore converts an open warning about the exhaustion of the myth cycle into a present recommendation for practical containment and sociological restraint.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the supplied collection, the capitalism-as-religion position is preserved but made more explicitly comparative, historically specified and epistemologically grounded. Money continues to exceed exchange functions; capitalist creativity continues to temporalize an earthly promise that cannot reach completion. What changes is the architecture of the explanation. The 2001 text moves from money’s paradox through Marx, Schumpeter and recurring economic myths. The 2022 text moves from the unbounding of markets through world society’s intransparency to money’s substitution for God as a cipher of social unity.

The settled result is therefore **continuity with reframing**, not a repudiation. The later text narrows the religious analogy’s scope, broadens its historical support, places private-property expansion ahead of entrepreneurial myth-making, and turns an earlier conditional warning into a present programme of containment. The exact remaining question is whether Deutschmann presents these changes elsewhere as a deliberate revision of the 2001 framework. The 2022 text itself does not say so.

### Counter-evidence

- The later text expressly limits the analogy: “It is clear that capitalism is far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” [F10].
- The earlier text already distinguishes capitalist from traditional religion by making economic myths cyclical rather than timeless [F8]. The later qualification therefore cannot by itself establish a new reversal.
- The 2022 text does not cite the 2001 article, so no explicit self-description such as “I revise my earlier account” is available.

### Open questions

- Does another work between 2001 and 2022 explicitly present the move from economic myths to unbounded markets and social self-description as a revision?
- How far would direct comparison with Deutschmann’s 1999 and 2019 books alter the developmental sequence?
- Does the later emphasis on private-property rights supplement the earlier Marx–Schumpeter synthesis, or replace its status as the primary explanation of growth? The supplied texts support changed emphasis but do not settle that hierarchy completely.
- How would the comparison change if religious traditions outside the later text’s Abrahamic scope were included?
