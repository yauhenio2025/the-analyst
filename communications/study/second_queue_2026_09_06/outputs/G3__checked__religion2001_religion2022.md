## From religious quality to social self-description

The first local use changes the object of economic sociology. **religion2001** rejects the familiar treatment of religion merely as capitalism’s cultural background and instead views “capital and the capital form of money” as possessing “intrinsically religious qualities” [F1]. The text expressly announces that it will integrate this idea into economic sociology [F19]. The concept’s purpose is therefore analytical rather than theological: it connects the paradoxes of money to capital’s growth imperative and then asks how entrepreneurship and innovation implement that imperative.

The argument begins from money’s dual status. Money represents relative commodity values, but it also embodies a privately appropriable capacity. Through the text’s account of Simmel’s *Vermögen*, its owner holds “an option of a higher order: the option to have options” [F3]. This capacity extends beyond current goods to possible goods and beyond purchasing power to social independence and control over time and space.

That claim carries a consequential distributional restriction. The “superadditum of wealth” develops “only where money is available beyond the requirements of daily living” [F7]. The poor do not possess a smaller quantity of an otherwise identical freedom; under subsistence constraint, money has a qualitatively different use. This qualification prevents the option-generating power of money from being attributed uniformly to every holder.

**religion2022** first supplies a different institutional definition of capitalism. Unbounded markets are not merely one functionally differentiated economic subsystem; they “form the core of the capitalist social system itself” [F2]. Spatial, social, material, and temporal expansion makes market relations and money constitutive of society’s organization.

The religion comparison then acquires an epistemic purpose. Hayek’s inaccessible aggregation of market knowledge is connected to Luhmann’s concept of social self-description: market ideology offers an image of social unity even though society cannot observe itself as a whole [F10]. The liberal narrative consequently both designates and conceals the society transformed by it. Money becomes a cipher for unity and takes over functions of managing contingency previously ascribed to God [F9].

This is adaptation, not a claim to reproduce the 2001 argument. The earlier text uses religious qualities to explain entrepreneurship, innovation, and changing institutions; the later text constructs “a comparative perspective on capitalism and religion” [F15]. The change broadens the object from capital and innovative action to world society, universalism, and the limits of social knowledge.

The strongest qualification comes from the later text itself. Capitalism remains unable to answer “the fundamental questions of religious faith,” and its universalism rests on moral minimalism rather than shared belief [F14]. Capitalism can therefore compete with religion and assume some of its social functions without becoming equivalent to religion in substance.

## From *Vermögen* to unbounded markets

Both works explicitly invoke Simmel to move money beyond its textbook role as an exchange medium. In **religion2001**, *Vermögen* names generalized capacity and culminates in the option over options [F3]. In **religion2022**, Simmel’s “absolute means” is reapplied to money “as the medium of unbounded markets” [F4]. What persists is the means/end reversal: because money serves an indeterminate range of ends, it becomes an end and imperative itself.

The later use adds an institutional and representational framework. Money’s option power is tied to the historical expansion of markets into labour, production conditions, finance, and everyday relations. It also represents society’s otherwise inaccessible unity and manages contingency [F9]. These are new uses of the Simmelian formulation, not evidence that the later work falsely reports Simmel.

The 2001 subsistence condition is not carried into the later local discussion. The paired passages therefore support a revised condition: money’s absolute-purpose character is explained at system level by unbounded markets, whereas the earlier text explicitly limits individuals’ option power according to whether their money exceeds daily needs [F16]. This local omission is consequential because it leaves the later reference to an imperative “for individual actors” less explicitly differentiated by wealth.

It does not, however, establish misrepresentation. The supplied Simmel original was not inspected, and the 2022 chapter does not claim to reproduce every condition in the 2001 article. What can be said is narrower: the later argument retains the means/end reversal while not restating the earlier distributional qualification.

## From cyclical myths to continuing imaginations

The 2001 account of innovation rests on two recombinations. First, it expressly combines Marx and Schumpeter, seeking to preserve their strengths while avoiding their shortcomings [F5]. Labour remains the creative source of surplus, but creativity replaces a quantitative law of average labour time; entrepreneurship remains indispensable, but innovation is reconstructed as cooperation rather than heroic individual action.

Second, constructivist paradigm and myth theories explain how innovations become communicable. Technical and organizational paradigms permit participants to act in at least a boundedly rational way [F6]. “Myth” here does not mean a mere falsehood: it is a socially resonant construction that orients heterogeneous actors, motivates cooperation, and gives uncertain development a selective direction.

Its temporal structure distinguishes it from religion. Religious myths usually claim timeless validity, whereas economic myths follow “rise–diffusion–institutionalization and decline” [F8]. Institutionalization creates stable knowledge and markets but eventually removes the myth’s innovative aura. The decline of one myth then becomes the starting point of another.

The 2022 chapter retains symbolic projections but renames and reweights them. A constant stream of Beckertian “imaginations” prepares and sustains entrepreneurial innovations [F12]. Instead of giving myth cycles the principal explanatory role, the chapter makes the “immense expansion of the possibility space of private property rights” the primary growth driver [F13]. Imaginations actualize possibilities generated by unbounded markets rather than independently supplying the core institutional rhythm of capitalist development.

The paired use therefore shows continuity with semantic and causal revision. Both formulations make projected futures necessary to innovation. The earlier one emphasizes cyclical institutionalization; the later one emphasizes continuous renewal within a never-ending growth process [F17]. Since **religion2022** cites Beckert 2016 and no supplied passage cites **religion2001**, resemblance does not establish direct transmission.

## Creative labour under a new causal center

Creative labour remains a bridge between the two arguments. **religion2001** states that “labour must be employed in a creative way in order to be profitable” [F18]. Its Marx–Schumpeter synthesis locates profitability in socially organized cooperation among entrepreneurs, employees, experts, financiers, and other participants.

In **religion2022**, creative labour enters the money nexus as markets extend to production conditions [F11]. The chapter then makes market competition the force that develops creative labour and compels “permanent growth through entrepreneurial innovations” [F18]. This preserves the relation among creativity, entrepreneurship, innovation, and growth while changing the explanatory center.

The receiving use gains a historical theory of market unbounding and private-property possibilities. It loses the earlier detailed account of intra-organizational cooperation, worker creativity, communicative mobilization, and cyclical myths. The change is a recombination of retained claims, not their simple disappearance.

## Judgment across the collection

Across both works, “capitalism as religion” remains a comparative and critical concept rather than a literal identification of capitalism with a church or creed. Money exceeds its exchange function; creative labour makes its possibilities indeterminate; entrepreneurial innovation converts those possibilities into growth; and this movement acquires quasi-religious force.

The consequential change concerns scale and purpose. **religion2001** moves from money’s paradox through capital’s growth imperative to entrepreneurship and cyclical myths. **religion2022** begins with unbounded markets, treats market liberalism as a self-description, and compares money with God as a representation of social unity and contingency. The later argument gains world society, market universalism, and an epistemology of intransparency. It drops much of the earlier institutional account of innovation and does not restate its subsistence qualification.

The collection thus settles that the concept undergoes **adaptation and argumentative reorientation without demonstrated misrepresentation**. It does not settle whether the 2022 use was transmitted directly from the 2001 article.
