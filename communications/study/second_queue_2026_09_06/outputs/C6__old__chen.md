## The reading

Chen’s article is structured as an immanent critique of Rahel Jaeggi’s pragmatist theory of progress, followed by a Kuhnian reconstruction of progress as a collectively constituted social fact. Its foundational problem is the normativity of historical judgment: how can a change count as “better” without appealing either to antecedent moral principles or to a teleological philosophy of history? Moralism and historicism are initially presented as opposed answers, but Chen argues that Jaeggi’s proposed escape—progress as cumulative problem-solving and enrichment of experience—quietly reinstates substantive standards while failing to accommodate discontinuity between forms of life.

The article’s decisive framework is a parallel between scientific paradigms and social forms of life. Jaeggi’s vocabulary of problems, crises, revolutions, and paradigm shifts supplies the bridge to Kuhn. Chen then turns this bridge against her: if forms of life resemble paradigms, their standards of problems, evidence, and successful solutions may be incommensurable. Progress therefore cannot reliably mean accumulation, whether of theoretical knowledge, experience, or practical know-how. This is the characteristic argumentative move of the article: accept an opponent’s analogy, extend it further than the opponent does, and derive a conclusion that overturns the original theory.

“Incommensurability” is consequently the pivotal imported concept. In its scientific origin it designates the absence of a neutral measure across paradigms; in Chen’s social adaptation it becomes stronger because rival social groups may disagree not only about solutions but about what constitutes a problem at all. Slavery, marriage, childhood, Nazism, and populism function chiefly as conceptual stress tests rather than historically developed case studies. They are used to show that emancipatory transitions can destroy capacities and experiences, while regressive movements can possess their own active consciousness of crisis and problem-solving. This reverses Jaeggi’s progress/regression opposition: neither accumulation nor problem-solving can independently carry normative weight.

Chen calls the alternative “psychologist,” but the term has a specialized and potentially misleading meaning. It does not primarily refer to individual psychology or empirical psychological explanation. It denotes the causal and constitutive priority of collective judgment, intention, desire, and community decision over neutral standards of progress. Kuhn’s scientific community and Searle’s account of intentionality-relative social facts are synthesized into a social ontology: movements become progressive because groups count them as progressive. This is not merely an epistemic thesis about how progress is recognized; it is the stronger ontological claim that evaluative recognition helps create the relevant social fact.

The article’s intellectual formation is visibly hybrid. Its problem-setting and opponents come from Frankfurt School critical theory: social pathology, emancipation, immanent crisis, forms of life, ethical life, and the critique of teleology. Its principal disruptive resource comes from post-Kuhnian philosophy of science: paradigms, puzzle-solving, scientific revolutions, theory choice, and incommensurability. Its positive ontology comes from Searlean social construction and collective intentionality. Deweyan pragmatism supplies Jaeggi’s model of experiential growth but ultimately serves as the position to be displaced. The resulting framework is an emergent synthesis of Kuhnian communitarian decision and Searlean constitutive rules.

The article argues cumulatively in its exposition but abductively and analogically at its core. It reconstructs Jaeggi, identifies a contradiction between paradigm discontinuity and experiential accumulation, tests possible repairs such as practical know-how, and then proposes the “psychologist pattern” as the explanation that best preserves nonteleology and plurality. What counts as sufficient evidence is predominantly conceptual consistency, authoritative textual citation, and the intuitive force of counterexamples. There is little empirical social science or detailed historical narrative. This permits a sharp diagnosis of conceptual tensions but leaves underdetermined how collective intentions are identified, whose judgments count, and how domination within a “majority” or “community” affects the constitution of progress.

The mature metaphors cluster around movement and growth: expanding and deepening morality, historical routes and ends, spirals of learning, accumulation and blockage of experience, paradigm shifts, and progress itself. Chen exposes the tensions among these metaphors but retains the largest spatial metaphor: one can move “toward” psychology and “away from” pragmatism. The title phrase “progress without ‘progress’” condenses the proposed separation of future-oriented striving from any general law, criterion, or reconstructed fact of historical progress. Its paradox is analytically productive, although the positive account risks replacing objective normativity with collective endorsement without fully explaining how endorsement differs from power, conformity, or retrospective victory.

## Counter-evidence

The article does not simply reject reasons, standards, or evidence. It explicitly says the psychologist account permits them, which qualifies any interpretation of the theory as straightforward irrationalism: “Of course, that does not mean that we do not have any reason or standard to make judgments.” Nevertheless, it assigns final authority to collective consciousness without fully explaining the status of reasons once no neutral criterion is available.

The claim that Jaeggi ignores incommensurability is also partly qualified by the reconstruction itself. Jaeggi recognizes discontinuity between moral paradigms and admits that identifying social problems is perspectival. Chen’s criticism is therefore better understood as claiming that Jaeggi does not integrate these acknowledgments consistently with accumulation, rather than that she is wholly unaware of discontinuity.

Finally, the text’s own examples sometimes retain substantive judgments that its psychologist framework is meant to suspend. It calls the transition to modern marriage “signiﬁcant progress” and distinguishes movements that are “truly progressive,” but it does not establish which collective, temporal population, or decision procedure constitutes the relevant “we.”

## Open questions

- How is the relevant collective identified when societies contain rival groups, unequal power, and incompatible judgments of progress?
- Does majority acceptance constitute progress immediately, or only retrospectively after institutions and values stabilize?
- What distinguishes collective intentionality from propaganda, coerced conformity, ideological domination, or simple political victory?
- If Nazism could constitute its own crisis and problem-solving narrative, what resources within the psychologist framework support Chen’s continued description of it as evil or regressive?
- Is “psychologist” the best label for a theory grounded in collective intentionality and social ontology, rather than empirical psychology?
- Does the claim that recognition creates progress conflate the institutional fact that something is publicly classified as progressive with the normative claim that it is progressive?
- Can local, domain-specific standards survive the rejection of a neutral transhistorical criterion, or does the account require stronger relativism than Chen acknowledges?
- The article cites Chen’s earlier work on social consciousness and social naturalism, but the present text does not explain how that prior framework supports the new psychologist account. This is a likely genealogical dependency requiring comparison with the earlier book.
