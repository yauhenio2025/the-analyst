# Reading

## The decisive distinction: conditional necessity, not an objective law of progress

The article’s strongest modal claim is conditional. It does **not** argue that every theory of progress must be psychological. It argues that a psychologist account is **“necessary if we insist on the validity of the parallelist argument”** between scientific and social progress [F1]. The condition matters: abandoning the parallelism remains an available alternative.

This conditional necessity follows from the article’s rejection of a stronger kind of necessity. Historical progress cannot, on its account, be governed by a neutral law or universal standard. The categorical statement that “a neutral, objective criterion of normativity is impossible” rejects an objective criterion for separating progress from regression [F2]. The article nevertheless does not infer that reasons and standards disappear. Its psychologist alternative makes collective consciousness decisive while allowing groups to offer multidimensional reasons for their judgments [F14].

## Why accumulation is possible in science but not secured in social change

The article grants a limited route around incommensurability. Kuhn’s instrumental formulation says that if knowledge means knowing-how, then it “clearly does grow” [F10]. This establishes a conditional possibility of accumulation, not general accumulation of theoretical truth. Its warrant is the scientific community’s shared principal goal of solving puzzles: common commitment lets distributed practical skill grow across paradigm changes [F13].

The article argues that this condition is absent or much weaker in social life. Social groups can disagree not merely over solutions but over what counts as a problem [F12]. Slaveholders may treat rebellion or productivity as problems, while abolitionists treat slavery itself as the problem. Consequently, the practical know-how accumulated within one form of life **“may not mean”** enrichment from the standpoint of its successor [F12]. This is the key qualification to the stronger conclusion that social progress “can hardly be” cumulative: the article moves from a particular modal risk to a general structural objection, supported by conflicting problem-consciousness rather than by a proof that accumulation never occurs.

The marriage example supplies a counterexample to proposed pragmatist thresholds. Modern marriage both negates traditional background values and generates serious functional problems, yet the text says its progressive status cannot simply be denied [F11]. This tests two proposed conditions—preserving background values and avoiding more serious problems—and argues that neither is necessary for historical progress.

## Crisis, reform, revolution, and regression

Jaeggi’s view, as reconstructed, contains conditional rather than uniform claims about social transition. Whether crisis is resolved by reform or only by revolution depends on the form and radicality of the contradictions [F9]. Thus vulnerability to crisis does not necessitate one kind of realization.

Her criterion of progress sets a functional boundary: practices and institutions count as changes for the better **“in so far as problems have been solved”** [F7]. Regression has a narrower threshold than merely failing to solve problems. Modernity is regressive only where systematic and self-caused mechanisms prevent it from facing crisis tendencies and block access to them [F8].

Chen’s objection is that this boundary does not reliably classify all limiting cases. Nazism and right-wing populism may involve active but distorted problem-identification rather than simple experiential blockage [F11]. Conversely, acknowledged progress can reduce practical capacities or experiences. The challenge is therefore not that the boundary is numerically vague, but that problem-solving and experience-enrichment may come apart.

## What is settled locally

Within this article, the best-supported conclusion is that Jaeggi’s analogy does not establish an invariably cumulative process of social learning. Incommensurability and disagreement over what constitutes a problem leave actual enrichment contingent. If the analogy with Kuhnian scientific change is retained, the article argues that it points toward collective, psychologist explanation rather than a neutral pragmatist law [F1], [F13].

The strongest counter-pressure comes from the article’s own acknowledgment that judgments remain reason-responsive [F14]. Collective intentionality may explain how a movement acquires the social status of “progressive,” but it does not by itself establish that a neutral criterion is conceptually impossible or explain how competing collective classifications should be criticized.

Because only one work is supplied, this is a **local inventory**. Any corpus-level conclusion about Chen’s position across other works, or its relation to Jaeggi’s full texts, would require those works as separate sources.

### Counter-evidence

The article’s strongest counter-passage to an unrestricted psychologist or relativist reading is its explicit preservation of reasons and standards [F14]. It also presents the psychologist account only as something that **“may offer a plausible alternative”** [F4]. These qualifications weaken any interpretation that the text has deductively established either the absolute impossibility of objective assessment or the unique truth of psychologism.

The know-how passage also limits the reach of incommensurability: under an instrumental definition, knowledge “clearly does grow” [F10]. The article’s objection therefore depends on showing that the scientific community’s shared goal has no adequate social analogue, not merely on invoking paradigm discontinuity.

### Open questions

- Does collective intentionality explain only why a movement is socially counted as progressive, or also why that classification is normatively justified?
- Does the dependence of normative judgment on values establish that a neutral criterion is impossible, or only that actual criteria are contested and socially situated?
- Could the scientific/social parallelism be revised around partial, local, or plural accumulation rather than either abandoned or converted into a psychologist theory?
- How much shared problem-consciousness is required before social know-how can genuinely accumulate across changes in forms of life?
