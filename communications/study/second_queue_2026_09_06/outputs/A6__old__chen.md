The reading, then:

Chen’s argument is organized around a conditional modal claim: **if** Jaeggi retains the analogy between scientific paradigm shifts and social transformation, **then** she cannot consistently describe social progress as cumulative enrichment and should adopt a psychologist account instead. This conditional is more defensible than the article’s stronger claims that neutral normative criteria are impossible or that collective judgment literally makes a movement progressive.

The critique identifies a genuine tension in Jaeggi’s framework. Paradigm replacement can discard concepts, skills, standards, and problem-definitions; therefore, “problem-solving” does not by itself guarantee cumulative growth. Chen’s examples of slavery, marriage, childhood, Nazism, and populism are intended as countermodels to the necessity claim that progressive change enriches capacities while regression blocks them. They establish at least conceptual and historical plausibility that transformation can simultaneously expand some capacities and diminish others. This successfully weakens any simple monotonic model of progress.

However, Chen frequently moves from **lack of a neutral comparison standard** to **incomparability**, and from context-dependence to the impossibility of objective normative judgment. Those are stronger conclusions than the premises establish. Incommensurability may impede direct or total comparison without precluding partial, translated, or morally justified comparison. Likewise, disagreement about what counts as a problem does not demonstrate that no cross-contextual reasons can be better than others.

The proposed psychologist alternative introduces an additional modal inflation. The claim that collective intentionality is relevant to progress discourse is plausible; the claim that collective classification **constitutes** actual progress is much stronger. Chen’s constitutive model risks collapsing “is collectively regarded as progressive” into “is progressive,” despite acknowledging that groups can regard persecution and genocide as problem-solving. The text says reasons remain available, but gives no criterion for distinguishing justificatory reasons from rationalizations once objective standards are excluded.

Several modal registers shift without sufficient notice. “Cannot” sometimes means logically inconsistent, sometimes conceptually unavailable, sometimes difficult in practice, and sometimes not neutrally decidable. “Progress is impossible” is not always distinguished from “no context-independent algorithm determines progress.” Similarly, claims about what social members “can hardly” share are sociological probability claims, not metaphysical impossibilities. The article is strongest when it makes modest possibility claims—progress may involve losses, regressions may contain problem-solving intentions—and weakest when it elevates these into universal impossibility and constitutive claims.

The treatment of thresholds is also underdeveloped. Reform and revolution are presented as alternatives determined by the “radical nature” of a crisis, but neither “radical” nor the point at which reform becomes paradigm replacement is specified. The resulting boundary is vulnerable to sorites objections. More generally, progress and regression may be multidimensional and gradual rather than mutually exclusive categories.

Overall, the article establishes that Jaeggi has not shown that social progress **must** be cumulative experience-enrichment. It also makes plausible that a Kuhnian analogy creates pressure toward contextual and community-sensitive evaluation. It does not establish that neutral criteria are impossible, that collective acceptance is sufficient for progress, or that psychologism is the only nonteleological alternative.

### Counter-evidence

- Chen does not reduce paradigm choice to arbitrary preference. He explicitly preserves evidential and rational considerations: “he does not deny the importance of good reasons and empirical evidence for paradigm shift”. This moderates, but does not resolve, the constitutive psychologism.

- The text acknowledges that social problem-identification is perspectival rather than automatically objective: “The existence and identiﬁcation of problems will be as disputed as their respective solutions”. This supports the critique of neutral problem-solving metrics.

- The psychologist proposal is expressly nonteleological and plural rather than offered as a law-governed historical mechanism: “it does not presuppose any criterion to evaluate whether a social change should be regarded as progressive.” This avoids historicist necessity, though it intensifies the relativism problem.

- Chen recognizes that scientific progress need not be irrational merely because it is community-based: “taking the psychological elements of scientists into account is a rational attitude.” This is a relevant defense against the equation of psychologism with arbitrariness.

- The article presents its final alternative cautiously as “plausible,” which is weaker than some earlier claims of necessity: “the psychologist pattern may offer a plausible alternative”. This softens the modal force of the conclusion.

### Open questions

1. Does incommensurability rule out all comparison, or only neutral, complete, and algorithmic comparison?
2. Why should collective intentionality constitute **progress**, rather than merely constitute a movement’s recognized social status as progressive?
3. Can oppressed minorities correctly call a movement regressive when the majority collectively counts it as progress?
4. What distinguishes justificatory collective reasons from ideological rationalization under the psychologist model?
5. Are progress and regression mutually exclusive, or can one transition be progressive along one dimension and regressive along another?
6. What determines the threshold at which reform becomes revolution or paradigm replacement?
7. Could a contextual, procedural, or pluralist standard avoid both teleology and moral relativism?
8. Is the scientific–social parallelism indispensable? If it is abandoned, the claimed necessity of the psychologist alternative no longer follows.
9. Does the claim that social facts are intentionality-relative apply equally to institutions, classifications, and normative properties?
10. What empirical evidence would establish the broad sociological claim that rival social groups “can hardly” share problem-definitions?
