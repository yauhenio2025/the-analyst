# Framework map: progress becomes visible as a contested act of classification

The article composes two principal frameworks around a shared inquiry: how social change can count as progress without appeal to a universal moral standard or historical telos. Its reconstruction of Jaeggi’s **declared pragmatist-materialistic framework** makes visible crises of forms of life, problem-solving capacities, learning, and blocked experience [F1–F5]. Its proposed **psychologist framework** instead makes visible the collective judgments through which groups constitute changes as progressive [F10–F12].

The source-evidenced limit of the first framework is that social groups need not agree about what the problem is: Jaeggi concedes that perceiving a crisis depends on “for whom” the situation is a problem [F7]. Consequently, the same component—problem-solving—can describe abolitionist action, slaveholding administration, or fascist persecution. The psychologist alternative preserves pluralism by locating the decisive classification in collective intentionality, but its corresponding boundary is explicit: it offers no neutral criterion that independently guarantees that the resulting judgment is right [F13].

## Components, dependencies, and competing explanations

Jaeggi’s reconstructed framework first reverses the usual normative dependency. Moralism and historicism derive progress from an antecedent good, right, or historical end; the pragmatist account instead gives progress priority over the good and defines it through “progress from” obstacles [F2]. Its social unit is the **form of life**, encompassing material and cultural reproduction rather than isolated conduct [F3]. A crisis arises when practices and their interpretive schemes cease to sustain one another. Progressive resolution then expands problem-solving capacity and future experience; regression systematically blocks access to crisis and learning [F4–F5].

Two frameworks therefore compete over the same phenomenon. The pragmatist explanation treats abolition or modern marriage as progress because a crisis is resolved through learning and enhanced capacities. The psychologist explanation treats them as progress because social members collectively rank one configuration of values above another [F11]. The second does not deny reasons or evidence; it denies that these supply a neutral, group-independent algorithm for progress [F9, F13].

## The analogy with science and its failure point

The article’s main analytical procedure is reconstruction followed by an analogy test. It identifies Jaeggi’s shared vocabulary with Kuhn—“problem-solving, paradigm shift, crisis, and revolution”—and tests whether the scientific/social parallel also carries Kuhnian incommensurability [F6]. This import preserves the structure of crisis and revolutionary replacement but changes domains: scientific paradigms become social forms of life.

The comparison initially appears favorable because practical know-how might grow even where theoretical claims are incommensurable. The article rejects that solution by distinguishing scientific communities from societies. Scientists can share the principal goal of solving puzzles, whereas members of rival forms of life can disagree about whether slavery itself—or slave resistance—is the problem [F7]. The analogy thus becomes the mechanism of critique: if forms of life really resemble paradigms, their standards and problem-consciousness may be discontinuous, so experience cannot automatically be described as cumulative [F8].

A second import comes from the constitutive-rule account of social facts. The article adapts the formula “X counts as Y in context C” to progress: collective classification does not merely report an independently progressive fact but contributes to creating the relevant social fact [F12]. This supports the psychologist alternative without providing a biography or inferred intellectual genealogy.

| Nazism and right-wing populism | Jaeggi’s binary would classify them as blockages of learning [F5] | They can themselves present persecution as a solution to a perceived crisis, so “problem-solving” does not demarcate progress [F15] | Collective constitution explains how movements acquire a progress-claim, but cannot make that claim neutrally correct [F13, F15] |

The strongest counter-passage to a neutral pragmatist distinction is the admission that crisis perception depends on whose situation is being assessed [F7]. It destabilizes the proposed dependency from objective crisis to progressive solution: before capacities can be measured, groups already disagree over the object, description, and value of the problem.

The psychologist alternative settles the article’s nonteleological requirement by replacing a law of historical progress with group-based classification [F10–F12]. What remains unsettled is exact: **if collective intentionality makes a movement count as progressive, what non-neutral but criticizable procedure distinguishes warranted collective judgments from fascist or exclusionary constructions of “progress”?** The article acknowledges that reasons remain available, but it does not specify a decision procedure beyond the collective judgment itself [F13].

### Counter-evidence

The pragmatist framework is not simply functionalist: it expressly recognizes that social problems are interpretive and disputed. That qualification prevents the critique from reducing Jaeggi’s position to technical efficiency. Nevertheless, the admission that problem perception varies “for whom” [F7] weakens the claim that experience enrichment can supply a formal distinction between progress and regression.

The psychologist alternative also does not claim that collective preference eliminates argument. It retains reasons and standards [F13]. Its unresolved problem is narrower: the article does not explain how those reasons constrain collective constitution without reintroducing a substantive criterion of progress.

### Open questions

1. What procedure evaluates competing collective classifications once neutrality is rejected?
2. Can a distinction between constituting a movement as “progressive” and justifiably calling it progressive be maintained?
3. Does practical know-how require total accumulation, or could progress preserve some capacities while rationally abandoning others?
4. Would another work by Jaeggi qualify the article’s reconstruction of experience enrichment? A corpus-level answer requires an additional source.
