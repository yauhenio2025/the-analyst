## The reading

Deutschmann’s article begins as an intellectual detective story and ends as a secular prophecy. Its initial narrative contract is exploratory: Benjamin has left an “enigmatic” fragment, conventional sociology cannot explain it, and the author will reconstruct the missing conceptual bridge between religion and capitalism. The title’s question mark sustains this provisional posture. Yet the inquiry gradually becomes an affirmation: money is presented as an “enigma,” capital as a this-worldly promise of omnipotence, entrepreneurs as analogues of prophets and priests, and innovation paradigms as capitalist myths. By Section IV, the text can announce that Benjamin’s conclusions have effectively been confirmed.

The article’s large-scale plot resembles a tragic cycle more than a linear demonstration. Capitalism promises “absolute wealth,” repeatedly realizes fragments of that promise through innovation, and repeatedly destroys the myths through which realization becomes possible. Innovation moves from uncertain inspiration through diffusion and institutionalization to sclerosis, crisis, and replacement. This cycle initially looks regenerative—even comic, because decline generates renewal—but the final section changes its valence. Acceleration, financialization, and neoliberal individualization threaten to paralyse the mechanism of renewal itself. The cyclical story therefore opens onto a terminal possibility: creative destruction may cease, producing depression and requiring society to abandon the promise of absolute wealth. The ending refuses empirical closure while supplying strong prophetic closure.

The major narrative turns are staged as revelations. First, the familiar opposition between rational capitalism and irrational religion is reversed: money itself proves undefinable and paradoxical. Second, Simmel supplies the ontological expansion of money from exchange medium to *Vermögen*, while Marx turns the static paradox into the dynamic imperative of capital accumulation. Third, the question “What makes capital grow?” shifts focalization from classical theorists to entrepreneurs, workers, experts, and collective processes of innovation. Fourth, economic paradigms are redescribed as myths, allowing the text to narrate capitalist development as alternating sacralization and disenchantment. Finally, the return to Benjamin transforms the preceding conceptual synthesis into a diagnosis of the present.

Voice and register are central to this progression. The article begins in a cautious academic register—surveying definitions, disciplinary divisions, and conceptual dilemmas—and repeatedly uses interrogatives to propel the inquiry. Its middle sections combine philosophical exposition with mechanistic economic vocabulary. Religious metaphors then become increasingly literal and systematized: money is a “god,” entrepreneurs are prophets and priests, paradigms require faith, neoliberalism preaches salvation, and absolute wealth becomes a drug. These shifts are largely strategic because they follow the architecture of the thesis. Nevertheless, the closing apocalyptic register exceeds the article’s own reconstructive standard. Claims about an approaching “true religious disillusion” are not established through the same historical or empirical evidence used in the innovation-cycle discussion.

The dominant focalization is that of a critical theoretical observer who claims access to capitalism’s hidden structure. Actors are credited with creating institutions and myths, but their own interpretations are usually subordinated to the narrator’s synthesis. Focalization briefly widens when the text contrasts rich and poor, restores workers’ creativity against Schumpeter, and embeds entrepreneurs in networks. Even so, workers, consumers, unsuccessful entrepreneurs, and non-Western societies rarely become speaking perspectives. They function primarily as positions within the explanatory model.

The narrative’s most productive tension is between circularity and culmination. The myth cycle is supposed to be constitutive and repeatable, yet the present is narrated as a potentially unprecedented breakdown. This gives the conclusion urgency, but it also creates an unresolved argumentative problem: if capitalism lives by recurrent crisis and renewal, why should current acceleration terminate rather than intensify that cycle? The text answers mainly through escalating imagery—overcharging, paralysis, withdrawal, grinding to a halt—rather than a demonstrated causal threshold.

A second tension concerns constructivism. Deutschmann says theory should reconstruct how actors create structures rather than posit objective laws. Yet his own narrative attributes inherent imperatives to money and capital and predicts what would “inevitably” follow from halted creative destruction. The article thus opposes deterministic economic theory while adopting a quasi-deterministic tragic emplotment of its own. This does not nullify its central achievement: it establishes a powerful interpretive synthesis connecting money’s abstract power, capitalist growth, entrepreneurial uncertainty, and innovation myths. But it establishes capitalism “as” religion more convincingly as an illuminating narrative model than as a fully discriminating sociological explanation.

### Counter-evidence

- The text repeatedly marks limits to its claims, which tempers the apparent movement from inquiry to certainty. — anchor: "Here I can only formulate my basic thesis:" — confidence: high

- The author acknowledges power asymmetry and worker dependence, so the entrepreneur-prophet narrative is not wholly heroic or managerial. — anchor: "It is not only that the worker depends on the entrepreneur, but, in spite of all power asymmetries, also the entrepreneur on the worker." — confidence: high

- The conclusion is grammatically conditional rather than an unqualified prediction; terminal crisis remains a possibility. — anchor: "But no new utopias will show the way out of the social crises that might be ahead." — confidence: high

- The article explicitly distinguishes capitalist myths from timeless religious myths, limiting any claim of simple identity. — anchor: "capitalist myths are subject to a historical movement of growth and decline" — confidence: high

### Open questions

- What empirical criterion would distinguish an economic “myth” from a mistaken forecast, strategic marketing campaign, ideology, or ordinary technological expectation?

- If the myth cycle normally renews itself through crisis, what mechanism makes present acceleration likely to paralyse it rather than produce another cycle?

- Does describing money as religious explain capitalist conduct, or redescribe familiar properties—trust, abstraction, uncertainty, and futurity—in sacred language?

- How would the argument change if focalized through workers, debtors, consumers, failed entrepreneurs, or societies outside the Occidental genealogy emphasized in Section I?

- Can the article consistently reject objective developmental laws while asserting an inherent growth imperative and an “inevitable” depressive spiral?

- Is the final call to relinquish absolute wealth an implication of the analysis, or a normative and prophetic register that the preceding reconstruction does not establish?
