## The reading, then:

The article tells a qualified romance of regained political agency. Its initial world is an apparently mundane but indispensable infrastructure concealed beneath the sea and governed largely by private commercial interests. The crisis arises when geopolitical rivalry reaches into this inherited network: states discover that infrastructures vital to security do not conform neatly to national strategy. “Private network realignment” names the ensuing effort to restore political direction. Yet the romance is deliberately restrained: neither the United States nor China achieves sovereign mastery, and France’s partial recovery of control remains expensive and vulnerable. The resulting emplotment combines romance with comedy in Hayden White’s sense—conflict produces provisional accommodation rather than final victory—and carries a satirical edge toward simplified stories of bipolar decoupling.

The narrative contract is established through a movement from material urgency to scholarly correction. Statistics about internet traffic, references to sabotage, and the image of cables “Hidden beneath the sea” invite the reader first to recognize an overlooked danger. The article then stages a revelation: prevailing statecraft approaches misdescribe a network historically built through speculative investment, privatization, and corporate decisions. This permits the author to occupy an intermediary, synoptic position above both state-centric and capital-centric accounts. The theoretical term “realignment” transforms a descriptive mismatch into a temporal plot: markets first produced infrastructural trajectories; security priorities later diverged from them; states now intervene ex post to “correct” those trajectories.

The dominant temporality is therefore historical and path-dependent. The narrative travels from the neoliberal policies of the 1980s and 1990s, through the dotcom crash and hyperscaler construction boom, into a present of renewed state intervention. Its “now” is a confrontational mid-2020s moment shaped by US–China rivalry, sabotage fears, AI-driven demand, and uncertainty about transatlantic relations. The future appears chiefly as trajectory and risk: present cases are called “prototypical,” SeaMeWe-6 is made consequential for the cable industry as a whole, and current bottlenecks forecast coming political choices.

The first case supplies the article’s clearest dramatic arc. A consortium initially selects HMN Tech; US incentives and threatened sanctions alter the commercial calculation; a decisive vote reverses the award; Chinese investors withdraw; China responds by developing parallel routes. This is not simply evidence of US coercive capacity. Narratively, it converts an abstract typology into a contest with recognizable antagonists, reversal, and counter-move. The cable consortium is the pivotal focal point because its changing calculation demonstrates that states act through, rather than simply over, private decision-makers. Nevertheless, the case’s geopolitical drama risks making corporations appear as objects of pressure despite the article’s declared intention to treat them as autonomous centers of power.

The France case changes both scale and register. It moves from great-power confrontation to a national story of strategic autonomy, industrial preservation, and qualified opportunity. The nationalization of ASN functions as the revelation that incomplete great-power control creates room for middle powers. France becomes the article’s most affirmative protagonist: neither passively aligned nor globally dominant, it converts inherited hub status and industrial capacity into autonomous action. But this success story receives an explicit limiting coda—budgetary pressure, weaker financing capacity, and dependence on US technology constrain autonomy. That qualification prevents triumphalist closure while also protecting the conceptual framework from an overly favorable case.

Registers perform distinct argumentative work. The opening uses infrastructural-security drama; the literature review adopts disciplinary adjudication; the framework uses taxonomic and mechanistic language; the case studies become chronological and actor-centered; the conclusion turns programmatic and prescriptive. These shifts are largely strategic rather than unconscious. More revealing is the recurrent corrective vocabulary—“disconnect,” “misalignment,” “reestablish control,” “correct,” and “realignment.” Although the explicit argument de-centers state control, this register tacitly treats government objectives as the standard against which market development appears deviant. The narrative thus retains a statist horizon that its intermediary theoretical position does not fully resolve.

Closure is deliberately incomplete. The article establishes the plausibility and comparative usefulness of private network realignment, but it does not narrate a settled new order. Networks continue expanding rather than dividing cleanly; interventions remain uneven; duplication can increase redundancy while also deepening confrontation; and middle-power autonomy remains conditional. The final move from explanation to policy recommendation gives the text practical closure, yet the injunction for “decisive, proactive public investments” moves beyond what two selected cases can conclusively establish. Narrative urgency and the desire for an actionable ending here outrun the comparative argument.

### Open questions

- Whether “realignment” can analytically distinguish legitimate risk reduction from coercive efforts to subordinate private actors remains unresolved, since the category includes sanctions and sabotage alongside investment.
- The source does not establish how corporations narrate their own strategic aims; their preferences are generally reconstructed through decisions, media reports, and official accounts.
- It remains unclear whether France is representative of middle-power opportunity or an unusually favorable case because it already possesses a major supplier, landing hubs, and state shareholdings.
- The article leaves unresolved whether duplicated cable systems should be interpreted primarily as geopolitical fragmentation, commercial expansion, security redundancy, or all three at once.
- The leap from two prototypical cases to policy prescriptions about proactive public investment requires broader comparative evidence across unsuccessful interventions and other infrastructure sectors.
