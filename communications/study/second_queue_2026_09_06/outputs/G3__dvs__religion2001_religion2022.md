# Reading: from intrinsic religion to comparative social function

## Position map

The collection contains two works published twenty-one years apart, but publication order alone does not establish a documented line of transmission or argumentative development between them.

**“Capitalism as a Religion? An Unorthodox Analysis of Entrepreneurship” (2001)** argues that capital and money possess intrinsically religious qualities and, in modern societies, take religion’s place. Its distinctive contribution is to combine Simmel’s *Vermögen*, Marx’s capital, Schumpeterian entrepreneurship, and constructivist theories of myths and visions into a dynamic account of innovation and growth. Its central qualification is distributive and historical: money supplies an “option to have options” only beyond subsistence, while economic myths differ from religious myths because they undergo institutionalization and decline rather than claiming permanent validity. [F1] [F2]

**“The Interpretation of Capitalism as Religion – Justification, Epistemic Possibilities, Critique” (2022)** treats capitalism as a social system of spatially, socially, materially, and temporally unbounded markets. It compares capitalism and religion as different responses to society’s inability to observe itself as a whole: religion constructs a transcendent observer, while market liberalism and money function as secular self-descriptions and contingency formulas. Its distinctive role is therefore comparative and epistemological rather than primarily entrepreneurial. Its major qualification is that the comparison concerns selected representational and practical functions: capitalism cannot answer the fundamental questions of religious faith, and the analysis of religion is expressly concentrated on Abrahamic world religions. [F3] [F4]

The principal gain in the 2001 use is a theory of capitalist **dynamics**: the religious quality of money links generalized possibility, growth, entrepreneurial mobilization, and cycles of innovation. The principal loss is differentiation between capitalism and religion at the level of the opening formula: saying that money and capital have “intrinsically religious qualities” risks sounding like an identity claim, although the later contrast between timeless religious myths and cyclical economic myths limits that implication.

The 2022 use gains a more differentiated **comparative framework**. It identifies a shared problem—representing an intransparent social whole—while distinguishing the answers: transcendent observation through God versus decentralized coordination and representation through markets and money. It consequently loses some of the 2001 article’s detailed account of entrepreneurial organization and myth cycles. The change is an adaptation of the concept’s analytical purpose, not a demonstrated correction of, or borrowing from, the earlier article.

## From entrepreneurial dynamics to social self-description

### Money, possibility, and growth in the 2001 argument

The 2001 article begins by moving religion from capitalism’s cultural environment into the conceptualization of capital itself. Rather than asking how religious ethics shape economic action, it proposes that “capital and the capital form of money” possess “intrinsically religious qualities.” [F5] This shift allows religion to explain a problem that a purely functional theory of money allegedly misses: money is not merely one means among others but a general means whose range changes its character.

Simmel’s *Vermögen* supplies the key transition. Money joins material access, social independence, and control over time and space in one medium. [F2] The retained condition is crucial: this generalized freedom belongs to money available beyond daily subsistence. Poverty is therefore not merely less *Vermögen* quantitatively. It lacks the freedom of choice associated with wealth and confronts the reverse form, debt. The religious analogy must consequently not be generalized into a claim that every holder of money possesses the same power.

Marx then changes the object from held possibility to process. Money preserves its potential only by returning with an increment; capital is “the processual unity of money” determined to produce more money. [F8] Growth is not simply an optional economic outcome. It becomes the practical, never-final resolution of money’s conflict between qualitative infinity and quantitative finitude.

That move gives the religious comparison its first precise function: growth carries a promise that cannot be finally redeemed. Yet the article does not stop at analogy. It asks how growth is organized under uncertainty and answers by recombining Marx with Schumpeter. The text retains labor’s creativity from its revision of Marx and entrepreneurial innovation from Schumpeter, but rejects both a quantitative law that automatically explains value and the isolated heroic entrepreneur. It explicitly proposes “a combination of the approaches of Marx and Schumpeter.” [F14]

Economic myths and technological visions then mediate between capital’s abstract demand for growth and collective innovation. Their success depends not simply on efficiency but on social resonance. They coordinate workers, inventors, financiers, experts, customers, and political supporters by selecting promising possibilities and motivating cooperation. The religious analogy is local and structural: just as religious access requires interpreters and symbols, entrepreneurial action requires mediated interpretations of an otherwise indeterminate field of options. [F10]

The strongest counterweight to treating these myths as simply religious is the text’s temporal distinction. Economic myths lose their innovative aura as they become institutionalized; their decline produces crises and searches for new alternatives. Religious myths, “as a rule,” claim timeless validity, whereas economic paradigms move through “rise–diffusion–institutionalization and decline.” [F11] The concept “capitalism as religion” thus does not erase institutional analysis. It is used to organize a theory of institutional creation, consolidation, exhaustion, and destruction.

### Unbounded markets and epistemic substitution in 2022

The 2022 chapter changes the scale and purpose. Capitalism is no longer introduced principally through the capital form of money and entrepreneurship. It is defined through the unbounding of markets until society becomes an “appendage” of markets and money. [F15] This supplies the structural condition under which money can assume functions formerly assigned to religion.

The central problem is now epistemic: world society can be observed only from positions within it, never as a complete totality. Religion addresses the missing-observer problem by constructing a transcendent creator and observer. [F3] Market liberalism supplies a secular alternative, not by making society transparent but by representing its unity through market coordination. Luhmann’s self-description and Hayek’s dispersed-knowledge argument are joined at this point. Markets process information unavailable to any single actor, while neoliberal ideology describes society through markets without becoming a scientifically true image of the whole. [F13]

Money’s religious function is correspondingly narrowed and specified. It becomes a cipher for social unity and assumes the contingency-management functions formerly attributed to God. [F16] This retains the 2001 claim that money exceeds exchange, but changes its immediate purpose. The 2001 account moves from generalized options to growth and innovation; the 2022 account moves from universal market mediation to the representation of an intransparent society.

The comparison also gives capitalism a different kind of universalism. Religions establish community through shared orientation to a transcendent God. Capitalism establishes a potentially global nexus through private contracts that do not require common religious or collective identity. Its universality rests on moral minimalism: reciprocity is reduced principally to exchange and respect for property rights. [F17] What capitalism gains in inclusiveness it loses in substantive social obligation, leaving broad scope for egoism and power inequality.

The chapter preserves two decisive limits. First, its religious discussion does not purport to cover all religions; it concentrates on Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. [F4] Second, functional substitution is not total replacement. Capitalism cannot answer questions about the beginning and end of the world or the finitude of human life. [F18] The strongest counter-passage to an identity thesis is therefore explicit: “capitalism is far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith.” The comparison establishes overlapping social functions, not equivalence between money and God or completeness as a religion.

## Continuity, revision, and independent routes

Across the collection, the most stable proposition is that money cannot be adequately understood as a neutral exchange device. In both texts it mediates an indeterminate field of possibilities and acquires a social significance exceeding ordinary economic scarcity. [F7] Both also preserve a growth dynamic: creativity and market possibilities are converted into recurrent innovations, so the promise attached to money never arrives at reconciliation.

The most consequential revision concerns what the religious comparison is for. In 2001 it provides “the systematic basis” for analyzing how capitalism creates and destroys structures. [F1] In 2022 it supports a comparison between two modes of social universality and two responses to intransparency. The later publication thereby offers a narrower claim about religious equivalence but a wider account of world society.

There are also independent argumentative routes. The 2001 article travels through Simmel, Marx, Schumpeter, theories of technological paradigms, and institutional accounts of innovation. The 2022 chapter travels through Polanyi’s embeddedness, Luhmann’s self-description, Hayek’s dispersed knowledge, Plessner’s masks, and economic theology. Shared vocabulary such as “money,” “religion,” and “absolute means” does not collapse these routes into one.

The common named sources provide stronger evidence than resemblance, but only for common source use. Simmel supports parallel extensions of money beyond exchange; Marx supports distinct dynamic and idolatry-critical uses; Benjamin supports two adaptations of “capitalism as religion.” [F7] [F8] [F9] None of these references demonstrates that the 2022 chapter borrowed its use from the 2001 article. The supplied texts contain no uptake clause in which the later publication cites or describes the earlier article. Publication chronology is clear—2001 and 2022—but argumentative development between them remains unresolved.

Weber presents the sharpest divergence. The 2001 article expressly declines the familiar Weberian route because it wants religion inside the concept of capital rather than in capitalism’s cultural background. The 2022 chapter, by contrast, uses Weber’s transformation of financial success from a sign of salvation into a substance and extends that pattern beyond Christianity. [F6] This is not necessarily contradiction: the first text rejects one explanatory framework, while the second reuses a more specific Weberian transformation within a comparative argument. Still, it shows that “capitalism as religion” does not retain one fixed relation to Weber across the collection.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the two works, “capitalism as religion” remains a strategy for refusing a merely technical conception of money. Its use nevertheless changes substantially. In 2001 it is an integrating theory of generalized monetary possibility, compulsory growth, entrepreneurial mediation, and cyclical institutional change. In 2022 it becomes a comparative account of how religion and capitalism represent social unity, manage contingency, and construct different forms of universalism.

The later use is more qualified: money assumes selected functions of God but does not answer ultimate religious questions; capitalism rivals religion without becoming identical to it. This is a legitimate adaptation rather than a misrepresentation merely because its purpose changes. Misrepresentation cannot be judged from the supplied corpus because the cited originals are absent. Direct borrowing between the two works is likewise not demonstrated. The warranted conclusion is continuity of problem, mutation of analytical purpose, recombination through overlapping sources, and unresolved transmission between the supplied publications.

### Counter-evidence

- Against complete equivalence between economic and religious myths: “Different from religious myths, which – as a rule – claim timeless validity, their development is characterized by a cyclical move of rise–diffusion–institutionalization and decline” — doc: religion2001.
- Against complete replacement of religion by capitalism: “It is clear that capitalism is far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” — doc: religion2022.
- Against treating market exchange as wholly asocial: “Markets institutionalize not naked egoism but the social norm of exchange, which ties the satisfaction of ego's interests to that of alter's.” — doc: religion2022.
- Against treating money’s generalized freedom as equally available to everyone: “However, this 'superadditum of wealth' (Simmel, 1989: 274) can develop only where money is available beyond the requirements of daily living.” — doc: religion2001.

### Open questions

- Did the 2022 chapter directly reuse or revise the 2001 article, or do their similarities arise through common authorship, common cited sources, and recurring problems?
- How accurately do the works represent Simmel, Marx, Benjamin, Weber, Luhmann, Hayek, and the other cited sources? Their originals were not supplied.
- How far can the 2022 comparison be extended beyond Abrahamic religions without changing its account of transcendence and social unity?
- Does the 2001 language of “intrinsically religious qualities” ultimately assert identity, functional substitution, or a deliberately strong analogy? Its internal qualifications narrow but do not fully eliminate this ambiguity.
