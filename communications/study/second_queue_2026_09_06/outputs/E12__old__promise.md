The article constructs a dynamic, constructivist economic sociology of capitalism by treating “capitalism as a religion” not as a classificatory identity but as a negative and structural comparison. Because no stable, transhistorical definition of religion is available, Deutschmann locates the comparison in the paradoxical, self-validating character of religion and money: both help constitute the reality through which they are observed. The religious analogy is therefore methodological before it is substantive. It displaces the conventional question of religious influence on capitalism with a question about how money supplies capitalism with a this-worldly transcendence—the promise of “absolute wealth.”

The framework is assembled in stages. Simmel supplies the account of money as *Vermögen*, an “absolute means” that combines purchasing power, property rights, social freedom, and command over time and space. Marx converts this static paradox into the process of capital accumulation: finite money can sustain its promise of qualitative infinity only by returning as more money. Schumpeter then provides the entrepreneur and innovation as mechanisms of growth, while Marx supplies labour’s indispensable creative capacity. Civilizational theory and institutionalism explain how the monetary utopia stands behind private property and other institutions. Finally, constructivist accounts of paradigms, visions, and myths explain how heterogeneous actors are mobilized under uncertainty.

The resulting middle-range mechanism is a “myth cycle.” Entrepreneurs and associated experts interpret the indeterminate possibilities represented by money through technological, organizational, and consumption myths. These symbolic constructions coordinate cooperation and investment, diffuse through imitation, and eventually become institutionalized. Institutionalization makes action more calculable but exhausts the myth’s innovative force, reducing profitability and generating crisis and renewed myth formation. This yields the article’s principal synthetic insight: capitalist rationality and profitability are not mutually reinforcing at every point. Rationality is greatest after institutional consolidation, precisely when innovation and extraordinary profit begin to decline.

The synthesis is ambitious but uneven. Its paradigmatic commitment to constructivist reconstruction is explicit near the end, although it is prepared throughout by the critique of objective definitions and equilibrium models. This commitment makes actors’ interpretations, uncertainty, creativity, and symbolic mobilization visible; it makes structural measurement, causal comparison, and adjudication among competing material explanations harder. The article operationalizes myths only through broad examples—technological paradigms, management fashions, consumption visions, consultants, and innovation cycles—and supplies no systematic comparative evidence or criteria for distinguishing a productive myth from ordinary expectations, ideology, strategy, or discourse. Its strongest theory-evidence connection is the internally articulated innovation cycle; its weakest is the extension from that cycle to capitalism’s essentially religious character and to predictions of financial decoupling or systemic breakdown.

### Counter-evidence

- [C1] The religious analogy is qualified by a consequential disanalogy: capitalist myths are temporary and cyclical, unlike religious myths’ usual claims to timeless validity. — anchor: "However, while religious myths usually claim timeless validity, capitalist myths are subject to a historical movement of growth and decline; they can never establish themselves permanently." — confidence: high
- [C2] The text does not reduce capitalism wholly to irrational faith; it grants that stable markets permit rational strategy during the institutionalized phase of the cycle. — anchor: "A stable market allowing rational strategies of the actors is being created." — confidence: high
- [C3] The framework recognizes class asymmetry, which complicates any claim that the promise of absolute wealth is experienced uniformly across capitalism. — anchor: "As Simmel emphasizes, poverty and wealth differ not only in quantitative, but in qualitative terms." — confidence: high
- [C4] The author’s critique of objective theory risks becoming too strong, since the article itself makes broad causal claims about money, labour, profitability, and institutional cycles. — anchor: "The driving forces of those dynamics cannot be described sufficiently by any 'objective' theory that relies on the analysis of fixed 'factors'." — confidence: medium

### Open questions

- What observable criteria distinguish an economic “myth” from a forecast, ideology, marketing narrative, technological expectation, or ordinary coordination device?
- How could the proposed generation–diffusion–institutionalization–decline cycle be tested against innovation processes that stabilize without crisis or decline without replacement?
- Does the religion analogy explain growth better than institutional, political-economic, ecological, or financial theories, or does it primarily redescribe capitalism’s motivational symbolism?
- How does the promise of absolute wealth operate among actors for whom money is chiefly debt and discipline rather than open possibility?
- What comparative cases—non-capitalist monetary systems, different capitalist institutions, or non-Western religious traditions—would establish the framework’s scope and guard against the centrism the article itself criticizes?
- Can labour alone be called genuinely creative without importing an unsupported human exceptionalism regarding machines and computational systems?
- What evidence would falsify the claim that the growth imperative is inherent in money’s form rather than produced by competition, credit obligations, property relations, or state policy?
