## Reading

The article constructs a critical geopolitical-economy account of AUKUS by opposing the state-centric and strategic assumptions of realist international relations. Its explicit paradigm treats security arrangements not primarily as responses to an external distribution of power, but as institutional sites where states, markets, class coalitions, and forms of capital are mutually reorganised. AUKUS therefore becomes less an alliance whose conduct is explained by China’s rise than a political-economic project whose security language helps coordinate accumulation, industrial policy, and state transformation.

The central theoretical innovation—though extended from the authors’ prior work rather than created wholly within this article—is **militarised neoliberalism** (MN). MN names a mutation rather than a termination of neoliberalism: market mechanisms persist, but are increasingly organised through security institutions, alliances, subsidies, export controls, procurement, and state finance. This move challenges the conventional opposition between neoliberal markets and interventionist states. State activism is interpreted as market-making for selected sectors and fractions of capital, not necessarily as deglobalisation or departure from neoliberalism.

The explanatory object is specifically the economic-security architecture surrounding AUKUS, especially Pillar II. The proposed causal sequence is: geopolitical narratives and bipartisan agreement reduce contestation; this agreement authorises regulatory and institutional changes; those changes repurpose economic and security agencies, mobilise public finance, and create transnational policy networks; these networks favour defence manufacturers, civilian conglomerates, critical-mineral firms, technology start-ups, and venture capital. The outcome is both state transformation and a redistribution of market opportunities, producing “new losers and winners.”

The article synthesises several bodies of theory: geopolitical economy and the critique of “geopolitical fetishism”; state-transformation theory; regulatory-control and transnationalised-state approaches; developmental-state analysis; and theories of venture capital’s structural power. These are presented as complementary components of MN. The synthesis generates a useful multiscalar account connecting bipartisan politics, agency reorganisation, industrial policy, and capital accumulation. It also carries unresolved tensions. Most importantly, the criteria that make these developments specifically *neoliberal*, rather than military Keynesianism, developmental statism, economic nationalism, or state capitalism, remain undertheorised. “Mutation” is flexible enough to absorb policies that appear to reverse earlier neoliberal commitments, creating a risk of conceptual unfalsifiability.

Methodologically, inference is iterative but predominantly theory-led. The authors begin with MN, identify three expected features, and organise archival and secondary material around them. Funding awards, legislative reforms, procurement contracts, export-control changes, institutional mandates, and public-private initiatives serve as observable indicators of regulatory control, state transformation, and class recomposition. These sources strongly establish that AUKUS is embedded in an expanding economic-security nexus. They establish less securely that the identified beneficiaries caused policy changes, that bipartisan agreement was the decisive enabling mechanism, or that the resulting order is best classified as neoliberal. The article frequently infers interests, coalition formation, and accumulation effects from policy outputs and benefits rather than tracing lobbying, deliberation, or counterfactual outcomes.

The geographic scope is partly explicit: the analysis covers the three AUKUS members and extends to Japan and South Korea. Its substantive scope is narrower than some of its rhetoric, concentrating on Pillar II, advanced technology, defence production, critical minerals, and finance. The authors explicitly exclude assessing whether AUKUS will cause regional conflict. Yet claims about reconstruction of the global economy exceed what the selected cases alone can demonstrate. The theory is strongest as a middle-range explanation of how contemporary security partnerships can become vehicles for market-making and institutional reorganisation; it is weaker as a general account of neoliberalism or the global geoeconomic order.

A realist alternative would ask whether industrial mobilisation, technology controls, and allied supply chains improve deterrence and relative capabilities against China. A developmental-state perspective would assess capability-building, learning, and national upgrading without presuming that state activism serves neoliberal accumulation. A historical-materialist analysis more demanding than the article’s own would require direct evidence of class agency, labour effects, ownership, profitability, and distribution. Those paradigms would make visible questions largely displaced here: whether the security threat is independently real, whether policies improve defence effectiveness, whether public investments generate broad social benefits, and how workers and excluded firms experience the asserted redistribution.

## Findings ledger

- [F1] The organising paradigm is explicitly critical geopolitical economy: it reconnects interstate security relations to internal state structures and social relations rather than treating states as unitary actors. — anchor: "There is indeed a need to reconnect geopolitical relations between states and the underlying social forces that reshape AUKUS." — confidence: high

- [F2] The paradigmatic adversary is explicitly identified as realist, state-centric international relations, whose strategic focus is said to omit the geoeconomic order. — anchor: "A common state-centric focus omits how AUKUS, as both a security partnership and a military alliance, engages in the emerging geoeconomic order." — confidence: high

- [F3] This paradigm makes the independent validity of strategic threat assessments difficult to investigate because geopolitical explanations are generally treated as partial or fetishised. — anchor: "This corresponds with Jayasuriya’s coinage (2021) of ‘geopolitical fetishism’ that privileges geopolitical contestation over the underlying social and class relations of capitalist transformation." — confidence: medium

- [F4] The specific phenomenon to be explained is AUKUS’s simultaneous operation as a security partnership and an institutional reorganisation of economic and political relations. — anchor: "AUKUS is distinctive in that it is both a security partnership and a mutation of neoliberalism emerging from the context of geoeconomic conflict and bipartisan politics." — confidence: high

- [F5] “Militarised neoliberalism” is the framework’s central concept and denotes market coordination linked to security institutions and alliances. — anchor: "Wijaya and Jayasuriya (2024) coin the transformation of the geopolitical and geoeconomic order over the past few years as militarised neoliberalism" — confidence: high

- [F6] MN supplements rather than replaces neoliberalism: the article conceptualises current changes as a mutation of the Washington Consensus order. — anchor: "MN is a mutation of neoliberalism that first found expression in what policymakers and scholars called the Washington Consensus." — confidence: high

- [F7] The concept overturns a simple market-versus-state opposition by construing interventionist security policy as a means of creating and coordinating markets. — anchor: "Such geopolitical fetishism yields an incomplete analysis, dismissing the fact that MN also takes the form of AUKUS, which reflects a fusion of market instruments and geoeconomic warfare." — confidence: high

- [F8] The primary mechanism is institutional layering: security imperatives enter economic agencies while economic functions enter security institutions. — anchor: "MN entails the gradual process of state transformation which is marked by the layering of security imperatives on economic institutions and vice versa." — confidence: high

- [F9] Bipartisanship is proposed as the social and political enabling condition through which otherwise competing factions provide market routes to favoured industries. — anchor: "We observe the reconciling of two otherwise competing political groups or factions, such as Democrat vs. Republican or Labor vs. Liberal, to provide a particular market route" — confidence: high

- [F10] Regulatory control is narrowed and adapted from an existing concept to mean geopolitically motivated coordination, policy networks, and regulation that reshape markets. — anchor: "Here, we adopt a narrower understanding of conceptions of regulatory control, referring to the varying market coordination, policy networks, and regulations motivated by geopolitics" — confidence: high

- [F11] The framework specifies three linked dimensions—bipartisan state form and regulation, combined capitalist development, and incorporation of a new business class. — anchor: "In the following sections, we analyse three salient features of MN embedded in AUKUS." — confidence: high

- [F12] The first mechanism connects bipartisan legitimacy to regulatory controls that recombine state support and market instruments for strategically selected sectors. — anchor: "underneath the preservation of hegemony are a series of regulatory controls which repurposes economic and security agencies and recombines market instruments" — confidence: high

- [F13] The second mechanism is state-led capitalist development through nearshoring, friendshoring, public capital, and coordination of private-public participation. — anchor: "These industrial policies see a set of activist state strategies—coordinating private-public participation, midwifing new industries, and building champions." — confidence: high

- [F14] The third mechanism incorporates venture capital and start-ups into security policy, with the state supporting their structural market power. — anchor: "we observe how the state reconciles the interests of VC with its accumulation vehicles (start-ups), thereby helping boost their ‘power to structure’" — confidence: high

- [F15] “Technological sovereignty” is analysed as a travelling policy concept whose connotations of autonomy and national survival legitimise market-making support for start-ups. — anchor: "the notion of technological sovereignty becomes a convenient banner to orchestrate market-creating actions for the survival of start-ups." — confidence: high

- [F16] “Spatial fix,” borrowed from Harvey’s geographical political economy, transfers the idea that surplus capital resolves accumulation pressures by moving into newly produced investment spaces. — anchor: "AUKUS has been the main component of financial capital’s ‘spatial fix’ (see Harvey 2001)." — confidence: medium

- [F17] The article combines MN with developmental-state theory but reverses the latter’s usual anti-neoliberal connotation by making state developmentalism constitutive of neoliberal globalisation. — anchor: "At the heart of the MN thesis, we see state developmentalism as constitutive of neoliberal globalisation, reshaping new geographies of production and creating the market" — confidence: high

- [F18] It also combines military-industrial-complex analysis with scholarship on universities and R&D, producing the expanded idea of a military-industrial-academic complex. — anchor: "The AUKUS-enabled industrial-military complex, or the upgraded version, in Troath’s coinage (2023), the ‘military-industrial-academic complex’" — confidence: high

- [F19] The article acknowledges historical resemblance to Cold War military-economic development but limits the analogy rather than treating current institutions as identical. — anchor: "the current development trajectory only partially resembles the Cold War military-economic institutions." — confidence: high

- [F20] The empirical scope explicitly includes archival and secondary data and extends beyond member states to Japan and South Korea. — anchor: "Our analysis is not only limited to AUKUS member states, but also extends to Japan and South Korea." — confidence: high

- [F21] The principal observable indicators are regulatory reforms, state financing, procurement, grants, agency repurposing, and public-private initiatives associated with Pillar II. — anchor: "In this paper, we engage with archival sources and secondary data pertaining to AUKUS and interrogate a manifest nexus of geopolitics and political economy." — confidence: high

- [F22] The theory-evidence connection is strongest where concrete institutional changes show economic agencies being incorporated into security architecture. — anchor: "the Department of Industry, Science, and Resources has been drawn closer to the security architecture of the state, making SMEs central in shaping defence-industrial policy." — confidence: medium

- [F23] The theory-evidence gap is greatest in the move from identifying beneficiaries to asserting coherent class coalitions and represented interests; the stated source base does not itself demonstrate influence or coordination. — anchor: "These economic groups’ interests are neatly represented as national interest, benefitting from the political manoeuvring of both political parties" — confidence: medium

- [F24] The inference is predominantly theory-to-evidence: the paper announces MN and its three features before presenting cases organised to elaborate those dynamics. — anchor: "Before explaining these dynamics, we discuss our conceptual framework below." — confidence: high

- [F25] The article gives an explicit negative scope condition by excluding evaluation of AUKUS’s effect on regional conflict. — anchor: "it is beyond the scope of this paper to assess whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific region towards conflict." — confidence: high

- [F26] The paper strains against its own anti-geopolitical orientation by conceding that AUKUS cannot simply be reduced to fetishism and occupies a genuine geopolitical conjuncture. — anchor: "This is not to say that AUKUS as a security partnership is completely geopolitically fetishist." — confidence: high

- [F27] The “mutation” formulation creates a possible falsifiability problem: policies reversing free trade are retained within neoliberalism rather than clearly marking its boundary or end. — anchor: "These policies, conceived of as the New Washington Consensus (NWC), not only justify the reversal of an ideological commitment to free trade" — confidence: medium

- [F28] The article’s strongest defensible contribution is middle-range: it identifies how Pillar II links specific state institutions, financing mechanisms, and fractions of capital, while broader claims about global reconstruction remain less directly tested. — anchor: "Pillar II has become a business in its own right." — confidence: medium

### Counter-evidence

- [C1] Bipartisanship is neither complete nor frictionless: internal Labor opposition complicates its treatment as a consolidated state form. — anchor: "despite some internal opposition" — confidence: high

- [C2] US bipartisan support is qualified by congressional disagreement and uncertainty about what bipartisan national-security agreement entails. — anchor: "Bipartisanship on national security in the USA, however, remains unclear as to what that means outside its traditional objectives." — confidence: high

- [C3] Delays and possible US non-delivery challenge any assumption that the alliance reliably generates the intended capabilities or benefits for all selected firms. — anchor: "severe delays emerging out of the devastating state of the UK submarine-building industry and the US Congress’s predisposition to deny the delivery of the submarine" — confidence: high

- [C4] The article recognises losers as well as beneficiaries, including sectors marginalised by uncertainty, which complicates the image of a seamless capitalist coalition. — anchor: "This has raised concerns among political blocs and further marginalised the interests of select defence industry players" — confidence: high

- [C5] Some policies may plausibly serve security, employment, resilience, or technological capability rather than merely masking accumulation; the article records these objectives but does not independently adjudicate them. — anchor: "AUKUS presents opportunities for Australia to bolster manufacturing, job opportunities, and government-owned and private defence contractors, and cultivate the requisite skills." — confidence: medium

### Open questions

- What evidence would distinguish militarised neoliberalism from military Keynesianism, developmental statism, economic nationalism, or state capitalism?
- What observations could falsify the classification of AUKUS as a mutation of neoliberalism?
- Did business groups and venture-capital firms cause the regulatory changes, or did they adapt opportunistically after security decisions had been made?
- How are the claimed “losers and winners” distributed across workers, regions, taxpayers, firms, and sectors?
- Does bipartisanship enable regulatory transformation, or does an independently established security consensus produce both bipartisanship and regulatory change?
- How representative are the selected grants, contracts, and firms of the wider AUKUS political economy?
- Do the programmes generate durable productive capability, or mainly transfers, asset appreciation, and dependence on public procurement?
- How would realist threat assessments change the causal account if China-related security risks were treated as independently consequential rather than primarily discursive?
