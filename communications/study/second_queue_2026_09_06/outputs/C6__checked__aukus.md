# Framework map: AUKUS as a reorganisation of markets, states, and security

The text’s composition makes visible AUKUS not only as an alliance directed against China, but as an institutional site where security imperatives, industrial policy, regulatory authority, and different forms of capital are recombined [F1], [F4]. Its declared framework is **militarised neoliberalism (MN)**, defined as market coordination linked to security institutions and alliances [F1], [F2]. This framework competes with state-centric realism over the explanation of the same object: realism foregrounds interstate rivalry, whereas MN examines the social relations and state transformations underlying that rivalry [F6].

The strongest source-evidenced limit is substantive: the paper does not assess whether AUKUS will produce regional conflict [F12]. A second qualification arises from its own applications: submarine delays and possible US non-delivery could leave Australia without the sovereign capability promised by Pillar I [F13]. Thus, MN illuminates institutional and distributive restructuring more directly than either military effectiveness or conflict probability.

## What the MN lens reorganises

The framework begins by changing what counts as the central problem. Mainstream realist and minilateral accounts treat AUKUS principally as a strategic response to major-power competition. The paper argues that this common state-centric focus omits how AUKUS participates in an emerging geoeconomic order [F6]. MN therefore does not reject geopolitics altogether; it relocates geopolitical rivalry within transformations of markets, institutions, and social relations.

Its foundational component is a **system of market coordination linked to security institutions and specific alliances** [F2]. “Militarised” does not simply mean increased defence expenditure. It denotes the layering of security imperatives onto economic institutions and the adaptation of economic institutions to military priorities [F3]. “Neoliberalism” is likewise not treated as synonymous with an untouched free market. The text presents MN as a mutation in which regulatory controls, state finance, and industrial activism reorganise markets in favour of selected groups.

That architecture makes **state transformation** the mechanism connecting geopolitics to political economy. Military agencies acquire control over financing and procurement, while economic agencies adopt the language and functions of security [F3]. State and market are consequently not rival explanatory poles: intervention actively constitutes markets, channels accumulation, and reorganises relationships among firms, finance, and public agencies [F4].

The decisive dependency is therefore:

**bipartisan coalition → regulatory authority and state transformation → market creation and sectoral accumulation.**

This is not merely a sequence inferred from repeated vocabulary. The text explicitly identifies bipartisanship as MN’s social foundation and says it permits regulatory controls that reconsolidate state- and market-led industrial policy [F7]. Those controls then generate opportunities for defence firms, critical-minerals suppliers, civilian conglomerates, SMEs, technology start-ups, and venture capital.

## The principal applications

The critical-minerals example enacts the framework particularly clearly. Cooperation under Pillar II joins supply-chain security to manufacturing, clean energy, defence capability, public finance, and export-control exemptions [F10]. Under a conventional alliance lens these measures appear as resilience or strategic competition. Under MN they become evidence of agencies being repurposed as investors, regulators, contractors, and financiers while selected suppliers gain access to transnational state support.

The same logic structures the text’s treatment of Japan and South Korea. Their inclusion broadens the analysis beyond formal AUKUS membership and shows how regulatory reforms and industrial partnerships can draw non-member defence industries into Pillar II. This supports the paper’s claim that “like-minded” coalitions reconnect markets and capital rather than simply enclosing sovereign power blocs.

The venture-capital application extends the framework from established industrial firms to a new business class. The paper treats defence-investment networks, innovation bodies, state-backed funds, and export-control reform as mechanisms that bring VC and start-ups closer to the state [F9]. Pillar II thereby appears not only as a technology programme but as an accumulation chain in which public capital and deregulation reduce risks for private investors.

## Named framework, inferred profile, and translations

**Militarised neoliberalism is declared**, not inferred: the text expressly says it approaches AUKUS through that lens [F1]. By contrast, “geopolitical economy” is best treated here as an **inferred method profile**. The paper repeatedly reconnects geopolitical relations to internal state structures, class fractions, and capital accumulation, but the supplied text does not declare adherence to a single named geopolitical-economy school [F5]. The inference is therefore about vocabulary and procedure, not authorial biography or training.

The framework also translates three concepts from cited discussions:

1. **Washington Consensus/neoliberalism:** MN is presented as a mutation rather than a simple termination of market-oriented neoliberalism. Security institutions, state finance, and industrial policy are incorporated into market coordination.
2. **Regulatory control:** the text explicitly narrows the cited conception to geopolitically motivated forms of market coordination, policy networks, and regulation [F8].
3. **Structural power of venture capital:** “power to structure” is applied locally to the state-supported relationship among capital providers, start-ups, and defence markets [F9].

These translations preserve attention to market constitution and class coalitions while adapting it to the economic-security nexus surrounding AUKUS.

## Strongest counter-passage and settled result

The strongest internal counter-passage concerns material delivery. The framework explains how submarine policy reorganises institutions and beneficiaries, but the text acknowledges that industrial delay and congressional non-delivery may produce little concrete capability and leave Australia without sovereign submarines [F13]. This prevents institutional mobilisation from being equated with strategic success.

What is settled locally is that the paper explicitly structures its inquiry through MN and uses it to connect bipartisanship, regulatory controls, state transformation, industrial coalitions, and venture capital. What remains unanswered is exact: **do these market-making and institutional transformations generate durable military-industrial capability, and with what effect on the probability of Indo-Pacific conflict?**

### Counter-evidence

The article cautions that AUKUS’s political and financial mobilisation does not guarantee delivery. Submarine-industry delays and possible congressional obstruction could leave Australia without a sovereign submarine capability [F13]. It also explicitly declines to determine whether the partnership increases regional conflict risk [F12].

### Open questions

- How are losses and benefits distributed across groups not included in the paper’s selected cases?
- Does the restructuring identified by MN increase deterrence, conflict risk, or neither?
