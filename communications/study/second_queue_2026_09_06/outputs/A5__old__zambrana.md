## The reading, then:

Zambrana’s article is organized by a productive conflict with Gillian Rose. Rose charges Marx and Marxism with preserving the neo-Kantian separation of thought from actuality; Zambrana accepts Rose’s criterion of speculative thinking but turns it against Rose’s judgment of Marx. Marx’s accounts of value, self-valorization, and interest-bearing capital are presented as speculative precisely because they do not oppose essence to appearance: capital’s apparently autonomous movement is a real and effective manifestation of its structure. The article’s central synthesis is thus explicitly “with and against Rose”: Rose supplies the method, while Marx supplies an object that exceeds Rose’s assessment of him.

The foundational opposition is representational versus speculative thinking. Representational thinking fixes knower and known, subject and object, intention and deed as externally related terms. Speculative thinking instead reconstructs their determinate identities through processes of negation, externalization, and return. This opposition is not simply abolished, however. Misrecognition remains necessary because speculative comprehension occurs as re-cognition: thought must first undergo the failure of its fixed distinctions. Representational thought is therefore both criticized and structurally required by the speculative movement that corrects it.

This methodological dialectic is reproduced in the treatment of essence and appearance. Zambrana rejects an account in which appearance conceals a deeper, independently existing essence. “Essence must appear”: the fetishized or mystified form is not merely false consciousness but capital’s real mode of manifestation. This permits the article to reinterpret inversion and irrationality as socially effective forms rather than subjective errors. Yet the synthesis does not eliminate the distinction between truth and misrecognition. It displaces that distinction into the difference between an isolated appearance and the same appearance comprehended within the totality of conditions producing it.

The internal Marxist conflict concerns whether commodity fetishism and production provide an adequate account of capital. Lukács’s commodity-centered theory is credited with identifying thingification and alienated social relations, but it is judged too restricted: reification becomes shorthand for capital as a whole, and revolutionary transformation risks being reduced to a change in consciousness. Zambrana’s distinction between fetishism and mystification preserves Lukács’s account of thingification while expanding analysis to money, finance, and self-valorization. The privileged explanatory term consequently shifts from commodity to value, from production alone to the metamorphoses connecting production, circulation, and finance.

Smallwood’s intervention intensifies rather than closes this expansion. The distinction between capitalism and its supposedly precapitalist conditions is rejected: Atlantic slavery is not a preliminary episode superseded by industrial wage labor but constitutive of the capitalist system and continuously active in its institutions and protocols. This destabilizes several mutually reinforcing binaries—free/unfree labor, modern/pre-modern, capitalism/slavery, system/prehistory—and exposes “free” labor as historically and structurally dependent on coerced labor. The excluded third position that becomes thinkable is neither a simple identity nor a clean separation between slavery and capitalism, but their differentiated articulation within one historical totality.

There is genuine spiral development: the same problem—how a thing relates to its coming-to-be—returns at the levels of Hegelian method, Marxian value, fetishism, finance, and colonial slavery. Each return enlarges the relevant totality. Still, the article’s synthesis leaves constitutive tensions open. If all appearances are real manifestations of capital, criteria are needed to distinguish mystification from adequate comprehension. If capital’s “totality of conditions” includes slavery, expropriation, production, circulation, and finance, the boundaries of that totality remain uncertain. Finally, the account oscillates between treating value as an “active” or “dominant subject” and insisting that capital is historically produced by violent social relations. That tension is productive—the point is to understand objective social forms as effective without naturalizing them—but it is not fully resolved.

### Counter-evidence

- The claim that Lukács reduces transformation to consciousness is qualified by the article’s admission that his account contains substantial truth: "Certainly, there is much truth to this rendering of Marx—a rendering that Rose reproduces"

- Although the argument rejects production-centered analysis, it does not deny production’s importance; it expands the field to include production, circulation, and finance: "Value appears in its reality as the subjectivity of the actuality that is capital by organizing production, exchange, but also ﬁnance"

- The article’s language of capital “freeing itself” from material limits could suggest actual independence, but it repeatedly qualifies that autonomy as seeming or mediated: "The inverted appearance of the value-form—paradigmatically, as we will see, interest-bearing capital—is a necessary form of appearance"

- The incorporation of slavery into capitalist totality is not presented as collapsing enslaved and wage labor into identical relations. The text retains the distinction between labor alienated freely and labor alienated coercively: "the commodiﬁcation of labor power as free labor, while in truth indexing its origin and perpetuation through updated forms of alienation of unfree labor in the trade"

- The article resists a closed or triumphalist synthesis by defining speculative thought as tracking non-identity rather than eliminating it: "speculative thinking tracks rather than dissolves the “lack of identity” that grounds actuality."

### Open questions

- If mystified appearances are real manifestations of capital, what criterion distinguishes mystification from an adequate speculative account without reinstalling a rigid essence/appearance hierarchy?

- How far does the “totality of conditions” extend, and what prevents the concept of capital from absorbing every historical condition into itself?

- Does calling value the “active subject” risk personifying capital in the same way that the critique of fetishism seeks to explain?

- How are the differences between plantation slavery, later racialized coercion, and wage labor preserved once all are placed within one capitalist totality?

- What concrete forms of revolutionary practice follow from shifting analysis beyond production and the wage form?

- Does the rejection of “primitive accumulation” as prehistory require abandoning the concept entirely, or can it be reconstructed as an ongoing process internal to capital?

- How should the article’s appeal to absolute necessity be reconciled with the political possibility that capitalist relations could have developed otherwise or could cease to exist?
