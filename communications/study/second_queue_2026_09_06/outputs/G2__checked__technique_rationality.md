# Reading

## Position map

The most consequential continuity across the collection is the rejection of **means-efficiency as a sufficient standard of social rationality**. In **“Technique” (1984)**, technique is reconstructed as effective and creative making/doing, then used to contest both the neutral-instrument thesis and technological determinism: technique is “neither an instrument nor a cause” of social creation but one of its dimensions [F1] [F4]. The important qualification is that technical activity considered narrowly can still be judged by efficiency; the broader denial of neutrality concerns the social-historical technological ensemble [F6].

In **“The ‘Rationality’ of Capitalism” (1997)**, the concept at issue is historically narrower. Instrumental rationality means conformity of an operation to an assumed goal, while capitalist economic rationality reduces that relation to quantitative maximization of “product” and minimization of “costs” [F9] [F10]. Rationality consequently serves both as capitalism’s distinctive form of legitimation and as the disputed name for its drive toward unlimited mastery [F11] [F12]. The qualification is again consequential: capitalist technology can be effective relative to its restricted ends even though that efficacy does not establish the rationality of those ends or of the regime [F14].

The works therefore share an inferential structure but differ in scope and method. “Technique” offers philosophical-historical reconstruction across a domain “co-extensive with the whole of human history”; “Rationality” declares an immanent critique of capitalist economic ideology [F20]. Their publication dates establish the order 1984–1997, but not a demonstrated intellectual development: the later essay does not cite “Technique” or identify its formulations as revisions. What follows can establish continuity, changed concentration, and divergence, but not explicit uptake.

## “Technique”: creation within social institution

### Effective making and the creation of types

“Technique” opens from a contemporary concern: technique has become more than an object of research and reflection and is now “the source of an ever-growing concern” [F22]. The essay presents public discussion as caught between admiration for technology and denunciation of it, corresponding to the theoretical antithesis between technique as a pure instrument and technique as an autonomous destiny.

Its reconstruction first separates senses that the subsequent argument needs. In contemporary usage, technique includes both “the power of producing” and “a coherent set of already produced means” in which that power is embodied [F1]. The concept therefore names neither an isolated tool nor disembodied knowledge. It relates productive capacity, practice, instruments, and an organized technical ensemble.

The stronger claim is that technique creates rather than merely rearranges. A technical object is not adequately understood as an alteration of a prior natural state. It establishes “a universal type” or *eidos* that can exist through multiple empirical instances [F2]. Physical regularities constrain creation, but they do not supply obligatory human purposes. Technique uses the world’s regularities and indeterminate openings to institute forms—wheels, pulleys, written signs, computers—for which nature supplies no model in the relevant sense.

This makes technique inseparable from the historical constitution of needs. The text does not treat human problems as fixed while techniques progressively discover necessary solutions. Ends and objects are historically instituted, and technique participates in specifying what can be desired and practically pursued.

### Dimension, not determinant

The central argumentative move rejects the separability presupposed by technological determinism. A causal thesis in which technique determines social organization would require determinant and determined to be independently isolable. The essay argues that this “essential prerequisite” is not fulfilled [F5]. A technical object exists technically only within an ensemble of dexterities, institutions, economic arrangements, and significations.

The positive alternative is not reverse causation. Technique is “neither an instrument nor a cause”; it is a dimension of society’s creation of its world [F4]. The topological metaphor sharpens that proposition: technique is an “everywhere dense sub-set” [F8]. The transferred relation is pervasiveness—technique appears wherever society constitutes what counts as effective and real-rational—but the set remains a subset. Institution and society are not reduced to technique.

The essay’s evidence follows this relational account. Its opening aligns anthropological material with philosophical reflection on technique and language [F3]. Later historical examples show that available techniques do not dictate a unique society. Workplace conflict provides a more concentrated mechanism: the selection of a technique is “both an instrument of class struggle and among the stakes of that struggle” [F7]. Technique has effects, but its selection, application, and organization are mediated by conflicts that the material object alone cannot determine.

### The qualification concerning efficiency

The strongest counter-pressure to a sweeping rejection of neutrality comes from the essay itself. “Technical activity, considered in and for itself,” does not evaluate the ends assigned to it, and in this restricted respect efficiency is its touchstone [F6]. This distinguishes a bridge that collapses from a technically sound bridge built where none is needed.

The qualification does not restore the neutral-instrument thesis at social scale. Once the object becomes an epoch’s aggregate technological ensemble, means are invested in installations, routines, knowledge, specialized personnel, habits, and significations. They both permit and constrain social choices. The local validity of efficiency therefore cannot establish the neutrality of the ensemble.

This limit also governs the political conclusion. Conscious technological transformation would remain conditioned by inherited technology and by unresolved questions of scale, ecological balance, centralization, and social coordination. The essay proposes technology as an object of collective political transformation without claiming that a desired post-revolutionary form can already be specified.

## “The ‘Rationality’ of Capitalism”: instituted criteria and immanent critique

### From instrumental rationality to rational mastery

The later essay distinguishes three connected senses. First, instrumental rationality means the conformity of an operation to a presupposed goal. Because it does not evaluate that goal, it “has no value in itself” [F9]. An efficient means of poisoning or mass destruction remains efficient without becoming defensible.

Second, capitalist economic rationality gives this formal relation a quantitative content. It is “maximization/minimization”—maximizing a regime-defined “product” and minimizing regime-defined “costs” [F10]. The definition is not neutral because capitalism determines what enters either category. Environmental destruction and the degradation of work can disappear from the calculation even while output rises.

Third, “rational mastery” names the wider social imaginary joining calculability, technical transformation, bureaucratic organization, and the pursuit of control across production, consumption, law, education, and politics [F12]. The quotation marks mark a dispute: this is not rationality without qualification but capitalism’s historically instituted conception of it.

### Rationality as legitimation

The concept’s principal argumentative role is to expose capitalism’s self-legitimation. The essay calls capitalism “the first social regime to produce an ideology according to which it would be ‘rational’” [F11]. Earlier societies legitimated institutions through religion, myth, ancestry, or tradition; capitalism presents its own historically instituted criterion as though it were universal.

This legitimation is circular. Capitalism establishes quantitative economic rationality as a central value and then cites conformity to that value as evidence of its superiority. The essay does not criticize this merely by appealing to an external conception of the good. It declares that its critique “will basically be an immanent one” and will judge capitalism according to its own criteria [F13].

That procedure connects conceptual and empirical objections. Economic theory claims calculability, separate imputation, determinacy, equilibrium, and optimization; the essay opposes these claims with heterogeneous capital, incomplete information, oligopoly, expectations, internal bureaucratic conflict, worker resistance, and indeterminate labor output. The argument is not that economic activity lacks all regularity. It is that the models turn approximate, institution-dependent regularities into universal quantitative laws.

### Restricted efficacy and historically scoped force

The later essay, like “Technique,” preserves a limited efficacy claim. Capitalist technology is “narrowly oriented,” yet “adequate to the ends being pursued” [F14]. Capitalism can produce a particular collection of goods and reduce the costs it recognizes. That concession helps explain its durability without validating either its ends or its exclusions.

The strongest negative formulation concerns the deregulated phase described by the essay: “In such a situation, there is no point in discussing any ‘rationality’ of capitalism whatsoever” [F16]. “In such a situation” is essential. The sentence addresses the contemporary dismantling of political and social controls, not every isolated capitalist calculation in every period.

The metaphors intensify this historically specific diagnosis. The “planetary casino” represents the world economy as speculative gambling [F23]. The market as a “moderately savage jungle” represents survival under power, manipulation, and coercion rather than selection for a social optimum [F15]. These images frame the result of capitalist rationalization; the essay’s explanatory concepts remain institutional and social-historical.

### The main continuity

The strongest paired finding concerns means and ends. In 1984, efficiency remains a valid technical criterion within an isolated operation, but it cannot establish the neutrality of a social-historical technological ensemble. In 1997, instrumental rationality likewise measures conformity to an assumed goal without validating that goal. Both works therefore concede means-efficiency while denying that it validates ends or the social whole [F17].

The objects are not identical. “Technique” asks how making/doing belongs to social creation; “Rationality” asks how capitalism turns a restricted form of calculation into a universal claim to legitimacy. What persists is the argumentative operation: isolate the limited truth of efficiency, then prevent its illicit elevation into an account of society.

### From the real-rational to rational mastery

The 1984 text says technique is present wherever a society constitutes what is, for it, the “real-rational.” The 1997 text identifies the “unlimited extension of ‘rational mastery’” as capitalism’s core social imaginary [F18]. The later formulation thus gives a historically specific object to the earlier general account: capitalism constitutes its world through calculability, mastery, maximization, and cost reduction.

The terms should not be treated as synonyms. “Real-rational” names what a society institutes as reality, resistance, and effective order. “Rational mastery” names a particular capitalist drive to extend control. The relation is therefore continuity with narrowed historical scope, not simple terminological identity. Because the later essay does not cite the earlier one, the corpus cannot show whether Castoriadis regarded this as an explicit revision.

### The repeated claim about labor

The closest verbal continuity concerns capitalist technology’s orientation toward labor. “Technique” describes a direction “towards the suppression of man’s human role in production”; “Rationality” says technical change is oriented toward “the reduction, then the elimination, of man’s role in production” [F19]. In the earlier essay, this supports the account of technological selection as part of workplace conflict. In the later essay, it becomes one component of the wider push toward rational mastery.

The later text therefore changes the claim’s argumentative concentration, not its basic content. It does not return to technological determinism: technical change appears alongside state formation, bureaucratization, anthropological mutation, market institutions, and social conflict.

### Methodological divergence

The methods change with the objects. “Technique” combines etymology, Greek philosophy, Marx, anthropology, historical comparison, and workplace conflict within a survey extending across human history. “Rationality” explicitly organizes an immanent critique of political economy and the effective operation of capitalism [F20].

This is the strongest evidence against describing the later work as a simple restatement. Creation, technical ensembles, and the indeterminacy of ends dominate the earlier argument; economic calculation, theoretical incoherence, and deregulated globalization dominate the later one. But methodological divergence is not evidence of rupture. The later essay’s failure to repeat “absolute creation” cannot establish abandonment, because its stated object does not require a new ontology of technical invention.

## What is settled

Across the supplied works, the position is structurally stable. Technique and rationality are not autonomous mechanisms standing outside social institution. Their meanings and effects depend on relations among means, ends, knowledge, power, conflict, and social imaginary significations.

The later work narrows and intensifies this framework. It treats capitalism’s distinctive rationality as an unlimited extension of quantitative mastery and makes rationality’s legitimating role central. At the same time, it retains the earlier qualification: restricted efficacy is real, but it cannot establish overall neutrality, legitimacy, or rationality [F21].

The strongest counter-passage to any thesis of total rejection is therefore not a contradiction but the paired concession: “Technique” allows efficiency as the narrow touchstone of technical activity [F6], and “Rationality” calls capitalist technology adequate to its restricted ends [F14]. These passages rule out describing either work as claiming sheer ineffectiveness.

The exact remaining question is whether the movement from the general “real-rational” to capitalist “rational mastery” was an explicit revision of “Technique.” The supplied texts establish publication order, repeated claims, and changed scope, but the 1997 essay provides no citation or uptake statement that would demonstrate such a development.

### Counter-evidence

No passage contradicts the central paired findings. The strongest qualifying passages are:

- “technique does appear as value-free, referred to efficiency as its only touchstone of value.” — doc: technique [F6]
- “True, this technology is itself narrowly oriented, but it is adequate to the ends being pursued” — doc: rationality [F14]

These qualify the global critique by preserving restricted efficacy; they do not restore neutrality or overall rationality.

### Open questions

- Does “rational mastery” explicitly revise the earlier category of the “real-rational,” or merely apply a compatible framework to capitalism?
- The publication dates establish order, but the 1997 essay’s lack of citation to “Technique” leaves uptake undemonstrated.
- The collection does not determine how the proposed conscious transformation of technology would resolve scale, centralization, and ecological constraints.
