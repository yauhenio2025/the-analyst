The article’s central modal achievement is to replace a strong necessity thesis—habit necessarily produces unfreedom—with a differentiated account of two deaths. “Natural death” is presented as necessary but temporally restricted: a social form necessarily stagnates once it has exhausted its determinate historical principle. “Spiritual death,” by contrast, is contingent but permanently possible: individual subjects can lapse into mechanical submission, although habituation does not force them to do so. The resulting “weak critical” thesis is that habit necessarily carries a threat of unfreedom, not that it necessarily actualizes unfreedom.

This differentiation is conceptually illuminating, but its modalities operate in several registers. The paper distinguishes conceptual necessity from historical tendency and explicit inevitability more carefully than the strong critical reading it opposes. Nevertheless, its own claims sometimes move among Hegel’s systematic logic, Elling’s reconstruction of that logic, developmental analogies, and empirical diagnoses of modernity. In particular, “historically necessary,” “inevitability,” “tendency,” “temptation,” and “risk” are not integrated into a precise account of probability or causal sufficiency.

The possibility of reflective habit is supported by a coherent ordinary example—failing to brush one’s teeth when circumstances change—and by the functional claim that automaticity can enable higher reflection. This effectively refutes the universal proposition that every habit is mechanically executed. It does not, by itself, establish the stronger social possibility that a whole institutional second nature can remain both stable and reflectively appropriable. The paper’s proposed route through philosophical education identifies a conceivable response, but does not show that it is sufficient or institutionally realizable.

The argument about natural death is the least modally secure. It posits a threshold at which a society has “fully realized” its principle, after which stagnation necessarily begins. Yet no non-circular criterion is given for determining when realization is complete, and the paper itself later questions whether an end of history is possible. This makes the claim vulnerable to vague-boundary and sorites-style objections: incremental habituation offers no obvious point at which vitality becomes necessary stagnation.

Modernity is treated as contingent in its concrete historical realization but structurally revealing of any society claiming to actualize universal freedom. This conditionalization is important: the paper does not need nineteenth-century Prussia actually to have completed history. Still, it moves close to historical necessity when diagnosing modern subjects as increasingly exposed to parabasis, while ultimately preserving contingency by requiring an active, though possibly unconscious, subjective reversal.

No major conflation of epistemic and metaphysical possibility occurs. The modalities are chiefly conceptual, developmental, historical, psychological, and practical. Nor does the argument straightforwardly derive obligations from purely alethic premises: its concluding demand for case-by-case reflection is better understood as a practical prescription grounded in its diagnosis of unavoidable risk. Even so, the transition from “risk is structurally present” to “we must always reflect” needs an additional deontic premise about appropriate responses to threats to freedom.

## Counter-evidence

The paper contains several qualifications that restrain its modal claims. It directly rejects the idea that its account proves inevitable unfreedom: “This volatility increases dramatically in modern society, but it never entails the conceptual necessity of unfreedom.” It also concedes that the proposed historical endpoint is disputable: “We may not be quite as optimistic as Hegel was about the finality of the world spirit's development in 19th-century Prussia.” Finally, it allows institutional measures to mitigate alienation even if they cannot eliminate risk, so the account is not simply fatalistic.

## Open questions

- What non-circular criterion determines that a society has “fully realized” its principle, rather than merely entering another gradual phase of reinterpretation?
- Is “the necessity of the threat” stronger than the claim that the conceptual structure merely permits spiritual death? The text does not formalize what makes a risk necessary.
- What probability, disposition, or causal tendency is expressed by “temptation,” “risk,” “threat,” and “actively encourages,” and are these terms interchangeable?
- Can philosophical education reliably mediate subjective authority and normative stability, or does that proposal presuppose the very reflective capacity endangered by habit?
- Would the analysis survive alternative histories in which modern societies repeatedly revise the meaning of universal freedom rather than approach a terminal actualization?
- What additional deontic premise licenses the move from an unavoidable possibility of deformation to the claim that subjects “must” continually scrutinize concrete norms?
