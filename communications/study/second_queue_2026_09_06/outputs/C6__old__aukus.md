## The reading

Wijaya and Hayes organise their analysis around **militarised neoliberalism (MN)**, a critical political-economy framework intended to displace state-centric, realist interpretations of AUKUS. Their central move is ontological as much as explanatory: AUKUS should not be treated primarily as an alliance responding to China, but as a site where state institutions, security imperatives, markets, class fractions, and accumulation strategies are being recombined. The foundational opposition is therefore **geopolitical appearance versus political-economic and social relations**. Conventional accounts see deterrence, alliances, sovereignty, and great-power competition; the authors ask which interests, markets, and institutional transformations these categories conceal.

MN is defined as both a **mutation of neoliberalism** and a **system of market coordination linked to security institutions and alliances**. “Mutation” permits the authors to argue simultaneously for historical continuity and institutional novelty. Interventionist industrial policy, subsidies, export controls, state finance, and military procurement do not represent neoliberalism’s simple demise. They reorganise markets and accumulation in favour of selected firms and forms of capital. This position opposes both realist interpretations and narratives of a straightforward “crisis of neoliberalism.” The paper’s distinctive thesis is that intensified state intervention may be constitutive of neoliberal restructuring rather than its negation.

The concept is genealogically dependent on earlier work, especially Wijaya and Jayasuriya’s formulation of MN and Jayasuriya’s “geopolitical fetishism.” Other settled concepts—state transformation, regulatory control, spatial fix, structural power, geopolitical scripting, and complex adaptive systems—are explicitly imported from adjacent critical literatures. The resulting terminological cluster places the paper within critical international political economy, critical geopolitics, and broadly Marxian theories of capital accumulation and class power. Concepts such as “fractions of capital,” “capitalist accumulation,” “spatial fix,” and “fetishism” carry substantial theoretical authority, although their mechanisms are more often demonstrated through patterned institutional examples than derived systematically in this article.

The causal architecture has three linked levels. First, **bipartisanship** supplies the political foundation for security policy and suppresses ordinary partisan contestation. Second, **regulatory controls and state transformation** layer security imperatives onto economic institutions while giving security agencies developmental and financial roles. Third, these changes reorganise capital by favouring defence manufacturers, civilian conglomerates, critical-mineral firms, technology start-ups, and venture capital. The paper consequently repurposes “bipartisanship”: rather than merely denoting elite consensus, it becomes a state form that condenses social relations and facilitates market-making for selected groups.

The characteristic argumentative method is **abductive and genealogical**, supplemented by comparative case accumulation. The authors begin from an inadequacy in existing explanations, introduce MN as the more encompassing lens, specify three salient features, and assemble examples from Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, and South Korea. Evidence consists predominantly of government announcements, legislation, funding allocations, contracts, institutional changes, corporate developments, archival sources, and secondary scholarship. Repeated conjunctions—“underneath,” “rather,” “not simply,” and “reconnects, rather than deconstructs”—function analytically by redescribing visible security measures as mechanisms of market formation and class recomposition.

Several metaphors do important conceptual work. The “lens” metaphor presents MN as a device that reveals relations obscured by realist framing. “Layering” models state transformation as incremental institutional sedimentation rather than abrupt replacement. “Fusion,” “enmeshment,” “intertwining,” and “marriage of convenience” depict security, state, and capital as increasingly inseparable. “Spatial fix” casts AUKUS investment as an outlet for financial capital, while “banner” explains how technological sovereignty legitimates market-creating intervention. These metaphors cluster around a common framework of recombination and relational embedding; they are mature and treated almost as mechanism descriptions.

The framing makes visible beneficiaries, institutional repurposing, and continuities between neoliberal market-making and security-led industrial policy. It makes less visible the strategic efficacy of AUKUS, the independent causal force of perceived military threats, bureaucratic conflict within states, and actors that lose despite belonging to broadly favoured sectors. The authors acknowledge uncertainty, internal opposition, implementation delays, and geopolitical realities, but these operate chiefly as qualifications around a framework whose explanatory direction remains from capitalist transformation toward security policy. The paper establishes a strong interpretive account of how AUKUS can function as political-economic restructuring; it establishes less decisively that accumulation imperatives, rather than security concerns, are causally primary.

## Counter-evidence

The text contains several qualifications that complicate a fully reductionist reading:

- The authors explicitly deny that AUKUS is wholly reducible to geopolitical fetishism. — anchor: "This is not to say that AUKUS as a security partnership is completely geopolitically fetishist." — confidence: high

- They delimit their claim by declining to assess whether AUKUS will produce regional conflict. — anchor: "it is beyond the scope of this paper to assess whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific region towards conflict." — confidence: high

- Evidence of internal Labor opposition complicates the presentation of bipartisanship as seamless political condensation. — anchor: "Fifty of 800 grassroots Labor branches and left-aligned unions passed motions opposing AUKUS and nuclear-powered submarine acquisition." — confidence: high

- Delays and congressional obstruction show that the apparent coalition does not guarantee implementation or stable benefits. — anchor: "severe delays emerging out of the devastating state of the UK submarine-building industry and the US Congress’s predisposition to deny the delivery of the submarine" — confidence: high

- The authors recognise losers within the defence sector, which complicates any simple account of uniform military-capital benefit. — anchor: "further marginalised the interests of select defence industry players such as South Australia’s naval shipbuilding sector and ASC" — confidence: high

- The historical analogy with Cold War military-economic institutions is explicitly limited rather than treated as identity across periods. — anchor: "the current development trajectory only partially resembles the Cold War military-economic institutions." — confidence: high

## Open questions

1. What evidence would distinguish security-driven industrial policy from accumulation-driven security policy when both produce the same institutional changes?
2. How are “particular interests” selected, and what observable process demonstrates that they were translated into national interest rather than merely benefiting after policy adoption?
3. Is bipartisanship a cause of state transformation, an institutional expression of a deeper class coalition, or an outcome of threat perception? The paper uses it in all three registers without fully separating them.
4. How does MN differ empirically from military Keynesianism, state capitalism, economic statecraft, developmental security policy, or the traditional military-industrial complex?
5. What would falsify the MN interpretation? The framework can potentially absorb both market liberalisation and state intervention as forms of neoliberal transformation.
6. Does “spatial fix” accurately describe the geographic displacement of overaccumulated capital here, or is it functioning more loosely as a synonym for investment opportunity?
7. How representative are the listed grants, contracts, and corporate beneficiaries relative to the full distribution of AUKUS expenditure and regulatory effects?
8. How do bureaucratic rivalry, implementation failure, military necessity, and technological constraints modify the proposed coalition of state agencies and fractions of capital?
9. Are Japan and South Korea components of the same MN formation as the three AUKUS members, or comparative cases displaying partially different developmental trajectories?
10. How much explanatory work is done by MN itself, as opposed to its component concepts—state transformation, regulatory control, geopolitical fetishism, structural power, and state developmentalism?
