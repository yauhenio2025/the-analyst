# Reading

## A local inventory of sequence, voice, and perspective

The article is academic exposition rather than a continuously narrated story, but it develops a sustained argumentative sequence containing several local narratives. It opens with Benjamin’s enigmatic claim that capitalism has an “essentially religious” character, then delays direct endorsement while removing the conceptual obstacle to comparison: no stable general definition of religion is available. The first decisive turn comes when the text applies the same definitional “test” to money. The question “can money be defined?” changes the analogy from an initially surprising proposition into a comparison between two phenomena said to resist objective definition [F1]. The order of presentation therefore produces an effect of discovery: religion’s conceptual difficulties are established first; money’s analogous paradoxes are disclosed only afterward.

Sections II and III successively convert this negative analogy into a positive account. Simmel supplies money as *Vermögen*, a generalized capacity and “option to have options,” while Marx converts money’s contradiction into the dynamics of capital. The transition to Marx is staged as a question—“But how can this enigma be resolved?”—and its answer replaces static paradox with an ongoing process of growth [F2]. The article then poses a second question, “how can capital grow?”, which shifts from the theory of money to entrepreneurs, labour, innovation, and economic myths. This creates a nested sequence: Benjamin’s thesis leads to money; money leads to capital; capital’s growth imperative leads to the myth cycle.

The article’s most consequential internal reversal occurs in its handling of Marx. Marx’s labour theory initially “does not appear to be very helpful,” but after labour has been redescribed as creative rather than merely measurable, the text declares that “Marx’s theory can somewhat unexpectedly be rehabilitated” [F3]. This turn also revises Schumpeter: the heroic entrepreneur becomes dependent on workers and wider coalitions. The resulting account does not assign innovation to one causal protagonist. Entrepreneurial agency organizes a collective process, but the text explicitly maintains that consumption waves and other innovations “can never be ‘made’ by any single actor” [F9].

Narrated time operates on several scales. The exposition retrospectively moves from nineteenth-century philosophy and the disciplinary separation of economics and sociology to “present-day capitalism.” Within its central model, however, time is cyclical: myths pass through “generation-diffusion-institutionalization and decline” [F4]. Institutionalization first establishes calculability, but then exhausts the myth’s innovative force and “must lead” to crisis [F5]. Presentation order thus encourages a causal reading—stabilization precedes declining profitability and renewed search—but the article offers this as a theoretical model, not as a fully narrated history of any one innovation.

The governing voice is an authorial, first-person academic “we” that guides readers through questions, syntheses, and returns: “Here we come to our second author” and later “we can come back to Benjamin” [F6]. That voice has access primarily to theories and models, not to directly narrated consciousness. Other perspectives enter through attribution: “According to Benjamin,” “As Simmel explains,” and Schumpeter’s description of entrepreneurial motivation. These are represented theoretical voices rather than independent testimonies.

Access to lived experience is limited and socially uneven. The sharpest focal shift occurs when the exposition moves from money as generalized freedom for the wealthy to money as discipline for the poor. The clause “the poor experience it as a disciplining medium” explicitly attributes experience to a class position [F7]. Yet this access remains mediated through Reddy and the narrator’s synthesis; workers or poor people do not speak directly. Similarly, the entrepreneur’s “dream to found his private empire” is quoted from Schumpeter, so it represents Schumpeter’s account of motivation rather than unmediated entrepreneurial testimony [F8].

Register helps mark these turns. Much of the article uses definitional and disciplinary language, but interrogative transitions—“But how can this enigma be resolved?”—create forward pressure [F2]. Religious metaphors such as “god of commodities,” “prophets and priests,” “faith,” and “salvation” then translate economic abstractions into the semantic field established by Benjamin. In the conclusion, the register becomes more prophetic and cautionary: “weaning off the drug of absolute wealth” recasts the analytical comparison as a possible future disillusionment [F10]. This wording intensifies the ending without converting its forecast into established fact.

## The turn and the ending

Section IV explicitly returns to the opening thinker, so the article achieves structural closure at the level of argument: Benjamin’s initially enigmatic fragment is now said to organize a synthesis of money, entrepreneurship, innovation, and myth [F6]. It also settles the central distinction between traditional and capitalist myths: the former generally claim timeless validity, whereas the latter repeatedly rise and decline [F4].

It does not, however, close the historical future. The question “Can the cycle of myths go on infinitely?” opens the final movement, and the conditional phrase “if the capitalist process of creative destruction were to grind to a halt” makes the ensuing crisis prospective rather than accomplished [F10]. The strongest counter-pressure comes from the article’s own claim that the decline of one myth becomes the starting point of another [F5]; this recurring mechanism supports continuation even as the conclusion imagines paralysis. What is settled is the text’s model of capitalism as a secularized promise reproduced through unstable myths. The exact remaining question is: **Can the cycle of myths go on infinitely?**

Because only one work is supplied, this is a local inventory; any corpus-level relation between this article and another work would require a second source and explicit chronological alignment.

### Counter-evidence

The conclusion’s prospect of the myth cycle grinding to a halt is countered by the earlier recurring mechanism in which “The decline of the old myth thus becomes the starting point of a new one” [F5]. The article therefore supplies reasons both for continued renewal and for possible paralysis. Its ending intensifies the latter possibility but does not empirically settle between them.

The apparent access to class and entrepreneurial experience is also qualified by mediation. The poor person’s experience is reported through Reddy and the article’s narrator [F7], while the entrepreneur’s “dream” is Schumpeter’s formulation [F8]. Neither passage supplies direct participant testimony.

### Open questions

- Can the cycle of myths go on infinitely?
- Does accelerated myth production renew capitalist growth, or does it paralyse the social cooperation required for innovation?
- How far can the proposed cycle explain particular historical cases, given that the article mainly presents a general model?
- How would workers, poor people, or entrepreneurs describe these processes in their own voices?
