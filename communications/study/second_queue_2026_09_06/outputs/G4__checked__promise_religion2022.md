## Money’s social and quasi-religious role

The most stable position concerns money. In 2001, money is not merely an exchange medium. Its object is the heterogeneous range of material, social, temporal, and spatial possibilities brought together in one form; its scope is capitalist money as *Vermögen* or generalized capacity; and its inferential role is to establish why ordinary economic or sociological accounts are insufficient. The article therefore calls money a multidimensional “absolute means.” [F1] Its abstract states the stronger comparative proposition directly: money is “a non-observable and basically paradox phenomenon, comparable to religion.” [F2]

The 2001 text also describes its relation to an earlier position outside the supplied pair: it explicitly claims to “take up and elaborate” Benjamin’s idea through Simmel, Marx, and constructivist economic myths. [F3] This is the collection’s clearest declared relation to an earlier work. By contrast, the 2022 text does not offer an equivalent self-description relative to the supplied 2001 article.

The later chapter retains the claim that money cannot be confined to an economic subsystem, but it reargues that claim through a different route. Capitalism is defined as “a social system based on unbounded markets,” not merely an economic order. [F6] Markets penetrate society spatially, socially, materially, and temporally; money consequently represents more than economic scarcity. It becomes a cipher for social unity and takes over contingency-management functions formerly attributed to God. [F10]

The paired formulations establish continuity because they concern the same object and perform the same inferential work. In 2001, “just like god, money is not simply a ‘medium’, but a ‘cipher’”; in 2022, “money become[s] a cipher for the unity of society.” [F12] The change is not simply one of vocabulary. The earlier support runs from the general reach and paradoxical status of money to its affinity with religion. The later support runs from the intransparency of world society and unbounded markets to the need for ciphers or self-descriptions of social unity. The conclusion persists, but the path to it changes.

The strongest counter-passage to any claim of simple continuity is the later chapter’s announced comparative problem: capitalism and religion are not merely parallel ciphers but different answers to society’s intransparency. [F7] The later argument thus gives the comparison a more explicit epistemic object than the earlier account. Even so, it does not disown the earlier monetary theory.

## Growth, labour, and private property

The second major continuity is the growth imperative. The 2001 account says that growth cannot be reduced to a culturally contingent profit motive: it is “embodied in the form of money itself.” [F13] Its supporting route passes through Marx’s M–C–M′, the dependence of capital upon labour, and a qualification of the labour theory of value. Labour creates surplus value only when employed in a creatively destructive way. Because the worker’s creativity and the entrepreneur’s organization both matter, the article proposes a combination of Marx and Schumpeter. [F4]

In 2022, growth remains tied to money’s access to creative labour, but the formulation foregrounds private-property rights and the unbounding of markets. The later text locates the dynamic in “the immense expansion of the possibility space of private property rights,” which brings creative labour’s immeasurable possibilities into the money nexus. [F13] Growth follows because competition continually presses toward the realization of those possibilities through innovation.

This is a change in support, not a reversal. The earlier text treats money’s form as the location of the imperative and explains its operation through capital, labour, entrepreneurship, and recurring myths. The later text relocates the immediate explanatory emphasis to the enlarged domain of property rights made possible by unbounded markets. [F13] The two accounts remain aligned because both connect monetary command over creative labour to open-ended capitalist growth.

The strongest qualification is that the later text does not reproduce the earlier detailed myth cycle. The 2001 sequence of myth generation, diffusion, institutionalization, and decline is replaced by a more condensed reference to ever-new imaginations and innovation. Because the later chapter neither names that cycle nor judges it inadequate, the collection supports changed emphasis, not an explicit abandonment of the earlier theory.

## The limits and scope of the religion comparison

Both texts prevent “capitalism as religion” from becoming a claim of simple identity. In 2001, capitalist myths differ from religious myths in temporal force: religious myths “usually claim timeless validity,” whereas capitalist myths undergo growth and decline and “can never establish themselves permanently.” [F5] The qualification is structurally important. The instability of capitalist myths explains why innovation must continue and why capitalism’s transcendence is a historical process rather than a fixed other world.

The 2022 chapter preserves the non-identity but states it in terms of substantive reach. Capitalism cannot answer questions about the beginning or end of the world or the finitude of human life. [F11] Its religiously comparable functions concern earthly contingency, social unity, performance, and promise—not the full subject matter of religious faith. The later concession narrows the proposition without undoing it.

The paired passages therefore show qualified continuity. [F14] The later limit is not a retreat from an earlier assertion that capitalism and religion are identical, because the earlier text had already distinguished their temporal structures. The strongest counter-passage to a claim of unchanged scope is instead the later text’s explicit choice of religious class.

In 2001, the difficulty of defining religion generally leads to a negative *tertium comparationis*: religion and money are approached through non-observability, paradox, and their determination of the conditions under which they are observed. In 2022, the chapter expressly says, “I am not speaking of all religions,” and concentrates on Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. [F9], [F15] This is an operative scope contraction from a general negative comparison to historically specified Abrahamic traditions.

That contraction also changes what can be compared. A negative account organized around paradox permits a broad analogy between money and religion. The Abrahamic focus permits a more concrete comparison among transcendence, God’s observer position, social universality, and money’s later representation of world society. [F7], [F15] The supplied texts do not establish that the later author regards the earlier approach as mistaken; they establish only that the later argument adopts a narrower comparator for a different explanatory purpose.

The largest development lies here. The 2001 text seeks to show how capitalism’s religious character illuminates entrepreneurship and innovation. Economic myths coordinate workers, entrepreneurs, financiers, experts, and consumers; they make innovation communicable without possessing a final rational foundation. Their rise and decline explain the alternation between uncertainty, temporary stabilization, declining profit, and renewed creative destruction. The Marx–Schumpeter synthesis supplies the article’s explicit theoretical support. [F4]

The 2022 chapter instead makes society’s “intransparency to itself” the primary common problem. [F7] Luhmann supplies the proposition that world society cannot observe itself from outside. Hayek supplies a parallel proposition about market knowledge: the relevant totality is unavailable to any participant. Market liberalism consequently becomes a self-description that permits communication about society without transparently depicting society as a whole. Money’s quasi-religious status follows from its representation of that inaccessible unity. [F10]

This later route extends the comparison at the epistemic level but does not provide the earlier institutional account of particular innovation cycles. The later text describes its contribution as proceeding through “three steps,” beginning with clarification of capitalism. [F8] That is a self-description of its current argumentative organization, not a statement that the 2001 myth-cycle account has been corrected or superseded.

What is settled is that the support changes from a developed sociology of innovation to a theory of unbounded markets and social self-description. The exact remaining question is whether the later chapter treats that shift as supplementation, replacement, or an independent route to a continuing position. The chapter does not say.

## From disillusionment to restraint

The two conclusions point in the same anti-utopian direction. The 2001 text imagines that a failure of creative destruction could put “the weaning off the drug of absolute wealth” on the agenda and extend Enlightenment criticism from traditional religion to money. [F16] Its concluding force is prospective: a future social crisis may produce religious disillusionment with the capitalist promise.

The 2022 text rejects another narrative promising final freedom or reconciliation. It calls instead for “sobriety and realism” in understanding and containing the consequences of unbounded markets. [F17] It also recommends restraint by sociology in constructing comprehensive self-descriptions. The later emphasis is therefore less on an anticipated collective break with money and more on practical containment, intellectual modesty, and the management of inherited consequences. [F18]

The strongest continuity lies in the rejection of renewed promises. The strongest difference lies in prescription: monetary disillusionment and critique in 2001; realism, containment, and disciplinary restraint in 2022. [F18] Because the later chapter does not refer to the earlier ending, this should not be described as an admitted retreat or correction.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the 2001–2022 publication sequence, the position shows **substantial continuity accompanied by a major reorganization of support**. Both texts treat capitalism as more than an economic subsystem, money as more than an exchange medium, and growth as an imperative tied to monetary command over creative possibilities. Both assign capitalism functions comparable to religion while preserving categorical limits to that comparison.

The principal development is from an account organized around absolute wealth, creative labour, entrepreneurship, and cyclical economic myths to one organized around unbounded markets, world society’s intransparency, and ideological self-description. The later chapter also narrows the religious comparator to Abrahamic traditions and specifies more directly which ultimate questions capitalism cannot answer.

What is settled is the continuity of the core money-and-growth position, the persistence of a qualified rather than identificatory religion analogy, and the change in explanatory architecture. What remains open is the relation claimed by the later text itself: because it neither names the 2001 work nor characterizes the changes retrospectively, the collection cannot determine whether the later route is meant as a supplement, replacement, or independent restatement.
