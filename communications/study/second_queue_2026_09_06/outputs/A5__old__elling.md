## The reading

Elling’s article reconstructs habit as the constitutive contradiction of Hegelian social freedom. Habit is not merely capable of producing either freedom or unfreedom under different conditions. Rather, freedom requires habitual embodiment, yet habituation can naturalize the norms whose authority must remain available to reflective appropriation. The contradiction is therefore foundational: removing habit would remove the practical stability of freedom, while complete identification with habit would erase freedom’s self-conscious character.

The article organizes existing interpretations into a strong critical pole, according to which socially realized freedom necessarily produces unfreedom, and a reflective pole, according to which habit enables flexible, rational agency. Elling’s “weak critical reading” mediates these positions by distinguishing actuality from risk: habit does not necessitate unfreedom, but necessarily creates its possibility. This synthesis preserves the reflective account’s claim that habit constitutes freedom and the critical account’s insistence that habituation is structurally dangerous. It rejects, respectively, the reflective tendency to treat pathological habits as incidental and the strong critical equation of actualized freedom with enslavement.

The decisive dialectical move is to translate the freedom/unfreedom antinomy into a life/death process. Death is construed not simply as cessation but as the negation through which more complex life can emerge. This permits Elling to divide “societal death” into natural and spiritual forms. Natural death belongs to the historical aging of an entire form of life and is necessary once its principle has been exhausted. Spiritual death belongs to an individual’s relation to norms and remains contingent, although always possible. These are not isolated tensions: historical stagnation weakens the living authority of social norms, thereby intensifying the individual temptation to retreat either into sovereign moral subjectivity or into mechanical submission.

Modernity brings this network of contradictions to its sharpest point. Its institutions claim to actualize universal freedom, but their complexity makes their rationality opaque to individuals. Subjects consequently absolutize their own normative authorship, depriving inherited norms of binding force. Because subjective freedom nevertheless requires objective embodiment, this assertion reverses into longing for external authority. The result is a parabasis between two mutually sustaining abstractions: the subject as sole author of norms and the subject as wholly subjected to them. Each pole generates the other because neither can integrate subjective authorization with objective normative stability.

The proposed reconciliation is philosophical self-reflexivity: subjects must understand themselves as both authors and subjects of social norms. This does not abolish the opposition. Indeed, Elling ultimately treats the irreducibility of subjectivity to social freedom as something that must be preserved. His synthesis is therefore closer to an unstable mediation than a final Hegelian closure. Habit must support freedom without becoming unquestionable; reflection must renew norms without dissolving their authority. The “solution” institutionalizes vigilance rather than resolving the antinomy once and for all.

That outcome generates a further tension. The article locates the problem mainly in moral psychology and education, even though its own analysis shows that institutional opacity and historical stagnation help produce the pathology. Philosophical reflection may clarify the contradiction, but the article does not establish that reflection can restore vitality to institutions whose historical development has stalled. Its weak critical position thus succeeds as a conceptual distinction between necessary threat and necessary unfreedom, but leaves open whether the distinction remains practically stable under modern conditions. Dialectical progress occurs in the article’s increasingly differentiated return to the same antinomy—first freedom/unfreedom, then life/death, then natural/spiritual death, and finally authorship/subjection. This is spiral development, but it ends by preserving rather than overcoming the contradiction.

### Counter-evidence

- The claim that modernity tends toward stagnation is qualified by the author’s refusal to endorse Hegel’s historical finality straightforwardly. — anchor: "We may not be quite as optimistic as Hegel was about the finality of the world spirit's development in 19th-century Prussia." — confidence: high

- Although the article emphasizes moral psychology, it acknowledges that institutions can partly mediate the conflict between subjectivity and normative stability. — anchor: "This can only partially be achieved by social institutions themselves." — confidence: high

- The account resists treating habit as intrinsically pathological by retaining its capacity to produce straightforward gains in freedom. — anchor: "habits have at least the potential of realizing straightforward gains in freedom. They just can't do so alone." — confidence: high

### Open questions

- The article invokes philosophy as the higher mediation but expressly leaves its operation unexplored; it therefore does not show how philosophical insight becomes an effective social habit. — anchor: "Thus, although we cannot explore this issue here" — confidence: high

- It remains unclear whether a society can continuously renew its norms without making them too unstable to provide habitual grounding. The text states both requirements but does not specify their workable balance. — anchor: "social life must be continually in progress to remain alive." — confidence: medium

- If opacity itself is socially produced, it remains uncertain whether educating individuals can remedy the contradiction without structural institutional change. — anchor: "Modern social habits structure life in ways that are not transparent to the individual subject." — confidence: medium

- The article leaves unresolved how subjects can diagnose their own spiritual death when the decisive pathology may consist in an absence of awareness. — anchor: "This absence is definitionally unavailable to subjects themselves, and hardly visible to the observer until it becomes socially widespread." — confidence: high
