# Reading

## How the text earns its contribution

The article builds a middle-range, dynamic theory of capitalist development rather than a general theory of religion. Its starting problem is that Benjamin’s claim that capitalism is “essentially religious” cannot be tested against an agreed genus of religion. The text therefore replaces a positive definition with a **negative comparison**: religion and money are alike insofar as both resist ordinary definition, organize their own observation, combine symbolic and real properties, and depend ultimately on faith or trust [F1] [F2].

From that comparison, the text constructs a theory in three linked stages. First, it combines Simmel’s money as *Vermögen* or “absolute means” with Marx’s capital as money driven to produce more money. This yields the concept of money as a promise of **absolute wealth**—command over material, social, temporal, spatial, real, and imaginary possibilities [F4] [F5] [F6]. Second, Marx’s dynamics of accumulation turn money’s contradiction—qualitatively infinite power embodied in quantitatively finite sums—into a growth imperative [F7]. Third, Schumpeterian entrepreneurship and constructivist accounts of paradigms explain how this promise is provisionally translated into innovations: entrepreneurs mobilize workers and supporting coalitions through technological, organizational, and consumption “myths” [F8] [F9] [F13].

The most distinctive mechanism is consequently not merely “capitalism resembles religion.” It is a cycle in which economic myths select possibilities, mobilize cooperation, diffuse, become institutionalized, lose their innovative force, and decline, provoking a search for alternatives [F14] [F15]. This mechanism connects the religious analogy to research on innovation, entrepreneurship, institutions, and growth. The text demonstrates that analytical connection conceptually and with historical or contemporary illustrations, but it does not empirically test the cycle as a general causal model [F18] [F19].

## The crucial synthesis

The synthesis is selective rather than foundational. Simmel and Marx are compatible at the level used here because both treat money as generalized social power rather than merely a neutral exchange medium [F4] [F6]. Marx and Schumpeter are made compatible only after reciprocal revision: Marx’s quantitative labour theory is rejected, while his claim about labour’s distinctive creativity is retained; Schumpeter’s innovation dynamics are retained, while his heroic individualism is replaced by a social and communicative model [F10, rejected by the check] [F11].

Civilizational theory contributes the relation between transcendental horizons and institutions. The text transfers this relation from conventional religious cosmologies to money’s this-worldly promise, making private property an institution constructed with reference to “absolute wealth” [F12]. Constructivist institutionalism then supplies the category of myth, but the article revises it dynamically: paradigms are not merely legitimating facades; they coordinate innovation across organizational and expert boundaries before declining [F13] [F14].

## Claimed novelty and demonstrated work

The claimed contribution is an interpretation of capitalism **as**, not merely as influenced by, religion that can synthesize otherwise separated research streams [F16]. Its substantive addition is the chain:

**money as absolute means → promise of absolute wealth → capital’s growth imperative → entrepreneurial interpretation → mobilization through myths → institutionalization and decline → renewed creative destruction.**

This is more than a relabeling because “religion” supplies definite analytical relations: an absolute but hidden horizon, interpretive intermediaries, faith-dependent symbolisms, and practices that render an indeterminate promise actionable [F5] [F16]. The innovation also supplements static institutional analysis by making the rise, consolidation, and decline of institutions the object of explanation [F18].

The demonstrated work is primarily conceptual. The article reconstructs how Simmel, Marx, Schumpeter, civilizational theory, and constructivist accounts can be combined; it applies the resulting vocabulary to technological paradigms, management fashions, consumption waves, and financialization [F13] [F19] [F22]. It does not demonstrate field-wide originality or provide a comparative empirical test showing that the religious interpretation outperforms rival dynamic theories.

The strongest evidential warrant is therefore **conceptual reconstruction plus illustrative fit**, not measurement. The text itself defines the observer’s task as reconstructing how actors create and transform structures [F18]. That methodological position is compatible with its theory, but it leaves unresolved whether social resonance, labour creativity, and the monetary growth imperative can be empirically distinguished as separate causes.

## Local assessment

This is a local inventory of one article. It settles how this text constructs its theory: through a negative analogy between money and religion, selective synthesis of Simmel and Marx, reciprocal revision of Marx and Schumpeter, and a constructivist cycle of economic myths. A corpus-level judgment about originality, influence, or superiority would require comparison with another work.

The strongest counter-passage is the article’s own acknowledgment that a thorough theory of institutions would exceed its scope [F21]. This qualification matters because institutions are the bridge between the promise of absolute wealth and concrete entrepreneurial coordination. Thus the construction of the myth-cycle mechanism is clear, but the exact remaining question is: **under what observable conditions does an economic paradigm’s social resonance produce profitable innovation rather than failed mobilization, speculative diffusion, or institutional lock-in?**

### Counter-evidence

The article does not supply a direct empirical test of the myth cycle or operational criteria for identifying when a paradigm’s resonance causes profitable innovation. Its explicit scope limitation on institutional theory [F21] weakens the bridge between the meta-institutional promise of money and concrete organizations. Its contemporary diagnosis is also modal—“may be interpreted”—rather than conclusive [F22].

A further internal tension is that the theory makes social resonance decisive for myths [F13], yet profitability eventually disciplines them [F15]. The text does not fully specify whether failed myths fail because of inadequate communication, material infeasibility, insufficient demand, financial constraints, or the interaction of these conditions.

### Open questions

1. What observable evidence would distinguish an economic myth from an ordinary forecast, strategy, ideology, or convention?
2. Under what conditions does social resonance generate productive innovation rather than speculative imitation?
3. How can the claim that only creative labour produces surplus value be tested against capital-intensive and automated production?
4. What determines the timing and scale of generation, institutionalization, and decline across incremental, sectoral, and economy-wide innovations?
5. Does financialization interrupt the myth cycle, accelerate it, or relocate it to financial products and valuation practices?
