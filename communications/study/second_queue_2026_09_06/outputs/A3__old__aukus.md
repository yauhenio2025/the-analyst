## The reading, then:

The article’s central comparative move is a redescription: AUKUS is **not adequately understood as only a security alliance**, but as a “mutation of neoliberalism” that reorganises relations among states, markets, military institutions, firms, and finance. This is more than a contrast in terminology. The authors argue that mainstream geopolitical and realist accounts privilege interstate rivalry as the relevant level of analysis, whereas the militarised-neoliberalism lens maps AUKUS onto processes of state transformation and capitalist accumulation. The mapping transfers structural relations—coordination, institutional layering, capital allocation, and coalition formation—rather than merely surface similarities. It thereby generates an explanation for policies and beneficiaries that deterrence-centred accounts allegedly leave unexplained.

The article repeatedly presents this as a corrective opposition between visible geopolitical events and underlying political-economic relations. That distinction is analytically productive: submarines, alliances, and rivalry are treated as visible manifestations, while regulatory controls, industrial policy, and capital flows supply deeper mechanisms. Yet the language sometimes approaches a depth metaphor in which geopolitics is merely appearance and political economy is reality. The authors partially avoid a false dichotomy by insisting that AUKUS remains a security partnership and occupies a conjuncture joining both domains. Their strongest formulation is therefore not geopolitical **versus** economic explanation, but the reciprocal “securitisation of economic policy and economisation of strategic policy.”

“Mutation” provides the governing historical analogy. Militarised neoliberalism retains market creation, competition, accumulation, and privileged private interests from neoliberalism, while altering the institutional means through interventionist industrial policy, security alliances, export controls, and state finance. This is structurally stronger than simply declaring a return of the state. However, the biological connotations of mutation—inheritance, identifiable lineage, and discontinuous change—are not specified or tested against rival classifications such as post-neoliberalism, economic nationalism, developmentalism, or state capitalism.

The old/new Washington Consensus contrast supplies temporal endpoints, but these are ideal types rather than a rigorously measured continuum. The old pole is anchored in liberalisation, deregulation, and open markets; the new pole in industrial policy, trade controls, and an economic-security fusion. The authors complicate any simple reversal by arguing that intervention does not destroy markets but actively constructs them for selected firms and sectors. Accordingly, the article’s scale runs from relatively market-led governance to increasingly security-layered market coordination, with “gradual” transformation indicating movement rather than categorical replacement. No explicit metric establishes how much layering amounts to a distinct mutation.

The article’s three-part typology—bipartisan regulatory control, combined capitalist development, and incorporation of venture capital and start-ups—organises the empirical analysis effectively. These types are not mutually exclusive: subsidies, defence procurement, export-control reform, and venture finance can instantiate all three simultaneously. Nor are they demonstrated to be exhaustive. Labour, universities, local communities, anti-AUKUS movements, environmental interests, and non-allied firms appear only marginally or not at all. Thus the typology works best as a mechanism-oriented selection of salient processes, not as a complete taxonomy of AUKUS.

The Cold War comparison is one of the article’s most disciplined analogies because it explicitly states both resemblance and difference. Past and present military-economic formations share state-supported procurement, industrial expansion, and the advancement of favoured business groups. What is not transferred wholesale is the institutional setting: the current formation includes universities, venture capital, dual-use technology, and state developmentalism in the Global North. Nevertheless, the analogy remains suggestive rather than demonstrative because the article does not compare procurement shares, industrial effects, or institutional powers systematically across periods.

Finally, the cases of Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, and South Korea perform representative work. They show recurring combinations of bipartisan legitimation, state finance, regulatory reform, and corporate benefit, supporting the proposed mechanism through cross-national repetition. But case selection is strategic rather than representative of the wider Indo-Pacific or global order. The evidence is especially rich for beneficiaries and enabling policies, while losers, failed investments, implementation gaps, and counterfactual cases receive less attention. Consequently, the article convincingly establishes that AUKUS has political-economic dimensions irreducible to deterrence, but establishes less securely that “militarised neoliberalism” is uniquely superior to adjacent explanatory frameworks.

### Counter-evidence

- Bipartisanship is not complete consensus: Australian Labor contained organised internal opposition. — anchor: "Fifty of 800 grassroots Labor branches and left-aligned unions passed motions opposing AUKUS and nuclear-powered submarine acquisition."

- US agreement is likewise qualified by congressional resistance over submarine capacity and funding. — anchor: "Republican members of the US Senate Armed Services Committee moved to block legislation enabling the sale of Virginia-class submarines to Australia."

- Material delivery problems weaken any inference from regulatory alignment to successful industrial transformation. — anchor: "severe delays emerging out of the devastating state of the UK submarine-building industry"

- The article concedes that AUKUS may leave Australia without the sovereign capability invoked to justify it. — anchor: "leaving Australia with no sovereign submarine capability"

- The authors delimit their claim by declining to evaluate AUKUS’s effect on regional conflict, so MN does not provide a complete strategic assessment. — anchor: "it is beyond the scope of this paper to assess whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific region towards conflict."

### Open questions

- What observable threshold distinguishes a “mutation of neoliberalism” from economic nationalism, developmental statism, state capitalism, or ordinary military Keynesianism?

- How would the framework classify policies that enhance security but do not create private market opportunities, or industrial policies unrelated to China and AUKUS?

- Are the three salient features jointly exhaustive, and where do labour, universities, environmental groups, local communities, and anti-AUKUS coalitions fit?

- What systematic evidence identifies “losers,” rather than only beneficiaries, and how are distributive effects compared against employment, technological, or strategic gains?

- Would comparison with non-AUKUS states pursuing similar industrial policies show that the proposed mechanisms are specific to AUKUS or characteristic of a wider global shift?

- How representative are the selected grants, firms, and programmes? Alternative cases involving failed projects, excluded firms, or unsuccessful start-ups could change the evaluation.

- Can the Cold War analogy be tested through comparable measures of procurement, state finance, industrial concentration, technological spillovers, and institutional control?

- Does “geopolitical fetishism” understate the independent causal force of military threats, or can the framework specify when geopolitical rivalry is a cause rather than legitimating rhetoric?
