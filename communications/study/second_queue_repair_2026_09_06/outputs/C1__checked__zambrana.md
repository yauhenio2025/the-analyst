# Local usage inventory: speculative thinking, actuality, value, fetishism, and mystification

This is a **local inventory of one article**. It establishes the relations among terms within Zambrana’s argument; comparison with another work or a disciplinary corpus would require an additional source. Because the article alternately reports and revises Rose, Hegel, Marx, Lukács, Ramas San Miguel, and Smallwood, differences of voice must not be mistaken for changes in definition.

## Speculative exposition and the severe style

The article’s governing methodological contrast is between **speculative** and **representational** thinking. Speculative thinking “re-cogniz[es] an object along with its misrecognition” by accounts that separate the object from its coming-to-be [F2]. Its task is therefore not to attach a historical origin to an already completed object. It presents the conditions and processes through which the object acquires determinacy.

The severe style names the corresponding discipline of exposition. Because it is concerned with the object’s strictures rather than didactic accommodation, its “severity” is methodological rather than simply tonal [F1]. The article connects the form of presentation to the character of capitalist modernity: an object marked by brokenness and misrecognition requires an exposition that does not smooth those divisions into an immediately accessible identity.

This is why **lack of identity** is not a defect speculative thought should eliminate. The term names the difference between actuality’s existence and the limited content supplied by a particular real necessity [F4]. Speculation presents that nonidentity while correcting the representational separation between an object and its development.

## Actuality, necessity, and manifestation

**Actuality** is the hinge between the methodological and historical arguments. The article defines action through its unfolding in material conditions “beyond the purported intention of the agent” [F3]. The causal burden therefore does not fall on intentions alone, nor on an abstraction detached from its conditions. What an action or structure is becomes determinate through what it produces in spatial, temporal, and intersubjective relations.

Likewise, **necessity** does not erase contingency. What cannot be otherwise is conditioned by a totality of contingent circumstances that contain possibilities within them [F5]. This lets the article describe capital as historically determinate without making its emergence a predetermined end. Retrospective intelligibility is not the same as teleological inevitability.

The proposition **“Essence must appear”** governs the treatment of manifestation [F6]. The article does not simply identify essence with any immediate appearance. Rather, it rejects an account in which essence is a hidden reality and appearance merely a deceptive surface. Capital’s appearances can distort or invert social relations while remaining constitutive manifestations with real effects [F6].

## Fetishism, reification, and mystification

The sharpest neighboring-term boundary lies among **reification**, **fetishism**, and **mystification**. Reification is a socially necessary objective appearance, but explicitly “not the only one” [F7]. Fetishism is distinguished by the reciprocal structure of personified things and thingified people [F8]. The article therefore resists using “reification” as a universal shorthand for every operation of value.

**Mystification** has a different emphasis. It names the forms irrationality takes as value organizes and produces a social totality beyond the sphere of production [F9]. It is not merely a mistake in consciousness. Nor does criticism end by revealing labor behind an apparently autonomous financial form. The analysis must also ask what such forms organize and produce.

The strongest counter-pressure comes from the Lukács passage in which objectivity conceals “every trace” of its underlying relation between people [F14]. The article does not deny this concealment or the social mediation of commodities. It rejects the implication that the resulting objectivity is merely phantom-like: “The sociality of things and the instrumentality of disposable people is not phantom-like,” but expresses capital’s inverted reality [F14]. The disagreement concerns the status of appearance, not whether capitalist forms can conceal their social relations.

Interest-bearing capital concentrates this issue. The passage attributing to money or a commodity the power “to valorize its own value independent of reproduction” immediately calls this “capital mystification in the most flagrant form” [F15]. Its evaluative language—“irrational form,” “misrepresentation,” and “objectiﬁcation” in the “highest power”—marks the formulation as critical rather than celebratory [F17]. Apparent independence is a form requiring explanation, not the article’s unqualified causal ontology.

## Value as subject and the material mechanism

The text nevertheless deliberately calls value the **“dominant subject”** of capital [F10]. Read alone, this could seem to turn an abstraction into an unexplained agent. In context, “subject” names an organizing relation whose manifestations and mechanisms must be traced.

The trade-plantation circuit supplies one such mechanism. Its closure is money’s return in valorized form through the sale of sugar, tobacco, rice, or indigo “made by the alienation of slave labor power by coercion” [F16]. Thus the causal account does not substitute “value” for concrete agents and relations. It links monetary return to coerced labor, plantation commodities, sale, and circulation [F16].

The article’s use of **self-valorization** consequently has two levels that must remain connected. At the formal level, value is the dominant subject of capital’s process [F10]. At the material-historical level, value’s return is accomplished through circuits involving commodities and coerced labor [F16]. The first level identifies the organizing form; the second prevents that form from becoming a literally self-causing abstraction.

## Capital’s temporal boundary: coming-to-be, not mere prehistory

The article’s most consequential historical boundary is the exclusion of the slave trade from capital’s **mere prehistory** [F11]. “Coming-to-be” does not mean a preliminary episode that ends once industrial capitalism begins. It means that the trade-plantation complex belongs to the historical constitution of the relations and circuits being analyzed.

The relation between free and coerced labor is genealogical but not identical. The article says that free alienation in a labor market is “first rehearsed” in the alienation of coerced labor in a slave market [F12]. The predicate “coerced” prevents the comparison from erasing the distinctive violence of enslavement. What the comparison establishes is a relation between market forms and capital’s genesis, not equivalence between workers’ and enslaved people’s legal or social positions.

The circuit passage makes this historical claim materially specific: monetary valorization closes through plantation commodities produced by slave labor power under coercion [F16]. Slavery is therefore not added as contextual background to a logic already fully explained through wage labor. It enters the account of how capital’s circulation and valorization became actual.

## What is settled and what remains open

Within this article, the principal uses are stable:

- speculative thinking comprehends an object with its coming-to-be and misrecognition [F2];
- actuality names conditioned unfolding beyond purported intention [F3];
- lack of identity remains the basis of speculative exposition [F4];
- fetishism centers personification and thingification [F8];
- mystification names capital’s irrational but effective forms beyond production [F9];
- value is the dominant subject of capital’s process, but that abstraction is grounded in material circuits [F10], [F16];
- the trade-plantation complex belongs to capital’s coming-to-be rather than its mere prehistory [F11].

The exact remaining question is taxonomic: **does the article treat every fetish form as a species of mystification, or are fetishism and mystification partially overlapping forms, distinguished respectively by thingification/personification and by irrational autonomy or organizing effectivity?** The article establishes the distinction but does not settle that relation [F8], [F9].

### Counter-evidence

- The strongest counter-passage to the article’s constitutive account of appearance is Lukács’s formulation of “an autonomy that seems so strictly rational and all-embracing as to conceal every trace of its fundamental nature: the relation between people” [F14]. The article answers that “The sociality of things and the instrumentality of disposable people is not phantom-like, but the expression of the reality of capital as an inverted (verkehrt) world” [F14].

### Open questions

- Does every fetish form count as a species of mystification, or do fetishism and mystification partially overlap while retaining different criteria?
- How far does the article’s “rehearsal” relation between coerced and free labor establish continuity without obscuring their legal and social differences?
- A corpus relation or standard-usage comparison cannot be settled without another work.
