# Reading

## Position map

The collection contains two dated texts by Christoph Deutschmann. *The Promise of Absolute Wealth: Capitalism AS A Religion?* appeared in 2001. It argues that capitalism is “essentially religious,” because money promises command over “the totality of human possibilities” and can redeem that promise only through continuous growth. Its distinctive route combines Simmel’s “absolute means,” Marx’s capital relation, Schumpeterian innovation, and economic myths. Its major qualification is that capitalist myths differ from traditional religious myths: they are temporary constructions that rise, institutionalize, and decline rather than claiming timeless validity [F1].

*The Interpretation of Capitalism as Religion – Justification, Epistemic Possibilities, Critique* appeared in 2022. It defines capitalism more institutionally and historically as “a social system based on unbounded markets,” then compares religion and capitalism as responses to society’s inability to observe itself as a whole. Its distinctive route combines the history of market liberalism with Luhmann’s self-description problem, Hayek’s dispersed-knowledge argument, and Economic Theology. Its central qualification is twofold: the religious comparison is restricted mainly to Abrahamic world religions, and capitalism can realize worldly “miracles” without answering religion’s fundamental questions [F2] [F3].

The consequential result is a mismatch between substantive continuity and declared relationship. The 2022 text retains major propositions found in 2001—money exceeds the role of exchange medium, takes over functions associated with God, and drives an unending dynamic through creative labour and property rights—but it never names or cites the 2001 article and does not describe these moves as a revision or continuation of it. The relation across the supplied works is therefore an externally demonstrable continuity with substantial reframing, not a relationship expressly claimed by the later text.

## What remains stable: money exceeds an economic medium

The strongest continuity concerns the object doing the religious work. In 2001, money is “a construct with real as well as virtual properties,” bridging the real and imaginary; as *Vermögen*, it provides not merely purchasing power but an option over possible options. In 2022, the same anti-reductionist point appears within the account of unbounded markets: money has “emancipated itself from the harmless role of an economic medium of exchange” and becomes a cipher for social unity [F10].

This is more than shared vocabulary. In both texts the proposition has the same object—money—and the same argumentative role: it defeats accounts that restrict money to an economic subsystem or scarcity-regulating medium. Yet the explanatory setting changes. The 2001 text develops the proposition through the paradox that money both symbolizes and possesses value, combining material, social, temporal, spatial, real, and imaginary powers. The 2022 text places it inside a theory of world society: because no observer can represent society as a whole, money and the market-liberal narrative operate as ciphers or self-descriptions.

The later formulation therefore does not abandon the earlier religious analogy; it gives it a more determinate epistemic function. In 2001, money and God are comparable because each is self-referentially sustained by faith and because each joins the finite to the infinite. In 2022, money more specifically assumes the function of representing social unity and coping with contingency. That is a clarification in content, although the later text does not present it as a clarification of the 2001 work.

The strongest counter-passage limits the analogy. The 2022 text says capitalism is “far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith,” even while conceding that it can realize concrete earthly needs “to a good extent” [F3]. The religious predicate is therefore not unrestricted: capitalism competes with religion in worldly performance, universality, representation, and contingency management, but not in answering questions of cosmic origin, ending, or human finitude.

## What changes: from the myth cycle to unbounded markets and social intransparency

The most consequential shift is not a repudiation of “capitalism as religion,” but a replacement of the argument’s organizing mechanism.

In 2001, the analysis moves from money’s paradox to capital’s growth imperative and then to the economic myths through which uncertain innovation is socially organized. Technological, organizational, and consumption paradigms mobilize heterogeneous actors; their social resonance, not intrinsic efficiency, explains their success. The cycle of generation, diffusion, institutionalization, and decline accounts for alternating phases of uncertainty, profit, stabilization, and falling profitability [F7].

In 2022, the primary object is “a social system based on unbounded markets.” Spatial, social, material, and temporal market expansion makes markets the core of society rather than one embedded subsystem [F11]. The religious comparison is then justified epistemically. World society and unbounded markets can be observed only from participant perspectives, while market liberalism operates as a “self-description” that communicates about society without truthfully representing it as a whole [F12].

This is an evidence and support change. The 2001 text’s support is predominantly a synthesis of theories of money, labour, entrepreneurship, and innovation. The 2022 text’s support is historical and epistemological: Polanyi supplies the contrast between embedded and unbounded markets; Luhmann supplies the problem of an unavailable total observer and ideological self-description; Hayek supplies the parallel claim that dispersed market information is unavailable in its entirety to anyone.

The new framework also changes what “religion” explains. In 2001, the analogy chiefly explains the promise of absolute wealth and the succession of myths that mediate innovation. In 2022, it chiefly explains how money and the liberal narrative represent an otherwise unrepresentable social whole. The latter does not show that the former mechanism has been rejected. Because the later text neither discusses the 2001 article nor explicitly withdraws its cycle theory, the defensible conclusion is displacement from the center of the argument, not disappearance from the position.

## Growth, creativity, and property: continuity with a changed causal emphasis

Both works connect growth to the inclusion of creative labour within the money nexus. The 2001 article says labour, unlike machines and other material resources, can produce genuinely new products and thereby transform money into capital. It corrects Marx by denying that the mere temporal expenditure of average labour guarantees profitability, while retaining the proposition that only creatively deployed labour can produce surplus value.

The 2022 essay retains creativity and permanent innovation but relocates the principal cause. It rejects instrumental reason as the primary driver and argues instead that “the immense expansion of the possibility space of private property rights” temporalizes the earthly Kingdom of God [F5]. Its final continuity claim is directed to Marx rather than to Deutschmann’s 2001 formulation: after describing private property’s reversal from a freedom-guaranteeing institution into collective compulsion, it declares, “Here, nothing needs to be added to Marx’s insights even today” [F13].

Accordingly, the later argument is not simply stronger wording for the same support. The 2001 chain is:

> money’s infinite promise → labour’s creativity → capital growth → socially mediated economic myths.

The 2022 chain is:

> market unbounding → expanded property claims over creative labour → permanent innovation and growth → money as social cipher and contingency formula.

The creative-labour commitment persists, but private property and market unbounding receive greater causal prominence. This is best described as reorganization and support escalation, not a declared correction.

An important earlier qualification must remain visible. The 2001 text does not say that all holders of money equally enjoy its freedom: the qualities of money as *Vermögen* develop only where money exceeds subsistence needs, while the poor encounter debt and constrained choice [F14]. The 2022 text approaches inequality differently. It argues that capitalist universalism rests on “the civilizational minimum of respecting the respective property rights,” which enables broad inclusion while leaving room for egoism and structural power inequalities [F14]. Thus neither text offers a simple emancipatory account of property or monetary freedom.

## A change in force: paralysis versus internal unstoppability

The clearest tension concerns whether capitalist dynamism can fail from within. In 2001, “Present-day market radicalism tends to paralyse the working of the myth cycle.” The claim is local and diagnostic: actors exposed to excessive complexity may withdraw into finance, knowledge industries, or entrepreneurship rhetoric, potentially bringing creative destruction to a halt [F8].

In 2022, “From within itself, the dynamic of these promises cannot be stopped,” with only the hedged addition that perhaps not even ecological crisis can stop it from outside [F8]. This is broader and stronger. Its object is no longer the institutional myth cycle but capitalism’s systemic promise-dynamic grounded in unbounded property rights.

The passages need not be treated as a direct contradiction. A mechanism that produces real innovation may be paralysed while the wider compulsion to seek growth continues through financialization or other forms. But neither text explicitly supplies that reconciliation. The settled point is that the 2022 wording raises the force from a contingent mechanism vulnerable to paralysis to an internally unstoppable systemic dynamic. The remaining question is whether the later text means that capitalist compulsion persists even when productive creative destruction stalls, or whether it has silently abandoned the earlier possibility of internal breakdown.

## The altered critical conclusion

The two endings share an anti-redemptive direction but differ in remedy and confidence. The 2001 article imagines that a breakdown could place on the agenda “the weaning off the drug of absolute wealth” and describes true religious disillusion as taking leave of collective obsession [F9]. This remains prospective—“what might then be on the agenda”—and depends on the conditional possibility that creative destruction grinds to a halt.

The 2022 conclusion rejects another universal narrative that promises final freedom and happiness. It instead calls for “sobriety and realism” in identifying and containing the unplanned effects of unbounded markets, while assigning sociology the limited role of providing a relatively impartial place to discuss liberalism’s blind spots and contradictions [F9] [F15].

This is not a retreat from critique. The later text lists climate effects, financial crisis, digital disruption, and democratic erosion as accumulating blind spots of the liberal narrative. Nor does it deny the market-liberal model’s historical efficacy: it concedes that the model would rank ahead of the republican model while immediately pairing that success with a “hardly surveyable” list of blind spots [F16]. What changes is the form of the critical horizon. The 2001 ending leaves open a collective disillusion from absolute wealth; the 2022 ending explicitly distrusts any new narrative of ultimate reconciliation and narrows the constructive response to containment, realism, and institutional discussion.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the two texts, the position is substantially continuous but reorganized. The 2022 essay retains the 2001 article’s central anti-reductionist commitments: capitalism is more than an economic subsystem; money is more than an exchange medium; capitalist universality and growth assume functions comparable to religion; creative labour and imagined possibilities propel an endless dynamic; and that dynamic does not culminate in salvation or reconciliation.

The later text nevertheless changes the architecture of the case. “Absolute wealth” and the economic myth cycle cease to organize the whole argument. “Unbounded markets,” private-property rights, social intransparency, and ideological self-description take their place. The force also changes locally: possible paralysis of the myth cycle becomes an internally unstoppable promise-dynamic, while the prospective disillusion of 2001 becomes the sober containment program of 2022.

What is settled is therefore substantive continuity with altered emphasis, causal organization, and critical prescription. What remains unsettled is the relationship the later text itself claims to the earlier one: it claims none. Any account of revision must remain an intertextual comparison by the reader, not a declared self-revision by the 2022 text.

### Counter-evidence

- The 2001 claim that market radicalism “tends to paralyse the working of the myth cycle” qualifies any account of capitalist growth as automatically self-renewing [F8].
- The 2022 statement that capitalism is “far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” prevents the God–money comparison from becoming an identity claim [F3].
- The 2022 text’s explicit continuity claim concerns Marx—“nothing needs to be added”—not the supplied 2001 article [F13].
- No anchored passage in the 2022 text names, cites, corrects, or affirms the 2001 article.

### Open questions

- Does the internally unstoppable “dynamic of these promises” persist through financialization even when the productive myth cycle is paralysed?
- Does “imaginations” replace the 2001 category of economic myths, or merely leave its cycle theory unstated?
- Would the 2022 emphasis on unbounded property rights preserve the 2001 synthesis of Marx and Schumpeter if that synthesis were addressed directly?
- Is the move from prospective “weaning off” absolute wealth to practical containment a substantive revision or only a change in concluding emphasis? The later text does not say.
