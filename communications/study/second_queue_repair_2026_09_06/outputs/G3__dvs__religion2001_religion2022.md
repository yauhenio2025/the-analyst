# Reading: From capitalist dynamics to social self-description

## Position map

The 2001 article, **“Capitalism as a Religion? An Unorthodox Analysis of Entrepreneurship,”** argues that capital and the capital form of money possess “intrinsically religious qualities” and uses that claim to build a dynamic sociology of entrepreneurship, innovation, growth, and social change [F1]. Its distinctive move is to combine Simmel’s account of money as generalized capacity, Marx’s account of capital as self-expanding money, and constructivist accounts of economic myths and visions [F3] [F6]. Its important qualification is that economic myths differ from religious myths: rather than claiming timeless validity, they pass through “rise–diffusion–institutionalization and decline” [F7].

The 2022 chapter, **“The Interpretation of Capitalism as Religion – Justification, Epistemic Possibilities, Critique,”** argues that capitalism should be understood as a social system based on spatially, socially, materially, and temporally unbounded markets, not merely as an economic subsystem [F2]. It relocates the religion comparison from the organization of entrepreneurial innovation to the problem of how world society represents its own inaccessible unity. Its distinctive move is to combine Luhmann’s concept of self-description with Hayek’s dispersed-knowledge argument and the market-liberal narrative [F10] [F11]. Its important qualification is twofold: the analysis of religion is concentrated on Abrahamic world religions [F9], and capitalism cannot answer religion’s ultimate questions about the world’s beginning and end or human finitude [F17].

The collection therefore shows continuity in the claim that money exceeds the role of exchange medium, but a major change in explanatory scale and purpose. The 2001 text asks how money’s religious quality drives and coordinates innovation; the 2022 text asks how unbounded markets and money represent social unity and manage contingency. Publication dates establish that the articles appeared in 2001 and 2022, but the later chapter does not cite the earlier article. It does cite Deutschmann’s 2019 book alongside Simmel, so transmission from the 2001 formulation through that intermediary remains possible but unverified [F20].

## Source use → receiving use

The receiving argument gains an account of capitalism as a world-encompassing system and an epistemology of social self-description. It loses, as its focal use of the concept, the 2001 article’s detailed mechanism connecting entrepreneurial mobilization, economic myths, institutionalization, and crisis. This is adaptation rather than demonstrated correction or misrepresentation: the later text gives “capitalism as religion” a different analytical job while retaining money’s expansion from means into socially commanding power.

## The 2001 local inventory: religion as a mechanism of capitalist dynamics

The 2001 article’s first decisive change is explicit. It declines the established line treating religion as a cultural background influencing capitalism and instead proposes that “capital and the capital form of money” themselves have intrinsically religious qualities [F1]. Religion therefore ceases to be primarily an external ethical cause and becomes a description of capital’s own structure. The gain is analytical proximity to money and growth; the risk is that “religion” could become too broad unless the paper supplies a mechanism.

Simmel and Marx provide that mechanism in two stages. Through Simmel, money becomes more than exchange: *Vermögen* enlarges freedom across material, social, temporal, and spatial domains [F4]. Money has intrinsic value because it does not merely purchase a fixed collection of goods; it preserves generalized possibilities. Through Marx, this static “option to have options” becomes processual. Money becomes capital only when it returns with an increment, so not every circulation or expenditure counts as capital [F5]. The qualification matters: the article does not simply call all money religious. Its central object is money in the capital form, where generalized potential is subjected to expansion.

The argument then gives the religious analogy a practical purpose. The growth imperative has “the power of a religious promise,” because what Protestant asceticism had treated as a salvation signal becomes the capitalist message itself [F15]. This is not yet a claim that capitalism reproduces a religion in every respect. It identifies a mutation in the function of success: accumulation no longer points beyond itself toward salvation but becomes its own validating message.

The paper’s most consequential recombination follows. It joins Marx’s account of capital and labour with Schumpeter’s account of entrepreneurial innovation, explicitly seeking to retain their strengths while rejecting their respective limitations [F6]. The entrepreneur is no longer a solitary hero: success requires mobilizing workers, inventors, financiers, salespeople, customers, and political supporters. Technological and organizational paradigms supply symbolic direction, bounded rationality, and motivation. Their force depends on social resonance rather than an independently demonstrated rational foundation.

Here “myth” is not merely a false proposition. It is a coordinating construction that selects technological possibilities and creates cooperation under uncertainty [F6]. Its economic character also introduces a limit to the religious comparison. Economic myths ordinarily do not claim timeless validity; they rise, diffuse, institutionalize, and decline [F7]. Once institutionalized, a paradigm loses its innovative aura and can produce lock-in, declining profitability, crisis, and a search for alternatives. “Capitalism as religion” thus becomes the systematic basis for studying how capitalism creates and destroys structures rather than how it administers stationary ones.

What the 2001 use gains is a meso-level account linking money, labour, entrepreneurship, symbolism, and innovation. What it does not independently establish is the fidelity of its accounts of Simmel, Marx, Weber, Schumpeter, Benjamin, Dosi, Kuhn, or Beckert to those authors’ complete positions. Those originals were not supplied. Its explicit citations establish declared uptake, not adoption of an entire system.

## The 2022 local inventory: religion as a comparison for social representation

The 2022 chapter initially distinguishes theological from secular money critique. Theological money critique treats action governed by monetary interests as idol worship, but the chapter expressly says that this is not the form it will discuss. Its adopted route is secular: Marx shows how human-made commodities and money acquire a power resembling traditional idols and conflicting with enlightened reason [F8]. The retained bridge is idolatry; the object shifts from worship of a false god to domination by society’s own economic artifacts.

The chapter next reconstructs capitalism. Markets alone are not capitalism. Capitalism names a social system in which markets become spatially, socially, materially, and temporally unbounded and move to the center of society [F2]. This broadens the object beyond capital accumulation or the economic subsystem. Labour’s creative capacities, financial markets, innovation, and growth remain present, but they now appear as moments in a more encompassing history of market penetration.

That enlarged object generates an epistemological problem. World society cannot be observed as a whole from outside itself. The chapter aligns Luhmann’s intransparency thesis with Hayek’s account of markets as synthesizing dispersed information unavailable in its entirety to any person [F11]. The alignment is carefully limited: the text says Hayek had not taken note of Luhmann, so it does not claim a transmission path between them. Their compatibility is functional—the two arguments identify limits on total social knowledge—not proof of a shared theoretical system.

Luhmann’s “self-description” then gives the religion comparison its new job. Market liberalism makes it possible to communicate about society as a unity, but it cannot render that unity fully observable or scientifically true [F10]. Like a religious cipher, it both designates and conceals its object [F12]. Religion is therefore no longer chiefly an analogy for entrepreneurial faith and innovation cycles. It becomes the comparative case for how society constructs an unavailable observer and represents its inaccessible totality.

The chapter restricts that comparison to the Abrahamic world religions [F9]. Those religions construct a transcendent creator-observer through whom believers can comprehend the world and themselves. After the Enlightenment dethrones that observer position, the underlying problem of intransparency does not disappear. The chapter argues that money assumes part of the vacated role: “money as the universal medium of unbounded markets” represents the unity of society or the world, while functions of earthly contingency management pass from God to money [F13].

Simmel’s “absolute means” is consequently retained but repurposed. In 2001, it established money’s intrinsic value as generalized power. In 2022, money’s capacity to serve an indeterminate range of purposes explains why it becomes an absolute purpose and imperative for actors [F14] [F20]. The later argument does not revoke the earlier account; it embeds the means–end reversal in the institutional setting of unbounded markets.

The gain is a macro-sociological comparison of two forms of universalism. Religious universality rests on shared belief in a transcendent counterpart. Capitalist universality rests on a boundless network of private contracts, minimal reciprocity, and property rights. This moral minimalism allows potentially universal market inclusion, but also leaves wide room for inequality and disruption. Money coordinates transactions “behind the backs” of actors and supplies a secular representation of social unity.

The chapter nevertheless preserves a boundary that prevents complete equivalence between capitalism and religion. Capitalism can outperform religion in meeting many worldly needs, but it cannot answer ultimate questions concerning the beginning and end of the world or human finitude [F17]. Nor does it provide reconciliation: it satisfies wishes while continually producing new ones. The result is comparison and functional succession, not identity in every respect.

## Continuity, mutation, and independent routes

### 1. The displacement thesis becomes more specific

Both works say that monetary forms assume functions formerly associated with religion. In 2001, capital and the capital form of money broadly “take the place of religion in modern societies” [F1]. In 2022, the displacement is analytically disaggregated: money represents social unity and assumes functions of earthly contingency management [F13]. The later formulation gains precision about which religious functions transfer. It also narrows the claim by preserving religion’s distinctive relation to ultimate questions [F17].

The strongest counter-passage to a simple replacement thesis is therefore the 2022 statement that capitalism is “far from being able to give an answer” to questions of cosmic beginning, end, and human finitude [F17]. What is settled is that both texts assign money religious or formerly religious functions. The remaining question is whether the 2022 functional specification revises the 2001 general displacement thesis through direct development, through the unsupplied 2019 intermediary, or by an independent return to shared sources.

### 2. The means–end reversal persists, but its explanatory setting changes

Both works explicitly use Simmel’s “absolute means.” The 2001 article makes it the basis for intrinsic monetary value: money embodies generalized options beyond any particular purchase [F4]. The 2022 chapter places the same reversal under conditions of unbounded markets: because money is indispensable for indeterminable purposes, it becomes an absolute purpose and imperative [F14].

This is continuity plus changed purpose, not a wholly new 2022 thesis. The later work gains a historical and institutional condition—market unbounding—while the earlier work supplies the richer account of money’s material, social, temporal, and spatial capacities. The strongest counter-consideration is that the 2022 citation names Simmel and Deutschmann 2019, not the 2001 article [F20]. Thus the conceptual alignment is secure, but the specific transmission route is not.

### 3. Marx moves from capital’s dynamic to secular idolatry

The 2001 Marxian use centers on the definition of capital as money returning with surplus value [F5]. This lets the article explain why generalized monetary potential becomes an imperative of endless growth. The 2022 use centers on the power that human-made commodities and money acquire over society [F8]. It thereby inserts Marx into a history of Enlightenment critique and the reversal of emancipation into domination.

The retained core is the autonomy-like power of economic forms created by humans. The changed purpose is substantial: one use explains accumulation and innovation; the other explains secular idolatry and social heteronomy [F19]. Neither use need misrepresent Marx merely because it selects a different element. Fidelity cannot be decided without the supplied Marx originals.

### 4. Weber’s sign-to-substance formula is retained, not invented later

The 2001 text already says that what ascetic Protestantism treated as a salvation signal becomes the capitalist “message itself” [F15]. The 2022 chapter states that financial success transforms from salvation’s signal into its substance and declares this transferable to non-Christian religions [F16]. The basic semantic mutation is therefore continuous across the collection. The later innovation is comparative extension, not the original sign-to-substance move [F21].

The strongest counter-passage to unrestricted extension is the chapter’s failure to specify or develop which non-Christian religions are meant. What is settled is the announced transfer. The exact remaining question is how the transfer would preserve or alter the historical and doctrinal conditions of Weber’s Protestant case.

### 5. “Myth” changes scale and object

In 2001, myths and visions coordinate heterogeneous actors around innovation. Their life cycle explains alternating stabilization and crisis [F6] [F7]. In 2022, the analogous symbolic problem appears at the level of society’s self-description: the liberal narrative allows communication about social unity while concealing that unity’s inaccessibility [F10] [F12].

The continuity lies in symbolic constructions enabling coordinated orientation without transparent foundations. The mutation lies in object and scale: technological development in one text, world society in the other [F22]. The strongest counterpoint is that the later chapter does not explicitly call market liberalism an “economic myth” in the 2001 cyclical sense. The two concepts should therefore not be collapsed. What remains open is whether self-description replaces the earlier myth concept, generalizes it, or belongs to a separate argumentative route.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the collection, “capitalism as religion” changes from a mechanism-centered concept into a representation-centered one. In 2001 it integrates Simmel, Marx, Schumpeter, and constructivist theories to explain how money’s generalized possibilities become growth imperatives and how entrepreneurs mobilize innovation through economic myths. In 2022 it integrates Marxian idolatry critique, Luhmannian self-description, Hayekian dispersed knowledge, and economic theology to explain how unbounded markets and money represent world society and manage contingency.

The later text gains epistemological scope, a theory of capitalist universalism, and a more exact account of which functions pass from God to money. It loses the earlier article’s detailed account of entrepreneurial organization and cyclical economic myths. Several semantic cores persist: money exceeds exchange; means become ends; financial success becomes salvation’s substance; and human-made economic forms acquire commanding power. Yet the corpus establishes adaptation and continuity, not direct document-to-document borrowing. The exact unresolved issue is whether the 2022 configuration developed through the 2001 article, through Deutschmann’s unsupplied 2019 book, or through renewed recombination of the shared cited traditions.
