# Local construction inventory

## How the article earns its contribution

The article earns its proposed contribution by converting Benjamin’s suggestive description of capitalism *as* religion into a dynamic account of capitalist development. Its claimed reach is broad: the interpretation “could be capable of synthesizing” research on innovation, entrepreneurship, and growth [F1]. Its demonstrated work is more bounded. It constructs a conceptual sequence connecting money’s promise of unlimited possibility to capital’s growth imperative, entrepreneurially organized creative labour, socially resonant innovation myths, institutional stabilization, declining innovative force, and renewed crisis.

The construction does not require a universal definition of religion. The article instead rejects an abstract, suprahistorical standard and adopts a negative basis of comparison [F2]. Money and religion are comparable not because they belong to a demonstrated common genus, but because each is treated as a self-referential enigma: the symbol is identified with what it symbolizes, and its efficacy ultimately depends on generalized trust or faith [F5]. This establishes a structural analogy, not an identity between capitalism and traditional religion.

The central borrowing from Simmel is money as *Vermögen*: a combined key to material and imaginary wealth, individual freedom, and control over time and space [F3]. The article names the horizon represented by these options “absolute wealth,” meaning command over the totality of human possibilities [F4]. Marx then supplies the dynamic step. The contradiction between money’s qualitatively unlimited possibilities and its quantitatively determinate form is not intellectually solved; it is practically reproduced through capital’s never-ending struggle for growth [F6].

The theory next revises both Marx and Schumpeter. It rejects the proposition that average labour time by itself secures profitability and requires labour to be employed in a creatively destructive way [F8]. It also socializes Schumpeter’s entrepreneur: innovation depends on mobilizing employees’ creative capacities rather than on an isolated heroic actor [F9]. The text explicitly proposes a Marx–Schumpeter combination [F10]. Its compatibility claim is therefore selective: Marx contributes labour’s creative capacity, while Schumpeter contributes entrepreneurial innovation under uncertainty. The complete foundations of neither theory are adopted.

Constructivist paradigm research supplies the mechanism that connects uncertain creative activity to coordinated social action. Paradigms guide innovation cognitively and motivationally, generating collective interest in joint problem solving [F11]. The article then recasts them as myths whose success depends on social resonance rather than intrinsic efficiency [F12]. These myths follow a cycle of generation, diffusion, institutionalization, and decline [F13]. Institutionalization initially makes coordinated, comparatively calculable action possible, but eventually removes a paradigm’s innovative aura and its capacity to induce further growth [F14].

The religious comparison is retained but qualified at the end of the construction. Traditional religious myths generally claim timeless validity and preserve a fixed mundane/transcendent distinction. Capitalist myths cannot establish themselves permanently; capitalism dynamizes that distinction through repeated construction, transcendence, and reconstruction of limits [F15]. Thus “religion” does analytical work here only if the analogy preserves the difference between permanent sacred representation and the cyclical instability of capitalist myths.

## How the synthesis works

The synthesis proceeds through compatible transfer rather than wholesale adoption.

First, Simmel supplies the content of money’s promise. Money is not merely an exchange medium; it combines otherwise heterogeneous capacities in a single form [F3]. Marx supplies the process through which that promise is pursued: money becomes capital through an imperative of growth [F6]. The resulting relation is complementary, but only after Marx’s quantitative account of labour is rejected [F8].

Second, Marx and Schumpeter are combined by reciprocal correction. Marx’s account retains labour’s distinctive creativity but loses the claim that temporal expenditure is sufficient for profitability [F8]. Schumpeter’s account retains entrepreneurial innovation but loses its individualistic form because creative destruction requires employees’ capacities [F9]. The article explicitly presents this as a combination designed to avoid both approaches’ shortcomings [F10].

Third, civilizational theory is extended rather than merely cited. Its concept of cosmological beliefs is no longer restricted to conventionally religious world views; money understood as capital or *Vermögen* becomes a candidate for the “holy cosmos” of modern society [F16]. This move provides an account of how institutions can be oriented toward the horizon of absolute wealth without equating money with a traditional god.

Finally, paradigm and myth research supplies mediation. Paradigms reduce indeterminate monetary possibilities into selected projects, guide participants cognitively and motivationally [F11], and succeed through social resonance [F12]. Their institutionalization creates temporary stability but also erodes their innovative force [F14]. This is the mechanism by which the article links a religiously structured promise to concrete processes of innovation and capitalist change.

## Claimed novelty and demonstrated work

The article’s explicit contribution claim is that capitalism *as* religion may synthesize research on entrepreneurship, innovation, and economic growth [F1]. A single article cannot establish field-wide originality or demonstrate that it has successfully synthesized those entire literatures. What it does demonstrate is a coherent conceptual construction:

1. money and religion are compared through paradox and faith rather than a universal definition [F2] [F5];
2. Simmel’s *Vermögen* is renamed at its utopian horizon as absolute wealth [F3] [F4];
3. Marx converts that promise into a dynamic growth imperative [F6];
4. Marx and Schumpeter are selectively revised and combined [F8] [F9] [F10];
5. paradigms become socially resonant myths that coordinate innovation [F11] [F12]; and
6. institutionalization produces a recurrent cycle of stabilization and declining innovative force [F13] [F14].

This contribution is middle-range rather than a general theory of religion or a complete theory of capitalism. Its analytical economy depends on distinctions the article itself preserves: religion versus capitalism-as-religion, money versus capital, absolute promise versus particular myth, entrepreneur versus creative labour, and diffusion versus institutionalization and decline.

## What is settled and what remains

Within the article, it is settled that the capitalism–religion comparison is constructed negatively through paradox and faith [F2] [F5]; that money’s promise is formulated as absolute wealth [F4]; that Marx supplies a growth dynamic [F6]; that Marx and Schumpeter are selectively combined [F10]; and that innovation paradigms are recast as socially resonant myths developing cyclically [F12] [F13].

No direct counter-passage rejects this construction. The strongest counter-pressure comes from the article’s own revisions and tentative language: labour time is explicitly rejected as sufficient [F8], myth success is attributed to resonance without comparative discrimination [F12], and the contemporary diagnosis is only something that “may be interpreted” as a symptom [F19].

The exact remaining question is: **under what observable conditions do creative labour and social resonance produce aggregate surplus and continued growth, rather than failed innovation, declining profitability, or permanent stalling of the myth cycle?**
