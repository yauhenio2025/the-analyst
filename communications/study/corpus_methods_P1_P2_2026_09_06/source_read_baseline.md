## AUKUS and subsea

AUKUS's object is a trilateral pact, particularly Pillar II, with Australia/US critical-mineral policy, US/UK venture-capital initiatives and Japan/Korea extensions. It is not one homogeneous national case. The text's core claim is a mutation of neoliberalism through state transformation and coalitional politics. Its concrete material includes Australia's designation as a US Defense Production Act domestic source, export-licensing changes, agencies' financing roles and venture capital as intermediary. Announced funding, an industrial opportunity and a completed result must remain distinct. The text explicitly brackets whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific toward conflict and discusses Pillar I delays; a comparison cannot treat it as measured strategic success. The account also names electoral wedging and geopolitical motivation, so neither motives nor effects reduce to a single economic mechanism.

Subsea explicitly presents two cases containing distinct actors: the US/China contest over SeaMeWe-6 and France's ASN nationalization. The consortium shifts from HMN Tech to SubCom after subsidy/incentive and sanction pressures. China Telecom and China Mobile withdraw, China Unicom remains; Chinese exclusion is not total. France acquires 80% of ASN through APE with the stated intention to buy the remaining share later; its supply/manufacturing position is stronger than its cable-financing position. The source treats French dirigisme as a qualified industrial strategy, constrained by finance, EU fiscal rules and US hyperscaler/sanction dependencies. Network duplication coexists with selective decoupling and expansion. A simple two-bloc partition or universal state mastery contradicts the account. The useful shared question concerns how state instruments reshape private decisions/markets, with sector, unit, aim/result and coverage limits retained.

## Castoriadis

Technique treats technique as a dimension of a society's institution, not a separable instrument or a simple cause. Ends, means, efficiency and value cannot be separated in the proposed way. Its critique of capitalist technical choices centers workplace conflict and the attempt to eliminate the producers' human role. A socialist alternative entails workers' management and a transformed technical ensemble. The final discussion explicitly concedes that economies of scale are not always fictitious and does not endorse a simple return to self-sufficient small communities. This concession matters to any common template of anti-technical or anti-economic thought.

What Democracy? distinguishes effective collective autonomy and self-limitation from representative liberal oligarchy, while crediting existing rights/open contestation as historically significant. Its positive economic sketch includes a genuine market, consumer sovereignty, producer self-management and explicit political decisions about production-cost conventions. It is not a refusal of all markets, administration or political institutions. The question at the end concerns desire/capacity to revive autonomy amid consumerist privatization; it cannot be read as an already implemented empirical solution.

The Rationality of Capitalism questions a society's purportedly rational ends and its economic theory, then separately explains relative efficiency, flexibility and resistance. Quantitative comparisons over time have constrained meaning, but the text expressly preserves broad claims such as output falling or consumption increasing. A critique of exact index/forecast calculations is not the denial of every quantitative observation. Its productivity critique and account of real resilience must both survive reconciliation. These three sources can be compared as approaches to institution, rationality and autonomy; the supplied dates do not establish stages in an intellectual development.

## Deutschmann

Both 2001 texts argue that money/capital has intrinsic religious qualities, rather than only being influenced by religious ethics. Money as Vermögen/absolute means opens an option to have options but takes a finite quantity; capitalist growth and economic myths mediate that paradox. Creative labor and entrepreneurial coordination are both necessary in the account: the entrepreneur depends on workers and institutional/social cooperation. Religious myths claim timeless validity; capitalist myths have growth and decline and dynamically reconstruct the mundane/transcendent boundary. The analogy is already qualified in 2001.

The Promise of Absolute Wealth additionally questions indefinite continuation of the myth cycle. Market radicalism and financialization can undermine the institutions entrepreneurship requires; the text's closing call for disillusion and leaving absolute wealth is already critical. A later caution cannot be presented as the first criticism of capitalist promises.

The 2022 text explicitly frames a comparative perspective: capitalism and religion answer society's intransparency to itself in different ways. Unbounded markets, world-society unity, contingency management, Luhmann/Hayek and competing market-liberal/republican freedoms organize its account. Its conclusion asks for practical containment and sociological restraint, beyond merely piecemeal reforms, rather than a new salvation narrative. It does not simply abandon the 2001 analogy; nor does the supplied set establish that all of its emphases are new. Reconciliation needs aligned propositions and different scope/qualification, not a chronology manufactured from dates or common words.
