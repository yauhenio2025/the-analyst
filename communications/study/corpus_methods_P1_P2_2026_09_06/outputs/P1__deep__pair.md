# Comparing state intervention in strategic networks and markets

## Comparison question and position map

The shared question is: **How do states reshape strategically important, market-organised infrastructures and capability sectors, through which institutional mechanisms, and with what reported limits or results?** This supports four common criteria: the strategic object being reshaped; the intervention instruments; the relationship between states and firms; and reported effectiveness. These criteria are justified because both documents reject purely state-centric explanations while examining renewed intervention in sectors substantially organised through markets and corporations. The comparison remains bounded: AUKUS is a defence and technology partnership, whereas the subsea cases concern a privately owned communications network, a cable project, and a supplier nationalisation.

AUKUS must therefore be kept at two analytical levels. Institutionally, it is a trilateral partnership whose Pillar I concerns Australian nuclear-powered submarines and whose Pillar II covers advanced capabilities including quantum technologies, cybersecurity, hypersonic missiles and artificial intelligence [F1]. Conceptually, the article presents it as militarised neoliberalism: “a mutation of neoliberalism emerging in the context of bipartisanship” [F2]. Japan and South Korea belong to the article’s wider empirical field, but not to the formal partnership [F3].

The subsea article likewise contains nested rather than interchangeable cases. SeaMeWe-6 is a transnational project and supplier-selection conflict; France’s acquisition of ASN is a national industrial intervention involving a strategically important firm. The source compares them because they illuminate different network-realignment strategies, not because a cable consortium and a nationalised supplier are equivalent units [F6–F8].

## Main similarities, differences and exception tests

### A common restructuring of market-organised strategic sectors

The strongest cross-document continuity is that neither text treats security competition and market organisation as separate domains. The AUKUS article describes its object as “the enmeshment of regulatory control, industrial and defence policy, and wider social relations that reshape the geoeconomic order.” The subsea article defines its approach as “Political interventions aimed at retroactively reshaping infrastructures and adjacent markets by influencing the decisions of private corporations.” In each account, intervention addresses a strategic sector already constituted through firms, markets and transnational relationships, rather than simply replacing markets with state command [F23].

This similarity is conceptual rather than institutional. AUKUS reconnects defence, economic and market institutions within a multinational partnership, while subsea-cable realignment is classified through industrial, regulatory and coercive strategies directed at corporate decisions. The common pattern is therefore **state-linked market restructuring**, not a single policy model [F24].

Both documents also resist a simple “return of the state” narrative. Under AUKUS, agencies take on roles as investor, contractor, regulator and financier [F10], but the intervention simultaneously benefits and incorporates firms, contractors and start-ups [F11]. In subsea cables, states cannot simply dictate network development because supplier markets, cable consortia and large technology or telecommunications firms retain consequential positions [F5], [F17]. In both, state activism operates through corporate structures that it seeks to redirect.

### Different institutional routes

AUKUS’s distinctive route is policy fusion. Bipartisanship is treated as a political foundation for regulatory controls that combine state- and market-led industrial policy [F9]. Its instruments range across alliance policy, industrial support, critical-minerals finance, defence procurement and research collaboration. The AUD 2 billion expansion in Australian critical-minerals financing is an announced financial instrument, not evidence that all intended industrial or security outcomes were achieved [F12]. Likewise, the Australia–Japan undersea-warfare project establishes collaboration and an intended capability direction, not a measured operational result [F20].

The subsea cases divide intervention more sharply by state position. China uses industrial support to improve a national supplier’s bid and then supports parallel infrastructure when the bid fails [F14], [F15]. The United States exploits regulatory and coercive leverage affecting consortium decisions and access to US-linked markets [F16]. France uses public ownership to preserve a supplier and manufacturing capacity [F18]. These are not successive stages of one strategy; they are distinct routes associated with different network and industrial positions.

France also supplies an important middle-power contrast. It does not possess US-equivalent coercive leverage or China-equivalent financial scale. Its response is to use an asset already embedded in the historical core of cable supply. France’s selective engagement with both US- and Chinese-linked projects shows that autonomous strategy need not mean equidistance or isolation, although the source reports growing limits imposed by US pressure and dependence on American technology firms [F19].

### Reported outcomes are not commensurable

The sources do not support a common numerical ranking of effectiveness. AUKUS evidence combines implemented financing measures, procurement-related market consequences, collaborative projects and unsettled future capabilities. The submarine programme remains subject to severe delays and possible US congressional obstruction [F13]. The AUKUS article therefore does not establish uniform realised capability gains across the three members.

The subsea case has a more immediate decision outcome: the SeaMeWe-6 consortium awarded the contract to SubCom [F16]. The source consequently says that the United States has “arguably been more successful thus far” in cable laying [F17]. Even this is sector- and time-qualified rather than a general verdict on US–China competition. China’s construction of parallel routes further shows that losing one supplier decision did not terminate its network strategy [F15].

Accordingly, the strongest supported effectiveness contrast is narrow: **US pressure achieved the reported SeaMeWe-6 contracting outcome, while AUKUS’s principal submarine capability remained uncertain** [F25]. This does not prove that regulatory coercion is generally more effective than alliance-led industrial policy. The objects, implementation horizons and outcome measures differ.

### Testing the common pattern against exceptions

The common pattern is that security competition encourages state intervention through markets and firms. The most important exception is that such intervention neither produces uniform domestic consensus nor grants governments complete control.

For AUKUS, fifty of 800 grassroots Labor branches and left-aligned unions passed motions opposing the partnership. Bipartisanship is therefore a dominant institutional settlement, not unanimous support [F26]. Japan and South Korea further qualify any uniform partner model. Japan began with arms-export rules that constrained the scale of its defence industry [F22], while South Korea’s export bank faced a legally specific financing constraint [F21]. A common label such as “Pillar II partner” would erase these different institutional baselines.

For subsea cables, France tests the expectation that network competition divides infrastructure neatly into US and Chinese spheres. Its selective cooperation with both sides demonstrates room for autonomous action [F19]. That exception is itself bounded: not every hub possesses a supplier comparable to ASN, and less territorialised infrastructures such as cloud or satellite services afford geographically central states less leverage [F27].

## Comparative judgment

The collection supports a defensible but conditional judgment: **contemporary security competition does not simply displace market organisation; it induces states to restructure strategic markets and infrastructures through regulation, finance, industrial policy, coercive leverage and selective public ownership** [F23], [F24]. AUKUS illustrates this as alliance-centred policy fusion under the concept of militarised neoliberalism. SeaMeWe-6 illustrates competing industrial and regulatory-coercive efforts to redirect corporate contracting. France demonstrates that a strategically positioned state can pursue a partly autonomous industrial route by acquiring a central supplier.

The evidence does not establish one universally superior strategy. Against a reader criterion of securing an immediate project decision, the US SeaMeWe-6 intervention appears more successful than China’s bid [F16], [F17]. Against a criterion of retaining domestic industrial capacity, France’s ASN acquisition is the clearest implemented measure [F18]. Against a criterion of delivering major sovereign military capability, AUKUS remains unresolved because submarine implementation is uncertain [F13], [F25]. These judgments are criterion-specific, not an overall ranking.

The most useful missing evidence would be a common set of longitudinal measures: realised rather than announced expenditure; contract and ownership changes; domestic value added; supplier market shares; delivered capabilities; network redundancy and resilience; and counterfactual evidence about what firms would have done without intervention. For AUKUS, equivalent jurisdiction-level evidence is needed across the three members and associated partners. For subsea cables, comparable post-intervention outcomes are needed for SeaMeWe-6, PEACE, EMA and ASN.

No established translation problem appears in the inspected material. Institutional vocabulary nevertheless requires care across national settings: bipartisanship, export control, public ownership and strategic autonomy do not designate identical arrangements. The findings cannot be generalised beyond AUKUS, the associated jurisdictions discussed by that article, and the selected subsea-cable cases.

### Counter-evidence

- Australian internal opposition qualifies claims of uniform bipartisan support: “Fifty of 800 grassroots Labor branches and left-aligned unions passed motions opposing AUKUS” [F26].
- China’s parallel PEACE and EMA projects qualify any claim that the SeaMeWe-6 decision removed it from cable-network development [F15].
- France’s selective engagement with both US and Chinese projects qualifies a binary bloc-alignment account [F19].

### Open questions

- Do AUKUS financing, procurement and R&D measures produce delivered capabilities, durable domestic value added and greater strategic resilience?
- How do SeaMeWe-6, PEACE and EMA affect supplier market shares, route redundancy, prices and security over time?
- Does ASN nationalisation improve French and European resilience or profitability after acquisition?
- What common outcome measures could compare defence capabilities with communications infrastructure without erasing differences in object and time horizon?
- How do domestic political opposition and differing regulatory traditions alter implementation across AUKUS members and associated partners?
