# Shared inquiry and position map

The collection asks how states reshape market-organized transnational systems when security competition intensifies. Both texts reject statecraft-only accounts, but examine different objects and mechanisms. Publication dates—*aukus* in 2025 and *subsea* in 2026—do not by themselves establish an intellectual sequence or direct inheritance.

- **aukus** examines AUKUS, especially Pillar II, across member states and selected engagement by Japan and South Korea. Its central claim is that AUKUS is both a security partnership and a “mutation of neoliberalism”: bipartisan state transformation reconnects security institutions, economic agencies and selected fractions of capital [F3]. Its distinctive role is a critical political-economy account of **militarised neoliberalism**, emphasizing beneficiaries, coalitions and capitalist accumulation rather than only policy effectiveness. Its principal qualification is that it does not assess whether AUKUS will cause Indo-Pacific conflict [F7].
- **subsea** examines the predominantly privately owned global subsea cable network through the SeaMeWe-6 dispute and France’s nationalization of ASN. Its central claim is that states pursue **private network realignment** by influencing corporate decisions through industrial, regulatory and coercive measures [F4]. Its distinctive role is to explain variation and uneven effectiveness using states’ and firms’ network positions. It qualifies any return-of-the-state thesis: realignment remains partial because entrenched market dynamics and corporate power constrain even great powers [F8].

The strongest continuity is therefore not that both texts make the same claim about the same institution, but that both reinterpret security-oriented intervention as a reorganization of markets rather than straightforward deglobalization. The principal divergence concerns analytical emphasis: *aukus* foregrounds state transformation and class-coalitional beneficiaries, whereas *subsea* foregrounds network position, corporate decision-making and variable policy effectiveness.

## Strongest substantive agreement

The clearest shared answer is that renewed security intervention reorganizes rather than simply supersedes market relations. *aukus* states that AUKUS “reconnects, rather than deconstructs, the global economy,” while *subsea* describes state action as attempts to “correct” market-driven infrastructure development [F9]. This is substantive agreement because both claims concern the relation between geopolitical intervention and inherited market structures, not merely shared vocabulary.

The agreement remains qualified. In *aukus*, the central explanatory object is a political formation: bipartisanship and policy fusion make selected defence manufacturers, civilian conglomerates, start-ups and financial capital beneficiaries of AUKUS. In *subsea*, the object is a physical and corporate network whose established topology limits state intervention. Thus, *aukus* explains **whose interests state transformation advances**, while *subsea* explains **which intervention strategies are available and why their effectiveness varies**.

Their firm-state accounts are complementary but not identical. *aukus* observes that “the state reconciles the interests of VC with its accumulation vehicles,” making firms participants in a state-supported accumulation project [F5]. *subsea*, by contrast, says that corporations “stand at the center of global infrastructure networks,” requiring states to manipulate corporate choices rather than assume obedience [F6]. The former emphasizes incorporation; the latter emphasizes positional leverage and constraint. Together they support a relational account in which states and firms are mutually conditioning actors, but they do not settle whether incorporation or corporate autonomy dominates across sectors [F10].

## Strongest qualification and unresolved disagreement

The strongest qualification concerns state capacity. *subsea* makes uneven effectiveness central: “their influence remains spotty and uneven” [F8]. SeaMeWe-6 shows successful US coercive-regulatory pressure against HMN Tech, but China responds with parallel cable projects, while France exploits its network and industrial position. No actor fully commands network development.

*aukus* likewise contains implementation limits, including submarine-building delays and the possibility that Australia could be left “with no sovereign submarine capability” [F8]. Yet its principal outcome is not a comparative measure of policy success. Its analysis can remain valid if AUKUS projects are delayed, because its claim concerns state transformation, resource allocation and beneficiary formation. Consequently, implementation failure would not directly contradict the militarised-neoliberalism thesis. The texts would conflict only if aligned evidence showed either that firms uniformly execute state priorities or that state interventions leave markets and coalitions unchanged; the supplied material shows neither.

A second unresolved issue is conceptual scale. *aukus* calls AUKUS a “mutation of neoliberalism,” whereas *subsea* takes an “intermediate position” between state- and capital-centric explanations [F3], [F6]. These are not contradictory propositions under matched conditions. The first classifies a broader political-economic formation; the second specifies an analytical stance and mechanism within privately owned infrastructure. The corpus does not establish whether private network realignment should be treated as one mechanism of militarised neoliberalism or as a distinct framework. Evidence of direct conceptual engagement or a common comparative case would be required.

## Collection-level judgment

Jointly, the collection licenses three conclusions. First, contemporary geoeconomic intervention works through inherited market structures and powerful firms rather than cleanly replacing them. Second, industrial policy, public finance, regulation and coercion can redirect strategic sectors, but their effects depend on institutional and network position. Third, security competition may generate selective reconnection and parallel capacity rather than uniform deglobalization.

The collection does **not** establish a single integrated theory. The exact residual question is whether the coalition-centered process described as militarised neoliberalism and the positional process described as private network realignment are nested mechanisms, competing explanations, or sector-specific complements. A decisive comparison would require passages applying both frameworks to the same infrastructure or policy project, with matched evidence on corporate preferences, state instruments, beneficiary distribution and implementation outcomes. The corpus also leaves the causal effect of these transformations on interstate conflict unanswered.

### Counter-evidence

- *aukus* records internal Australian opposition, US congressional obstruction and submarine-production delays. These qualify claims of seamless bipartisanship or assured implementation, without negating the documented allocation of finance and institutional reorganization.
- *subsea* records successful US pressure in SeaMeWe-6, countering any categorical claim that corporations or infrastructure inertia make state intervention ineffective. It also records Chinese parallel systems and French maneuver, countering an inference of comprehensive US control.
- Continued network expansion does not prove the absence of fragmentation: duplicated routes, exclusion of Chinese suppliers and sanctions may produce simultaneous expansion and geopolitical segmentation.

### Open questions

1. Is private network realignment a mechanism within militarised neoliberalism, or does its emphasis on corporate autonomy require a distinct framework?
2. Under matched sectoral conditions, when does state-firm coalition formation dominate, and when do corporate network positions constrain state objectives?
3. Who bears the costs and captures the gains from network redundancies, nationalization, subsidies and sanctions?
4. Do parallel strategic infrastructures improve resilience, deepen bloc segmentation, or do both simultaneously?
5. Neither source supplies a causal assessment of whether these political-economic transformations increase the probability of interstate conflict.
