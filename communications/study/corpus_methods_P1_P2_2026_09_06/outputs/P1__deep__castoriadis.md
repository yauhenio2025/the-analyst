# Comparative reading: technique, democracy, and capitalist rationality

## Comparison question and source-to-case map

The shared question is: **How do the three texts explain the relation among collective ends, institutional power, technical or economic means, and social conflict—and what follows for claims that modern institutions are neutral, rational, or democratic?** This question is justified because each text treats a social-historical form rather than a bounded national case, and each asks how apparently separable instruments or procedures acquire meaning within an instituted society [F1].

The cases are therefore **conceptual approaches**, with empirical examples nested inside them:

- **`castoriadis1984_technique`, “Technique” (1984 edition)** surveys technique from Greek *technē* through Marx and contemporary technology to possible post-revolutionary arrangements. Its distinctive claim is that technique is a constitutive and creative dimension of the human world, not simply a neutral instrument or autonomous cause [F2]. Social organisation is itself a nested technical-institutional object. Qualification: the historical and cultural examples do not constitute standardized empirical cases, and technical objects cannot be detached from their ensembles and local significations.
- **`castoriadis1990_what_democracy`, “What Democracy?” (1990)** defines democracy as collective autonomy, self-institution, and self-limitation. It contrasts this standard with contemporary Western “liberal oligarchies,” whose formal liberties coexist with privatized public power [F3]. Athens, the United States, France, England, Czechoslovakia, Romania, and rich and poor world regions are illustrative nested cases, not uniformly documented units. Qualification: Athens demonstrates possible democratic forms but is explicitly not a modern template, particularly because of scale.
- **`castoriadis1997_rationality_of_capitalism`, “The ‘Rationality’ of Capitalism” (composed September 1996–August 1997 and published in 1997)** treats capitalism as a historically specific social regime whose core imaginary is the unlimited extension of “rational mastery.” It distinguishes theoretical economic ideology, effective economic operation, and the sources of capitalism’s efficiency and resilience [F4]. Qualification: national examples are uneven, while product, cost, capital, and output are not stable comparative quantities across time or countries.

The texts’ publication or composition dates do not by themselves establish a progression. The 1990 text refers to the earlier “Technique,” and the 1997 text reargues several concerns found in both earlier pieces, but the supplied evidence supports **continuity and specification**, not a documented linear development or an order of composition within same-year materials.

The common criteria below are justified because all three texts address them: (1) the social form’s defining object; (2) how ends and means are related; (3) the institutional location of power; (4) the role of conflict or collective agency; and (5) the text’s diagnosis or proposed alternative. These are qualitative criteria. The corpus supplies no common quantitative standard and therefore licenses no ranking.

## Main similarities, differences, and exception tests

### A common rejection of separable, neutral instruments

The strongest continuity is the rejection of the idea that a means can be evaluated apart from the society that institutes its ends. “Technique” states that technique is “an element of the constitution of the world as a human world.” “What Democracy?” defines democracy through self-institution: political rules have democratic force only insofar as the collectivity can participate in establishing and revising them. “The ‘Rationality’ of Capitalism” adds that the capitalist regime itself decides what will count as a product and how it will be valued [F15].

The objects differ. The 1984 passage concerns technique’s role in world-making; the 1990 passage concerns the political source and limits of law; and the 1997 passage concerns economic valuation. Their inferential role is nevertheless parallel: each blocks the treatment of an instrument, procedure, or metric as socially self-explanatory. This is a **reargued common position**, not merely shared vocabulary.

The 1984 text develops the broadest ontological claim. Technique creates types and possibilities not already supplied as natural models, and societies cannot choose from a technological spectrum as though each technique were an isolated tool [F2, F5]. The 1990 text translates the inseparability of means and ends into a democratic criterion: institutions are democratic only when those subject to them can effectively participate in their formation [F8]. The 1997 text gives the argument a more specific immanent target: capitalism calls its own maximization practices rational while institutionally defining both the outputs and the costs to be maximized or minimized [F11].

### Effective power, not formal classification

A second continuity is the priority given to actual social relations over formal descriptions. The technical object is not reducible to its material form because it belongs to a technical ensemble and a network of significations [F6]. Likewise, democracy cannot be inferred from constitutions, representation, or elections when effective decisions remain in oligarchic hands [F9]. Economic rationality cannot be inferred from equilibrium models when firm decisions emerge from incomplete information and bureaucratic conflict [F12].

This common route produces different diagnoses:

- In the technical case, the error is **object isolation**: treating the tool as a self-contained causal unit.
- In the democratic case, it is **juridical formalism**: treating elections and legal liberties as proof of popular power.
- In the capitalist case, it is **theoretical abstraction**: treating firms, factors, prices, and equilibria as separable and determinate when the text describes them as socially interdependent.

The contrast is consequential because “What Democracy?” supplies a positive political criterion—effective equal participation—whereas “Technique” primarily establishes why technology must become politically contestable. The 1997 text offers an immanent critique of capitalism’s rationality but does not provide, in this source, a comparably developed complete institutional alternative.

### Conflict is constitutive, not residual

All three approaches make collective agency necessary, but they assign it different roles. In the 1984 account, conflict helps determine which available technology is put into effect; the selected technique is both an instrument and a stake of class struggle [F7]. In the 1990 account, democratic agency is constitutive of legitimacy itself: equality means the effective equal possibility of participating in power [F8]. In the 1997 account, working-class struggles and state intervention are reported to have thwarted capitalism’s spontaneous pressure toward low wages and overproduction, thereby contributing to its historical resilience [F13].

This is not a claim that every conflict is democratic or emancipatory. Managerial and bureaucratic clan struggles in capitalist firms are also conflicts, but they do not amount to collective autonomy [F12]. Conflict is therefore a shared mechanism only at a high level of abstraction. Its actors, institutional setting, and political meaning must remain explicit.

### Testing the pattern against exceptions

The strongest pattern—institutions and instruments cannot be separated from their social context—survives the corpus’s main exceptions, but only in qualified form.

First, the 1984 text grants that a technical activity considered narrowly can be evaluated by efficiency. A bridge may be competently or incompetently calculated. The exception does not restore social neutrality at the aggregate level: the choice to build a bridge, its location, and the technological system in which it belongs remain social and political questions [F5].

Second, the 1990 text acknowledges that Western regimes possess real rights and liberties and are relatively open. The classification “liberal oligarchy” therefore does not mean that these regimes are equivalent to totalitarian or closed regimes. The exception qualifies the diagnosis without establishing effective popular rule [F9].

Third, the 1997 text acknowledges capitalism’s substantial productive efficiency—its capacity to “deliver the goods.” The criticism is not that capitalism produces nothing or never innovates. It is that this efficiency is measured against institutionally selected products and narrowly defined costs, while depending historically on contestation and political controls that its own unrestricted logic would not produce [F11, F13].

These exceptions prevent a simple negative ranking. Against a criterion of output growth narrowly defined, capitalism may appear effective. Against criteria of ecological sustainability, effective democratic participation, or non-mutilating work, the same evidence supports a sharply different judgment. The corpus itself favors the latter questions, but it supplies no commensurable measurements with which to rank regimes.

## Supported judgment and most useful missing evidence

Across the collection, the defensible judgment is that **technique, democracy, and capitalist rationality are not autonomous domains governed by neutral tools, formal procedures, or universal metrics**. Each is instituted within a social-historical world. Technique participates in creating that world; democracy requires the collectivity’s effective capacity to institute and limit itself; capitalism elevates a particular quantitative and expansionary form of mastery while defining the products and costs by which it claims rationality [F15].

The decisive contrast is political. Democracy-as-autonomy makes explicit collective participation the criterion of legitimate institution. Capitalist rationality subordinates decisions to expansion, calculability, and managerial or oligarchic structures; contemporary technique can embody that orientation but is not, in the 1984 argument, its autonomous cause. The texts therefore support making technology and economic organization objects of democratic judgment rather than treating them as an unquestionable “realm of necessity.”

The most useful missing evidence would be: standardized institutional observations of who decides and with what revocability; comparable measures of citizens’ information, time, education, and participation; workplace studies linking technical choice to actual worker or managerial control; and ecological and human costs reported alongside output. Such evidence would need common time boundaries, explicit units and denominators, and culturally validated definitions. Without it, the collection supports a conceptual and institutional contrast, not a universal empirical law or ranked comparison.

### Counter-evidence

- The technical argument preserves a narrow efficiency distinction even while denying societal neutrality: "technical activity, considered in and for itself, takes no account of the value of the ends proposed to it." — doc: castoriadis1984_technique
- Western regimes retain real openness and liberties despite their oligarchic structure: "individuals and groups enjoy certain rights and certain liberties" — doc: castoriadis1990_what_democracy
- Capitalism’s productive performance is expressly acknowledged: "The system is highly effective at producing, and unprecedented technical development has occurred." — doc: castoriadis1990_what_democracy

### Open questions

- Which institutional arrangements can preserve effective popular participation at the scale of millions or billions?
- How would democratic control of technology operate across large, interdependent production systems?
- What common measures could incorporate ecological damage, work quality, political participation, and output without imposing capitalism’s own valuation scheme?
- Which claims about Western oligarchies and post-1980s economic outcomes hold when disaggregated by country, institution, and period?
- How should “technique,” “technology,” and “technoscience” be aligned, if at all, across the three texts?
