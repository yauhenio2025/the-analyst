# Shared inquiry and position map

The collection asks whether capitalism can be interpreted **as religion**, rather than merely as an economic order historically influenced by religion, and what such an interpretation explains about money, growth, innovation, social unity, and critique. The texts share an argument, but they were published in only two known years—two in 2001 and one in 2022. No order of development can be inferred between the same-year articles.

- **`deutschmann2001_capitalism_as_religion`** examines capital and the capital form of money in modern societies from an explicitly sociological perspective. Its central claim is that they possess intrinsically religious qualities and take religion’s place, while its distinctive contribution is to connect Simmel’s *Vermögen*, Marx’s capital, and constructivist accounts of entrepreneurial myths. Its most important qualification is methodological: “my aim is to explore the possibility” of integrating this idea into economic sociology, not to establish an unqualified identity between capitalism and religion [F1].
- **`deutschmann2001_promise_of_absolute_wealth`** likewise treats modern capitalism as essentially religious, but organizes the claim around money’s promise of absolute wealth. Its distinctive role is to describe entrepreneurs as interpreters of that promise and to develop a cycle of technological, organizational, and consumption myths. The analogy remains qualified: capitalist transcendence is historically dynamized, unlike traditional religion’s fixed distinction between mundane life and transcendence [F2].
- **`deutschmann2022_interpretation_of_capitalism_as_religion`** defines capitalism as a social system of unbounded markets and compares religion and capitalism as responses to society’s inability to observe itself as a whole. Its distinctive route is through market liberalism as a societal “self-description,” with money assuming a representational function formerly attributed to God. It expressly denies equivalence: capitalism cannot answer “the fundamental questions of religious faith” [F3].

# Strongest agreement and strongest qualification

## Strongest agreement: money is more than a neutral medium

The most stable substantive position is that money cannot be reduced to a technically neutral instrument. The first 2001 article states: “The intrinsic value of money rests on the generalized power potential which it gives to its proprietor.” The second calls money a “‘higher potency’ of the general concept of property” [F5]. Their shared object is money in modern capitalist society, and their conceptual warrant is Simmelian: money combines present purchasing power with access to unrealized material, social, temporal, and spatial possibilities.

The 2022 text preserves this position while changing its scale. It says that money “has long since emancipated itself from the harmless role of an economic medium of exchange” [F6]. Here the object is no longer principally the proprietor’s option set but money as the universal medium of spatially, socially, materially, and temporally unbounded markets. The result is continuity without simple repetition: the generalized capacity attributed to money in 2001 becomes part of an account of how global market society represents and coordinates itself. This is a reargued claim through a different institutional and epistemic route, not merely shared vocabulary.

## Strongest qualification: religious function is not religious equivalence

The strongest qualification concerns the meaning of “as religion.” The 2001 texts make the affirmative side forcefully. One announces that “capital and the capital form of money will be viewed as phenomena with *intrinsically* religious qualities,” while the other says that the growth imperative “has the power of a religious promise” [F4]. Yet the same-year articles already restrict the analogy: economic myths are historically transient, and capitalist transcendence is repeatedly constructed and destroyed rather than fixed.

The 2022 text sharpens this restriction: “It is clear that capitalism is far from being able to give an answer to the fundamental questions of religious faith” [F4]. It also insists that differences appear in “the mode of representation of God and money.” The apparent conflict disappears once the predicates are aligned. The 2001 claim concerns functional and structural religious qualities—trust, promise, transcendence, cultic growth, and mythic mediation. The 2022 limitation concerns theological reach and mode of representation. Saying that money assumes a social representational function does not say that it answers questions about creation, death, or ultimate reconciliation.

The collection therefore does not license the categorical proposition that capitalism **is equivalent to** a religion. It licenses the more precise judgment that capitalist money performs some religion-like functions and organizes a this-worldly structure of promise and transcendence, while remaining distinct from religious faith in community, ultimate reference, and eschatological scope.

# Joint judgment and remaining questions

Jointly, the collection licenses four conclusions:

1. Capitalism is more adequately treated as a social order organized by money and unbounded markets than as a merely economic subsystem.
2. Money’s importance lies not only in exchange but in generalized command over possibilities, property, labour, time, and social coordination.
3. Capitalist growth translates that open possibility into a permanent imperative through creative labour, entrepreneurship, institutions, myths, and the expansion of private-property claims.
4. Capitalism is religion-like because money bears promises, represents social unity, organizes this-worldly transcendence, and depends on faith-like projections; it is not equivalent to religion because it lacks religion’s mode of community and cannot answer ultimate questions.

The exact unresolved issue is whether capitalism’s growth-and-imagination cycle can continue indefinitely, collapse, or be politically contained without reproducing another totalizing promise. The 2001 sources pose disillusionment as a future possibility; the 2022 source recommends containment but supplies no demonstrated stopping mechanism [F9], [F10].

Deciding this would require evidence beyond conceptual analogy: passages or empirical studies specifying mechanisms by which ecological limits, financial instability, political regulation, or changes in property rights actually interrupt—or instead reproduce—the growth imperative. Evidence distinguishing temporary crisis from durable institutional containment would be especially decisive.
