# Corpus reading

## Shared inquiry and position map

Across the supplied collection, the shared inquiry is how a society creates, legitimates, and might consciously transform its technical, economic, and political institutions. The texts repeatedly reject isolated or formal explanations: technique cannot be detached from the social ensemble; democracy cannot be reduced to elections or legal equality; and capitalist rationality cannot be inferred from quantitative efficiency. No intellectual chronology is inferred from the documents’ order or edition dates. The comparison instead tracks continuity and divergence among their stated objects and warrants.

- **`castoriadis1984_technique`**, *Technique* (1984), ranges from Greek *techne* and anthropological accounts of language and tools to modern capitalist technology and prospective post-revolutionary production. Its central claim is that technique is creative and socially constitutive, neither a neutral instrument nor an autonomous destiny: “It is meaningless, under these conditions, to speak of neutrality and freedom of choice” [F1]. Its distinctive role is to connect technical objects, social organization, work, and political transformation. Its chief qualification is that “absolute creation” is not creation *ex nihilo*: “Techne always finds its starting-point in what is already there” [F1]. It also leaves the institutional design of transformed technology unresolved.

- **`castoriadis1990_what_democracy`**, *What Democracy?* (1990), addresses democracy as a political regime and evaluates contemporary Western societies. It defines democracy as collective autonomy and explicit self-institution: “Democracy is the regime of selflimitation [autolimitation], in other words, the regime of autonomy, or of self-institution [auto-institution]” [F4]. Its distinctive role is to specify political equality, publicness, representation, and principles for a democratic economy. It qualifies its constructive program in two ways: democracy has no externally guaranteed final limit, and its application at the scale of modern mass societies remains unresolved [F6].

- **`castoriadis1997_rationality_of_capitalism`**, *The “Rationality” of Capitalism* (1997), examines capitalism’s historical specificity, theoretical economic ideology, actual operation, and relative resilience. Its central claim is that capitalist legitimacy reduces rationality to quantitative maximization and minimization [F7], while academic political economy describes a fictive world rather than actual capitalism [F8]. Its distinctive role is an expressly immanent critique, testing capitalism against its own criteria. Its principal qualification is that capitalist efficiency concerns narrowly selected products and costs, excluding environmental and human harms; the future course of the regime remains undetermined [F9].

The collection’s strongest continuity is therefore not a single doctrine about one object, but a recurring mode of judgment: institutions must be examined as social-historical formations rather than as neutral means, self-sufficient mechanisms, or formal rules. That continuity does not erase differences of object. The 1984 text principally analyzes technique; the 1990 text defines democracy and diagnoses liberal oligarchy; the 1997 text critiques capitalist rationality and economic theory.

## Strongest agreement and strongest qualification

### Strongest substantive agreement: capitalist technique reduces the producer’s human role

The closest proposition-level agreement occurs between the 1984 and 1997 texts. *Technique* states that capitalist technology is directed “towards the suppression of man’s human role in production” [F11]. *The “Rationality” of Capitalism* describes technical evolution as “predominantly oriented toward the reduction, then the elimination, of man's role in production” [F11].

The object, time, definition, and warrant align. Both concern capitalist technical development over its historical course; both identify a direction rather than an accomplished endpoint; and both treat that direction as socially motivated, not as the autonomous unfolding of technology. The 1984 account supplies the workplace mechanism: management seeks independence from workers amid conflict over pace, quality, and organization. The 1997 account places the same tendency within capitalism’s drive toward “rational mastery.” The later text therefore reargues and broadens an aligned claim; the supplied material does not establish that it merely presupposes the earlier formulation.

A second strong agreement supports this analysis. The 1990 and 1997 texts both deny that capitalism realizes a genuine competitive market. The former says, “there is no market under capitalism,” while the latter states that the market “has never been” truly competitive under capitalism [F12]. This agreement matters because it blocks the claim that technical and economic choices are selected through a neutral optimizing mechanism.

### Strongest qualification: common anti-formalism is not one common answer

The broadest cross-corpus continuity is also the one most in need of qualification. In 1990, “equality signifies: the effective, and not on-paper, equal possibility for all to participate in power” [F13]. In 1997, capitalist rationality is “defined in a purely quantitative way as maximization/minimization” [F13]. The 1984 text similarly concludes that, at the scale of a whole technical ensemble, “It is meaningless, under these conditions, to speak of neutrality and freedom of choice” [F13].

These passages converge against judgments based solely on formal status, quantitative output, or isolated efficiency. They do not answer the same substantive question. The 1990 proposition is a normative definition of political equality; the 1997 proposition diagnoses capitalist ideology; the 1984 proposition concerns the social embeddedness of technique. Their relation is qualified agreement at the level of method and criterion, not a unified theory in which political equality, technical non-neutrality, and economic irrationality become synonyms.

The strongest unresolved issue lies in the transition from criticism to institutional construction. The 1984 text asks “How to reconcile this adaptation of tools to their users with the universality inherent in modern production” [F15]. The 1990 text states, “I am not claiming that these principles are sufficient to resolve all questions” [F15]. Thus the collection supports worker and citizen control as a direction, but not a settled account of scale, coordination, specialization, ecological constraint, information, or conflict between producer and consumer decisions.

## Overall judgment

The collection jointly licenses three conclusions.

First, technique, economy, and politics are socially instituted and cannot defensibly be represented as autonomous mechanisms. Second, capitalism’s claims to rationality are not vindicated by either competitive-market theory or technical efficiency: actual markets are oligopolistic, costs are selectively defined, and capitalist technical development tends to diminish producers’ agency. Third, autonomy would require effective collective participation in political and economic decisions, including transformation of production rather than merely a change in ownership or electoral personnel.

The collection does **not** license a complete institutional synthesis. Its exact unresolved question is how a large, technically complex society can combine worker control, consumer autonomy, informed public decision-making, specialization, ecological self-limitation, and coordination without rebuilding a separate managerial apparatus. The 1984 and 1990 texts explicitly acknowledge this gap, while the 1997 text principally diagnoses capitalism rather than filling it.

What would decide the issue is a passage or additional source specifying decision rights and conflict-resolution procedures across workplace, local, and society-wide levels: who chooses technologies, who sets investment and ecological limits, how affected nonworkers participate, how information is made public, and how delegates remain revocable at mass scale. Comparative historical evidence from durable institutions implementing those arrangements would be needed to move from normative compatibility to a warranted judgment of feasibility.

### Counter-evidence

- Technical activity considered in isolation can still be judged by efficiency; the denial of neutrality applies to the social-historical technical ensemble, not to every local engineering comparison: “There can be no doubt that technical activity, considered in and for itself, takes no account of the value of the ends proposed to it.” — doc: `castoriadis1984_technique`
- The 1990 text does not deny all achievements of Western regimes; it recognizes rights, liberties, and relative openness even while classifying them as liberal oligarchies: “individuals and groups enjoy certain rights and certain liberties” — doc: `castoriadis1990_what_democracy`
- The 1997 text does not deny capitalism’s productive capacity; its criticism qualifies what is produced and which costs count: “The system is highly effective at producing, and unprecedented technical development has occurred.” — doc: `castoriadis1990_what_democracy`

### Open questions

- How can effective democratic participation operate in collectivities of tens of millions or more?
- How should authority be divided among producers, consumers, local communities, and society-wide institutions?
- What procedures could reconcile user-adapted tools with standardization, specialization, and large-scale production?
- How should ecological externalities and irreversible harms constrain collective economic decisions?
- What institutional evidence would show that revocable delegation and informed participation can avoid a renewed managerial bureaucracy?
