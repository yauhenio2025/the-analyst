# Comparison question and position map

**Comparison question:** How do states reshape market-organized, security-relevant networks, through which instruments and corporate actors, and with what demonstrated effects and limits?

The comparison treats the documents as analyses of **conceptual approaches with nested empirical cases**, not as two equivalent policies. The AUKUS article examines a security partnership and its wider Pillar II political economy across the three members, with extensions to Japan and South Korea. The subsea article examines a global infrastructure sector through two nested cases: the US–China contest over SeaMeWe-6 and France’s nationalization of Alcatel Submarine Networks (ASN).

## Why these criteria are comparable

Four analyst-proposed criteria answer the shared inquiry and are supported by both texts:

1. **What is the inherited market organization?** This identifies the market structures states seek to reshape.
2. **How does the state intervene?** This permits comparison of regulatory, industrial, financial and coercive instruments.
3. **What role do corporations and capital play?** Both texts reject accounts that reduce firms to passive state instruments.
4. **What result and limitation does the text report?** This prevents aims and mechanisms from being mistaken for demonstrated outcomes.

These questions align political-economic mechanisms without treating AUKUS—a security partnership—and the subsea-cable network—a transnational infrastructure sector—as the same empirical object.

# Consequential similarities, differences and exception test

## Shared diagnosis: security intervention reorganizes rather than simply displaces markets

Both texts reject a binary contrast between “the state” and “the market.” The AUKUS article describes “a fusion of market instruments and geoeconomic warfare,” while the subsea article locates the present moment in “a persisting market-liberal organization of the global economy and a resurgence of state interventionism.” [F12] In both, inherited private or market coordination remains operative while governments redirect investment, ownership and access.

The commonality has limits. **aukus** advances a broader structural claim: security policy internalizes and privileges particular fractions of capital, making AUKUS a mutation of neoliberalism. **subsea** offers a narrower mechanism: states intervene after private networks have developed in directions that diverge from strategic priorities. The former foregrounds class coalitions and accumulation; the latter foregrounds network position, corporate leverage and strategic choice.

## Shared corporate centrality, but different causal emphasis

Both documents make firms constitutive rather than secondary. The AUKUS text identifies civilian enterprises, defence contractors, state-owned enterprises and start-ups as groups receiving a politically constructed market route. The subsea text argues that corporations stand at the center of many infrastructure networks and that states must influence their decisions. [F13]

Yet the corporate configurations differ:

- In **AUKUS**, the salient relationship is coalition formation among political blocs, agencies and heterogeneous capital groups.
- In **SeaMeWe-6**, it is consortium decision-making under competing subsidies and sanction threats.
- In **France–ASN**, it is state acquisition of a strategically positioned supplier.

Accordingly, “corporate centrality” does not imply corporate autonomy of the same kind in every case. France’s ownership strategy, Chinese support for state-linked HMN Tech, and AUKUS support for venture-backed firms represent distinct ownership and governance relations.

## Different intervention repertoires follow different institutional positions

The AUKUS article reports a dense mix of industrial finance, procurement, export-control reform and state support justified through technological sovereignty, supply-chain resilience and defence capability. [F5] The subsea framework differentiates industrial, regulatory and coercive realignment and associates their availability with network position. [F6]

SeaMeWe-6 supplies the sharpest coercive result: US threats changed the consortium’s supplier decision. France, lacking similar extraterritorial leverage, relied on ownership and industrial capacity. AUKUS is broader still: its instruments operate through allied regulatory alignment, public finance and security-linked market creation rather than one discrete infrastructure decision.

## Intended policies versus reported results

The evidence supports several **implemented mechanisms**—grants, financing arrangements, export-control changes, contracts and ASN’s acquisition—but not a uniform conclusion that they achieved their larger strategic aims.

- **AUKUS:** technological sovereignty, resilience and sovereign industrial capacity are stated policy rationales. The article documents beneficiaries and institutional changes, not successful deterrence, complete industrial sovereignty or delivery of nuclear-powered submarines.
- **SeaMeWe-6:** the supplier switch is a reported decision and therefore a comparatively concrete result. It does not demonstrate durable bifurcation because China pursued PEACE and EMA.
- **France–ASN:** acquisition is a completed or agreed ownership intervention. Forecast revenue growth and future resilience remain expectations rather than demonstrated outcomes.

## Testing the strongest pattern against the inconvenient case

The strongest common pattern is: **states respond to security competition by redirecting market-organized networks toward national or allied objectives.** [F12]

France is the principal exception to any stronger claim that this produces two neatly divided US and Chinese blocs. The subsea article reports that France “selectively engages with both United States and Chinese projects,” even while nationalizing ASN and improving domestic security capacity. [F14] Likewise, the AUKUS article’s Japan and South Korea material complicates a closed three-member bloc: both are non-members seeking Pillar II opportunities through their own regulatory and industrial strategies. [F15]

The surviving pattern is therefore **selective realignment**, not comprehensive decoupling. Security competition creates new interventions and alignments, but firms, middle powers and non-member partners retain room for differentiated strategies.

# Comparative judgment

The collection supports a qualified judgment that contemporary security competition **reworks market institutions through them rather than straightforwardly replacing them**. Both texts independently identify state action as dependent on firms, finance and inherited market structures. The AUKUS analysis most strongly explains the distributive and coalition-building character of this process; the subsea analysis more precisely differentiates intervention strategies and shows how network position conditions their effectiveness.

The corpus does **not** support a universal law of state resurgence, a ranking of national effectiveness, or a claim that globalization has been replaced by two closed blocs. SeaMeWe-6 demonstrates consequential coercive leverage, but parallel Chinese cables demonstrate incomplete control. France shows that a highly connected state can pursue selective autonomy, while also showing the financial and coercive limits on middle-power action. AUKUS shows broad market creation and institutional repurposing, but not completed strategic sovereignty or proven regional-security outcomes.

The most useful missing evidence would be common outcome data: project delivery, cost and delay measures; ownership and procurement shares before and after intervention; the durability of corporate relocation or supplier exclusion; distribution of gains among domestic and foreign firms; and comparable measures of resilience. For AUKUS specifically, evidence is needed on whether funded Pillar II capacities become operational and domestically retained. For subsea cables, longitudinal evidence is needed on traffic, pricing, redundancy, maintenance capacity and whether parallel routes actually reduce vulnerability.

### Counter-evidence

- The AUKUS article records internal opposition despite its bipartisanship thesis: 50 of 800 grassroots Labor branches and left-aligned unions passed opposing motions. This qualifies, but does not negate, the claim that bipartisan elite support enabled regulatory action.
- France’s industrial autonomy is constrained by weaker financing, EU fiscal pressure and dependence on US technology customers. [F10], [F14]
- The SeaMeWe-6 supplier reversal supports US coercive effectiveness, while PEACE and EMA show that the same intervention encouraged duplication rather than comprehensive exclusion. [F11]
- Continued global cable expansion contradicts a strong deglobalization interpretation; AUKUS likewise describes reconnection favoring selected groups rather than wholesale destruction of global markets. [F12]

### Open questions

- How much AUKUS-linked funding produces durable domestic capacity rather than temporary contracts or gains captured by foreign prime contractors?
- Do ASN nationalization and French cable redundancies measurably improve repair times, security and bargaining power?
- How do traffic and capacity shares across SeaMeWe-6, PEACE and EMA change after completion?
- Can the two conceptual frameworks be integrated without erasing their differences between class coalition, corporate bargaining and network position?
- Does selective alignment persist under stronger US sanctions or intensifying conflict?
