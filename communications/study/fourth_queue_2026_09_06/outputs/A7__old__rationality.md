### Value hierarchy

The discourse places **individual and collective autonomy** at the top: it is the only end expressly described as “valid and worthwhile.” Closely subordinate are the enabling values of critical lucidity, democratic contestation, intellectual honesty, worker solidarity, and public regulation. Human welfare, meaningful life, justice, and environmental integrity are treated as ends that economic organization must serve.

By contrast, productive efficiency has only conditional value. It is not denied altogether—capitalism “does deliver the goods”—but it loses legitimacy when detached from judgments about ends and when its accounting excludes “the destruction of the environment” and “the flattening of human lives.” Profit, acquisition, and “rational mastery” are reconstructed as capitalism’s dominant values, not endorsed as the author’s own.

### Counter-evidence

- The text concedes capitalism’s real productive capacity rather than treating it as wholly ineffective: “For the moment, capitalism still manages somehow or other to deliver these goods.”
- It acknowledges that markets have functioned within restricted ends despite injustice and instability: “it has somehow or other functioned within its limits and according to its own finalities.”
- It does not claim that noncapitalist societies are inherently rational or morally ideal: “Not that these societies would have been positively ‘irrational.’”
- It refuses to ground its critique merely in the circularity of social self-legitimation, since every society institutes its own criteria: “it is not here that we would have something that can constitute the grounding for a critique of capitalism.”
- It does not establish a complete alternative economic program. Its positive political ideal—autonomy—is explicit, but the institutions, allocation mechanisms, and environmental constraints of an autonomous economy remain underdeveloped.
- Although state intervention is treated favorably in particular contexts, state bureaucracy is also criticized through “the hypertrophic growth and absurdity of state bureaucracies.” The text therefore does not endorse statism without qualification.
- The author allows that global capitalism could remain economically successful for some time: “global companies might do better and better until the day the sky falls on our heads.” His critique is consequently ethical, political, ecological, and anthropological—not a simple prediction of imminent economic collapse.

### Open questions

1. What concrete institutions would allow an autonomous society to conduct production without reproducing bureaucratic or capitalist mastery?
2. How should environmental destruction, meaningful work, urban ugliness, and social solidarity be evaluated without reducing them to the monetary calculation the text rejects?
3. What legitimate role, if any, should markets retain within an autonomous society?
4. How should the text balance individual autonomy with collective decisions that constrain consumption, investment, or technology?
5. Which public bodies should regulate transnational capital when nation-states have been economically “disarmed”?
6. How can worker solidarity be reconstructed under globalized production and extreme international wage disparities?
7. What standards distinguish socially beneficial technology from technoscience oriented toward “the elimination of man’s role in production”?
8. If every society ultimately institutes its own values, on what basis can autonomy, justice, and environmental integrity claim cross-cultural validity?
