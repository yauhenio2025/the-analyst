## Analysis

### ACTANTS

1. **A1 — Global subsea cable network**  
   **Type:** technology. **Role:** Material backbone of global communications and terrain of geopolitical-economic competition; it carries “**over 97% of all internet traffic**.” **Agency:** mediating, because its topology distributes connectivity, dependence, vulnerability, and leverage. **Position:** obligatory passage point. **Interests/program:** capacity expansion, interoperability, continuity, and redundancy, as inferred from demand that “**currently surges with the expansion of AI and cloud-based services**.” **Source:** Abels (2026), pp. 94–104.

2. **A2 — Private corporations and cable consortia**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** Principal investors, owners, operators, and project decision-makers; “**Much of the subsea cable network…is developed, owned, and operated by private corporations**.” **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point. **Interests:** commercial returns, bandwidth sales, manageable regulatory exposure, and freedom from state interference. The network’s expansion remains “**driven by the commercial interests of tech and telecommunication firms**.” **Source:** pp. 94–103.

3. **A3 — United States government**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** Regulatory-coercive network realigner that uses market access, financing, diplomatic pressure, and sanctions to exclude Chinese firms. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point because access to US firms and markets gives it extraterritorial leverage. **Interests:** block Chinese suppliers, protect security, support SubCom, and prevent routes landing in China or Hong Kong. The United States “**relies mostly on coercive and regulatory network strategies**.” **Source:** pp. 99–103.

4. **A4 — Chinese government**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** Industrial network realigner using subsidies, state firms, and parallel projects to increase Chinese centrality. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point within Chinese-controlled financing and industrial ecosystems. **Interests:** capture cable-supply markets, advance domestic champions, and center data flows on China. China “**aims to capture 60% of the global market**.” **Source:** pp. 99–103.

5. **A5 — French state**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** Hub state pursuing strategic autonomy through ownership, industrial policy, landing regulation, and infrastructure protection. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point for French landings and state-controlled industrial capacity. **Interests:** sovereignty, industrial retention, network resilience, employment, and autonomy from both great powers. France “**selectively engages with both United States and Chinese projects**.” **Source:** pp. 101–103.

6. **A6 — SeaMeWe-6 cable system**  
   **Type:** technology. **Role:** Contested 19,000-km project through which supplier selection, financing, sanctions, and geopolitical alignment were negotiated. **Agency:** mediating. **Position:** obligatory passage point for the focal consortium and a prototypical transregional route. **Interests/program:** high-capacity connection from Singapore to France; it “**offers more than triple**” the capacity of SeaMeWe-5. **Source:** pp. 99–101.

7. **A7 — SeaMeWe-6 consortium**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** Collective procurement and financing body that translated state pressure into a supplier vote. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point for the contract. **Interests:** low project cost, financeability, access to customers, and avoidance of sanctions. A “**majority opt[ed] to award the contract to SubCom**.” **Source:** pp. 99–100.

8. **A8 — HMN Tech**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** Chinese cable manufacturer and layer promoted through subsidies but constrained by US sanctions. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** intermediary/challenger. **Interests:** win major contracts, gain global market share, and overcome Huawei-related security suspicions. Its roughly “**$500 million**” SeaMeWe-6 bid was “**substantially lower**” than SubCom’s asking price. **Source:** pp. 99–101.

9. **A9 — SubCom**  
   **Type:** organization. **Role:** US cable supplier that ultimately received the SeaMeWe-6 contract. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** intermediary, though one of only four globally relevant suppliers. **Interests:** contract acquisition and preservation of incumbent market position. Export–Import Bank support let it “**reduce its price to $600 million**.” **Source:** pp. 99–100.

10. **A10 — US sanctions and Entity List regime**  
    **Type:** text. **Role:** Legal inscription converting US security judgments into export, investment, and commercial restrictions. **Agency:** inscribed/delegated. **Position:** obligatory passage point for firms needing US technology, customers, or market access. **Interests/program:** deter engagement with targeted Chinese firms. Listing HMN Tech “**effectively imposed investment and export restrictions**.” **Source:** p. 100.

11. **A11 — US hyperscalers and technology customers**  
    **Type:** organization. **Role:** Major prospective buyers whose purchasing power amplifies US sanctions. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point in cable-capacity markets. **Interests:** high bandwidth, reliable interconnections, and regulatory compliance. US officials warned that stakeholders could be “**unable to sell capacity to US tech firms**.” **Source:** pp. 96, 100.

12. **A12 — PEACE and EMA parallel cable projects**  
    **Type:** technology. **Role:** Chinese counter-programs duplicating or amending routes after exclusion from SeaMeWe-6. **Agency:** mediating/inscribed. **Position:** intermediary. **Interests/program:** preserve Chinese-controlled connectivity and supplier demand. EMA “**was launched in direct reaction to US interference in SeaMeWe-6**.” **Source:** pp. 101, 103.

13. **A13 — Alcatel Submarine Networks (ASN)**  
    **Type:** organization. **Role:** Strategic cable manufacturer and layer whose ownership became an instrument of French network realignment. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** obligatory passage point in an oligopolistic supply market. **Interests:** industrial continuity, market growth, and access to capital. ASN “**leads the global market with more than 800,000 km of optical fiber deployed**.” **Source:** pp. 101–103.

14. **A14 — Orange and Orange Marine**  
    **Type:** organization. **Role:** Partly state-owned cable investor, consortium participant, installation and maintenance provider, and bridge to both US and Chinese projects. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** intermediary with hub significance. **Interests:** connectivity, commercial partnerships, resilience, and continued French industrial capacity. Orange “**has not ruled out cooperation with Chinese firms**.” **Source:** pp. 101–102.

15. **A15 — French landing sites, especially Marseille**  
    **Type:** technology. **Role:** Geographic nodes that make France indispensable to many transregional routes. **Agency:** mediating. **Position:** obligatory passage point. **Interests/program:** route aggregation and onward connectivity; “**Marseille in particular has emerged as a global cable hub**.” **Source:** pp. 101–102.

16. **A16 — Nokia**  
    **Type:** organization. **Role:** Former parent and seller of ASN. **Agency:** intentional. **Position:** intermediary. **Interests:** cost reduction and divestment from non-core operations amid financial pressure. Nokia was considering “**strategic divestments from non-core operations**.” **Source:** p. 102.

17. **A17 — Agence des participations de l’État (APE)**  
    **Type:** organization. **Role:** French public holding agency used to acquire and govern ASN. **Agency:** delegated. **Position:** obligatory passage point for the nationalization transaction. **Interests:** protect sovereignty and avert systemic risk; APE focuses on firms “**whose disappearance could entail a systemic risk**.” **Source:** p. 102.

18. **A18 — Private network realignment**  
    **Type:** concept. **Role:** Analytical program describing state attempts to redirect privately organized infrastructure. **Agency:** inscribed. **Position:** intermediary in the article’s explanatory network. **Interests/program:** reconcile strategic objectives with corporate decision-making. It denotes “**political interventions aimed at ex post reshaping**” predominantly private networks. **Source:** pp. 95–97.

### TRANSLATIONS

1. **T1 — A4 → A8: industrial enrollment**  
   **Type:** enrollment. China enrolled HMN Tech as a national champion through “**extensive financial support**” and subsidies that enabled a low SeaMeWe-6 bid. Interests were aligned by making HMN Tech’s commercial expansion serve China’s goal of market centrality. **Success:** partial. **Resistance:** US diplomatic pressure, sanction threats, Huawei-related distrust, and consortium concern over downstream market access.

2. **T2 — A3 → A7: coercive interessement**  
   **Type:** interessement. US officials reframed HMN Tech from the cheapest supplier into a project-wide sanctions risk; “**factoring in potential sanctions, HMN Tech became an increasingly costly option**.” Alignment occurred by linking consortium bankability and capacity sales to compliance with US preferences. **Success:** successful for supplier selection. **Resistance:** Chinese partners “**initially held firm in defending the contract**.”

3. **T3 — A3 → A9: financial enrollment**  
   **Type:** enrollment. The Export–Import Bank and trade-development grants strengthened SubCom’s bid, including “**‘training grants’ to select consortium partners**.” US industrial and security interests were aligned with SubCom’s commercial interest in winning the contract. **Success:** successful. **Resistance:** HMN Tech’s lower subsidized bid.

4. **T4 — A10 → A2/A7: mobilization through sanctions**  
   **Type:** mobilization. The Entity List delegated US policy into routine corporate due diligence and procurement avoidance. Consortia became reluctant to use HMN Tech because they feared “**sanctions and a loss of business with US hyperscalers**.” **Success:** partial but broadening. **Resistance:** projects outside US customer ecosystems and Chinese-supported parallel routes.

5. **T5 — A7 → A9: procurement enrollment**  
   **Type:** enrollment. The consortium translated cost, security, and customer-access concerns into a decisive vote for SubCom. **Success:** successful. **Resistance:** withdrawal of China Telecom and China Mobile, taking “**approximately 20% of the total investment volume**.”

6. **T6 — A3 → A4/A12: counter-mobilization**  
   **Type:** mobilization. US exclusion did not simply terminate Chinese infrastructure activity; it redirected China toward PEACE and EMA. Interest alignment joined Chinese connectivity, HMN Tech contracts, and state strategy in “**Chinese-controlled connections**.” **Success:** partial. **Resistance:** US sanctions restrict access to US technology customers.

7. **T7 — A5 → A13: ownership enrollment**  
   **Type:** enrollment. France converted ASN from a privately owned strategic supplier into a state-controlled capacity through APE’s purchase of 80% for €350 million. Alignment joined sovereignty, jobs, supply security, and expected profitability; the acquisition was called both “**‘strategic’ and ‘profitable’**.” **Success:** successful at the 80% stage. **Resistance:** fiscal limits and the transition before acquisition of Nokia’s remaining 20%.

8. **T8 — A13 → A5: problematization of industrial dependence**  
   **Type:** problematization. ASN’s possible sale or plant closure defined loss of domestic supply as a sovereignty and bottleneck problem. The feared consequences included “**layoffs and a loss of industrial capacity**.” **Success:** successful in legitimating nationalization. **Resistance:** the significant short-term public cost of acquisition.

9. **T9 — A15 → A5: mobilization of geography**  
   **Type:** mobilization. France translates Marseille’s route centrality into leverage over project planning and industrial strategy. Interests align because the “**hub character of French landing points**” makes France a linchpin. **Success:** partial. **Resistance:** France remains weaker in project financing and lacks US coercive reach.

10. **T10 — A5 → A14: selective strategic alignment**  
    **Type:** interessement. Partial public ownership and policy coordination enroll Orange in French resilience while preserving commercial flexibility. Orange joined US-associated SeaMeWe-6 but is also “**listed as a partner**” to the Chinese-owned PEACE cable. **Success:** partial. **Resistance:** US sanctions and dependence on US technology companies constrain selective engagement.

### BLACK BOXES

1. **B1 — “The internet” as seamless global connectivity**  
   The visible service hides seabed routes, repeaters, landing stations, cable ships, manufacturers, financiers, consortia, and jurisdictions. The box is stabilized by mundane operation: cables are “**Hidden beneath the sea and seemingly mundane in their operation**.” **Constituents:** A1, A2, A6, A11, A15. **Opening conditions:** outages, sabotage, surveillance disclosures, route disputes, or supplier sanctions.

2. **B2 — Private procurement as a commercial decision**  
   A supplier award appears to be a price-and-capability choice but hides subsidies, diplomatic pressure, customer dependencies, security reviews, and sanctions. It was destabilized when a cheap HMN Tech bid became “**an increasingly costly option**” after political risk was included. **Constituents:** A3, A4, A7–A11. **Opening conditions:** contested votes, sanctions threats, financing gaps, or withdrawal of investors.

3. **B3 — Cable supplier market**  
   “Cable supply” conceals an oligopoly, manufacturing plants, intellectual property, vessels, labor, finance, and maintenance capacity. It is stabilized by the dominance of “**just four globally relevant suppliers**.” **Constituents:** A8, A9, A13, A16. **Opening conditions:** sale of a supplier, capacity shortages, sanctions, plant closure, or wartime repair demand.

4. **B4 — ASN as an ordinary corporate asset**  
   Nokia’s subsidiary accounting concealed ASN’s role in French sovereignty, employment, repair capability, and global supply. The box opened when Nokia considered selling and France feared ASN would “**fall into the wrong hands**.” **Constituents:** A5, A13, A16, A17. **Opening conditions:** divestment, collateralization of intellectual property, foreign acquisition, or closure of Calais production.

5. **B5 — National security review**  
   Security language black-boxes intertwined industrial and geopolitical interests. The article notes interventions “**often aim to support domestic champions and limit the opponent’s influence**,” beyond stated security concerns. **Constituents:** A3, A8–A11. **Opening conditions:** investigative reporting, disclosure of grants, comparison of bids, or evidence of market-share effects.

6. **B6 — State control of infrastructure**  
   Statecraft accounts can conceal corporate autonomy, historic private investment, and network inertia. The article opens this box by arguing that “**corporations, not states…stand at the center of global infrastructure networks**.” **Constituents:** A1–A5, A18. **Opening conditions:** failed state interventions, corporate circumvention, consortium resistance, or privately financed expansion.

### CONTROVERSIES

1. **C1 — Who should supply SeaMeWe-6?**  
   **Actants:** A3, A4, A6–A11. The contest concerned whether low cost and Chinese industrial support outweighed US-defined security and sanctions risks. It opened B2, B3, and B5. **Status:** resolved for this procurement, with SubCom selected. **Resolution:** consortium vote followed by investor substitution and Entity List designation.

2. **C2 — Security versus industrial protectionism**  
   **Actants:** A3, A4, A8–A11. What is contested is whether restrictions primarily protect infrastructure or strategically exclude Chinese competitors. The source says interventions “**often aim to support domestic champions**.” It opens B5. **Status:** ongoing. **Resolution mechanism:** none; competing security narratives and market outcomes continue.

3. **C3 — Fragmentation versus duplication and expansion**  
   **Actants:** A1, A3, A4, A6, A12. The issue is whether rivalry deglobalizes the network or produces overlapping systems. Evidence favors expansion: networks “**continue to expand**” through “**parallel and interlocking systems**.” It opens B1 and B6. **Status:** ongoing. **Resolution mechanism:** future topology, ownership, traffic, and interoperability patterns.

4. **C4 — Who should own ASN?**  
   **Actants:** A5, A13, A16, A17. The controversy concerned whether ASN was a disposable Nokia asset or a strategic French capacity. It opened B3 and B4. **Status:** largely resolved through state acquisition of 80%, with the remaining 20% pending. **Resolution:** nationalization through APE.

5. **C5 — French autonomy versus dependence on US ecosystems**  
   **Actants:** A3, A5, A10, A11, A14. France seeks balanced engagement, but “**fears of sanctions and a deep reliance on US-American tech companies limit France’s capacity**.” **Status:** ongoing. **Resolution mechanism:** diversification, ownership of industrial capabilities, and cable redundancy, though no complete solution is identified.

6. **C6 — Market efficiency versus resilience**  
   **Actants:** A1, A2, A5, A13–A15. Private actors may underprovide redundancy and security, while states justify public investment to address systemic risks. The article recommends intervention “**where private investments are insufficient to address systemic risks**.” **Status:** ongoing. **Resolution mechanism:** public investment, nationalization, repair capacity, and regional/global governance.

### INSCRIPTIONS

   **Program:** prevent transactions that strengthen entities deemed contrary to US security interests. **Inscribed in:** A10. **By:** A3. **Flexibility:** rigid. **Anti-program:** consortium procurement from HMN Tech and unrestricted export or investment flows. Anchor: “**acting contrary to the national security or foreign policy interests of the United States**.”

2. **I2 — Strategic route without Chinese landing**  
   **Program:** connect Asian partners while avoiding direct Chinese connectivity. **Inscribed in:** A6. **By:** A3 and aligned consortium members. **Flexibility:** rigid once laid. **Anti-program:** direct network dependence on China. Anchor: the United States seeks to “**prevent subsea cables from landing in China and Hong Kong**.”

3. **I3 — Chinese-controlled parallel connectivity**  
   **Program:** reproduce transregional connectivity under Chinese ownership and supply. **Inscribed in:** A12. **By:** A4 and A8-linked organizations. **Flexibility:** rigid materially, flexible in financing and landing partnerships. **Anti-program:** total exclusion of Chinese firms from global cable development. Anchor: projects “**amend existing routes with Chinese-controlled connections**.”

4. **I4 — French notification and safety control**  
   **Program:** make cable laying in French waters visible and governable. **Inscribed in:** French decree governing ocean infrastructure, represented by A5. **By:** A5. **Flexibility:** flexible administrative discretion. **Anti-program:** unnotified or unsafe cable deployment. Anchor: operators must “**notify the state before laying cables in French waters**.”

5. **I5 — Strategic state ownership**  
   **Program:** retain cable manufacturing, intellectual property, labor, and deployment capacity under French control. **Inscribed in:** A13 through A17. **By:** A5. **Flexibility:** delegable through corporate governance. **Anti-program:** foreign capture, Calais closure, or loss of access to ASN. Anchor: APE would “**acquire 80% of ASN for €350 million**.”

6. **I6 — Resilience through redundancy and maintenance**  
   **Program:** withstand sabotage, breakdowns, and coercive disruption. **Inscribed in:** A1 and public investment programs. **By:** states and EU institutions. **Flexibility:** delegable. **Anti-program:** single-route or single-supplier dependence. Anchor: governments should prioritize “**creating cable redundancies and investing in deployment and maintenance capacities**.”

### NETWORK EFFECTS

1. **E1 — Extension**  
   Rivalry produces new routes rather than simple contraction; PEACE and EMA extend Chinese-linked connectivity while SeaMeWe-6 adds new landings. **Key actants:** A1, A6, A12. **Mechanism:** parallel state-supported and private investment. Anchor: “**the network is currently getting upgraded and expanded**.”

2. **E2 — Durability**  
   Nationalization, repair fleets, cable ships, and underwater robotics make French-linked infrastructure more maintainable and politically durable. **Key actants:** A5, A13, A14. **Mechanism:** ownership and maintenance capacity. Anchor: France controls “**a significant share of global cable manufacturing and laying capacity**.”

3. **E3 — Mobility**  
   Sanctions travel beyond US territory through contracts, technology dependencies, customer relations, and expectations. **Key actants:** A3, A10, A11. **Mechanism:** firms pre-emptively avoid HMN Tech to preserve US business. Anchor: “**consortia have become reluctant to work with the Chinese firm**.”

4. **E4 — Scale**  
   A supplier decision in one consortium affects the global market position of HMN Tech and future procurement norms. **Key actants:** A6–A11. **Mechanism:** precedent, sanctions compliance, and concentrated supply. Anchor: SeaMeWe-6 will “**likely have consequences for the future of the subsea cable industry as a whole**.”

5. **E5 — Durability through material inertia**  
   Existing routes constrain rapid geopolitical redesign because “**Networks are tedious to construct and, once built, slowly respond**.” **Key actants:** A1, A6, A12, A15. **Mechanism:** sunk costs, long construction timelines, fixed geography, and scarce suppliers.

6. **E6 — Scale through hub centrality**  
   Marseille’s accumulated landings elevate France from a territorial state to a consequential network actor. **Key actants:** A5, A15. **Mechanism:** route convergence makes France “**a linchpin in the planning of transregional cable projects**.”

### OBLIGATORY PASSAGE POINTS

1. **OPP1 — Global cable infrastructure (A1)**  
   Communications, cloud services, outsourcing, and financial transactions must traverse the physical network. **Why obligatory:** it carries “**over 97% of all internet traffic**.” **Control:** ownership, operation, route topology, landing access, and repair capacity.

2. **OPP2 — Cable consortia and corporations (A2/A7)**  
   State strategies must pass through corporate investment and procurement decisions because states seek to reshape infrastructure “**by manipulating the actions of private corporations**.” **Control:** bids, votes, capital contributions, contracts, and capacity sales.

3. **OPP3 — US market and sanctions system (A3/A10/A11)**  
   Projects seeking US customers, finance, or technology must account for US restrictions. **Control:** Entity List rules, landing reviews, grants, diplomacy, and the threat that stakeholders could not “**sell capacity to US tech firms**.”

4. **OPP4 — Oligopolistic supplier base (A8/A9/A13)**  
   New projects must pass through a very small set of capable manufacturers and installers. **Control:** scarce production, vessels, intellectual property, pricing, and delivery slots; the sector has “**just four globally relevant suppliers**.”

5. **OPP5 — French landing geography (A15)**  
   Many Europe–Asia and transatlantic routes rely on French territory, especially Marseille. **Control:** landing permissions, safety rules, terrestrial interconnection, and geographic route convergence. France hosts “**51 international cables**.”

6. **OPP6 — ASN under APE ownership (A13/A17)**  
   Access to a major share of global cable manufacturing and laying now passes through French-controlled corporate governance. **Control:** ownership, investment, manufacturing at Calais, cable ships, and contract allocation. ASN “**leads the global market**.”

### Counter-evidence

- US displacement of HMN Tech did not eliminate Chinese involvement: “**China Unicom remained**” in SeaMeWe-6.
- US realignment is incomplete because PEACE and EMA preserve Chinese-controlled routes; “**some of their data will inevitably pass through these cables**.”
- France’s nationalization does not create comprehensive autonomy because its role “**is significantly less pronounced than in cable supply**” for project planning and financing.
- Corporate agency remains constrained as well as empowered: the consortium reversed supplier choice once sanctions threatened its customer base.
- Claims about deliberate Chinese sabotage remain uncertain in the text. The Matsu and TPE incidents are reported, but the supplied evidence does not conclusively establish state direction.
- The article’s expansion thesis coexists with selective fragmentation: the United States is “**avoiding direct connectivity with China**.”
- State ownership may improve control but creates fiscal exposure; France’s options are constrained by “**budgetary limits**” and EU fiscal rules.
