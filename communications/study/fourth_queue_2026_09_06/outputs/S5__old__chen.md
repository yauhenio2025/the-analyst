### Counter-evidence

- Jaeggi already acknowledges that social problem perception is standpoint-dependent, which weakens any portrayal of her as simply assuming objectively given problems — anchor: “depends on that, for whom a particular situation is actually a crisis or a problem.”
- Jaeggi’s theory expressly permits revolutionary replacement rather than only linear reform, so “accumulation” may be intended more flexibly than retention of every prior capacity — anchor: “only with a revolution (a radical paradigm shift).”
- Dewey’s model does not require final solutions without new problems; it describes inquiry as “a continuous spiral,” which supports open-ended learning even when capacities are revised.
- Kuhn concedes instrumental growth in knowledge-how — anchor: “knowledge clearly does grow” — providing at least partial support for Jaeggi’s accumulation language.
- Chen concedes that paradigm choice still involves evidence and reasons, so the psychologist proposal is not intended as pure voting or arbitrary preference — anchor: “he does not deny the importance of good reasons and empirical evidence.”
- The claim that collective judgment makes a movement progressive sits in tension with the article’s critique of fascist and exclusionary movements; widespread collective endorsement could constitute “progress” descriptively while remaining condemnable — anchor: “it is the majority of group members that ultimately choose.”
- Chen’s examples establish that progressive changes can involve losses, but they do not by themselves prove that no higher-order accumulation—such as reflexive capacity—survives those losses. The article frames this as inability “to solve this kind of problem” alongside ability to solve another, leaving aggregate comparison unsettled.

### Open questions

1. Does “collective belief” merely constitute the social status of progress, or does it also justify calling the change genuinely progressive? The text says judgment “makes them progressive,” but also preserves “reasons or evidence.”
2. Which community is relevant when societies contain minorities, dissenters, and transnational actors? The proposed final decision belongs to “the majority of group members,” but group boundaries remain unspecified.
3. How does the psychologist account criticize collectively endorsed oppression without reintroducing a substantive normative criterion?
4. Does incommensurability rule out only neutral comparison, or also context-sensitive rational comparison between forms of life?
5. Could Jaeggi define enrichment at a reflexive or metacapacity level—such as improved ability to identify and revise problem frames—rather than as retention of specific know-how?
6. What evidence would distinguish a collectively constituted progressive movement from propaganda-induced or coercively manufactured agreement?
7. Is “psychologist” the most accurate label, given that the alternative relies heavily on social ontology, collective intentionality, and community structure rather than individual psychology?
8. Can “progress without ‘progress’” support durable critical-theoretical institutions, or is it limited to local, forward-looking political commitments?
