### Overall interpretation

“Absolute wealth” is not treated as a stock of possessions or an achieved economic state. It is a **utopian promise**: “individual command over the totality of human possibilities.” It is caused by the peculiar form of money as an “absolute means,” but then becomes causally productive in its own right. It motivates accumulation, grounds private property, requires entrepreneurial and expert interpretation, and drives recurring innovation.

The central causal architecture is:

> **money as generalized power → promise of absolute wealth → entrepreneurial interpretation through myths → mobilization of creative labour and cooperation → innovation → temporary profit and growth → institutionalization and lock-in → declining profitability and crisis → new myth**

This is not a stable equilibrium mechanism. It is a historically open, self-reinforcing but self-undermining cycle. Successful myths create the institutional stability that ultimately exhausts their innovative force. Absolute wealth therefore remains permanently promised but never finally realized, producing the “ever-increasing indebtedness” that Deutschmann adopts from Benjamin.

### Counter-evidence

- Marx’s temporal labour theory does not by itself explain profitability; this limits any simple claim that labour expenditure guarantees redemption of the promise. — anchor: “does not provide a sufficient explanation for the profitability of capital”
- Exploitation and profitability may correlate, but the author expressly denies necessity. — anchor: “even this is not necessarily the case”
- Economic myths cannot be manufactured at will by entrepreneurs or firms. — anchor: “the success of consumption waves is always a socially emergent phenomenon, which can never be ‘made’ by any single actor”
- The continuity of creative destruction is not assured, so the causal cycle is contingent rather than automatic. — anchor: “whose future is anything but guaranteed”
- The proposed interpretation of financial decoupling is qualified rather than asserted as proven. — anchor: “may be interpreted as a symptom”
- Deutschmann does not claim that money grants absolute command in reality; it contains a “promise” or “utopia,” and the miser’s attempt to possess its power directly is called “an illusion.”

### Open questions

1. What empirical evidence would distinguish causation by the promise of absolute wealth from causation by ordinary profit incentives?
2. How could “social resonance” be measured independently of an innovation’s eventual success?
3. Under what conditions does myth diffusion produce productive innovation rather than speculative bubbles?
4. How much of the causal account applies outside Western societies shaped by Christian secularization?
5. What institutions could sustain cooperation and innovation without invoking the promise of unlimited wealth?
6. Can the myth cycle slow or stabilize without producing the predicted crisis of wages and employment?
7. Does financialization block productive creative destruction, or can it sometimes finance new technological myths?
8. What would “weaning off” absolute wealth mean institutionally for money, property, employment, and social reproduction?
