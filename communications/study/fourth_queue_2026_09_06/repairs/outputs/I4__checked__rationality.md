# Reading

## The cooperation problem: capitalism depends on constraints its dominant actors resist

The text documents a consequential incentive conflict, but not a classic free-rider or commons game. Its strongest case is a **system-level contradiction between capitalist firms’ cost-cutting incentives and the conditions for capitalism’s collective stability**. Left to themselves, employers seek low wages and high productivity; generalized across firms, that tendency restricts mass consumption and risks overproduction. Workers’ struggles and state demand management counteracted it, expanded consumer markets, and supported the postwar boom—even though the capitalist establishment initially fought those measures.

The shared outcome must be stated carefully. The text does not show employers and workers jointly agreeing on welfare, autonomy, or distributive justice. The narrower outcome is the continued functioning and expansion of the capitalist economy: avoiding its “drowning in its own production” and sustaining demand. This is desirable principally in the system’s own terms, not necessarily in workers’ terms. Indeed, the text describes production as collectively generated—“the product of the cooperation of a collectivity”—while emphasizing conflict over its organization and distribution. [F1] [F2]

The key mechanism is a composition problem. For an individual firm, lower wages can appear as lower costs. For the system, wages also finance demand for firms’ output. The text says that working-class struggles forced wage increases and working-time reductions, thereby creating “enormous domestic consumer markets.” [F4] Cooperation was therefore not produced by voluntary alignment among firms. It arose through **organized conflict and political constraint** imposed on firms’ immediate incentives.

The same structure appears in investment decisions. The text says firms must invest enough to absorb productive capacity, but that nothing guarantees they will do so because investment depends on uncertain expectations of future demand. [F8] This is an additional coordination and information failure: individually cautious investment decisions can generate unemployment and contraction. The passage does not, however, specify strategic withholding by firms or show that each firm expects others to bear the cost. It therefore supports an expectations-driven coordination problem, not a free-rider diagnosis.

The strongest counter-passage is that capitalism has nevertheless “somehow or other functioned within its limits and according to its own finalities.” [F12] This challenges any claim that the incentive conflict makes capitalist operation impossible. It does not erase the barrier; it narrows the conclusion. The text’s claim is that capitalism’s relative stability has depended on workers’ struggles, political reactions, and institutional restraints that dominant capitalist actors initially opposed—not that the system invariably collapses whenever firms pursue their immediate interests.

Nor does the text establish a fully common good. Workers’ demands arose from opposition to employers, while capitalism’s productive expansion includes costs and harms the text explicitly rejects. The defensible diagnosis is therefore:

> **Firms’ incentive to minimize wages and make investment according to private, uncertain profit expectations obstructs the system-level need for sufficient demand and stable utilization of productive capacity. Historically, worker organization and state intervention partially overcame this barrier, despite resistance from the capitalist establishment.**

This is an incentive and coordination problem embedded in distributive conflict, not a documented tragedy of the commons or straightforward free-riding game.

### Counter-evidence

- Capitalism’s continued functioning weakens any claim that the identified conflict necessarily prevents all collective production or system survival. [F12]
- The corrective institutions generated inflationary pressure and political reaction; the text does not show that they permanently resolved the underlying conflict. [F7] [F9]
- Workers and employers do not share all relevant goals. The system-level outcome of sustained capitalist expansion should not be treated as an agreed common good merely because both groups participate in production.
- The text attributes productive resilience partly to conflict-induced constraints, not to consensual cooperation among firms, workers, and the state.

### Open questions

- Did individual employers understand that generalized wage suppression could weaken aggregate demand, or does the text establish only the structural consequence?
- Which specific “moderate policies” were available, and were they politically or administratively feasible? [F9]
- How much of the postwar expansion is attributable to wage growth and demand regulation rather than reconstruction, technological change, demographics, or other factors?
- Under what conditions can worker organization and state intervention be sustained against the establishment resistance the text describes? [F6]
- Does contemporary global production weaken the earlier domestic link between national wages and demand for national firms’ output?
