# Conditional relations connecting progress to other propositions

The paper’s most useful distinction is between **conditions proposed as criteria of progress or regression** and **conditions that expose the limits of those criteria**. In Chen’s presentation, Jaeggi treats solved problems as sufficient for evaluating practices and institutions as progress, without stating that problem-solving is necessary for every instance of progress [F1]. Jaeggi’s criterion for describing modernity as regressive is more restrictive: it must be demonstrable that modernity systematically fails or is unable to face crisis trends for self-caused reasons and that access to those trends is blocked [F2].

Chen does not merely reverse these implications. His criticism is that Jaeggi’s use of the paradigm-shift analogy becomes unjustifiable **if** she also retains a linear accumulation of experience [F3]. The objection turns on qualified possibilities: if forms of life are incommensurable and problem-recognition depends on cultural background or value, competence at solving one kind of problem can coexist with inability to solve another [F7]. This does not establish that every transition loses capacities, but it blocks an unconditional inference from progress to uniform experiential accumulation.

The paper’s proposed alternative is likewise conditional. If the parallel between scientific and social progress is retained, Chen argues, it points toward a psychologist rather than pragmatist account [F12]. A second, related implication limits what a formal criterion can accomplish: if Jaeggi seeks a strict, historically valid distinction between progressive and regressive practices, Chen argues that the resulting standard cannot remain purely formal and neutral [F14]. These are argumentative implications, not causal claims or demonstrated biconditionals.

These tests block several unwarranted converses and generalizations. From the proposition that solved problems permit evaluation as progress, it does not follow that every activity represented by its participants as problem-solving is progressive [F1] [F10]. From the growth of instrumentally defined scientific know-how, it does not follow that social experience accumulates across incommensurable forms of life [F4] [F13]. Nor does identifying a transition as progressive entail that every experience or practical capacity grows: modern marriage and the creation of childhood are introduced as limiting cases [F9] [F11].

Chen’s final alternative remains conditional rather than universally established. Retaining the scientific/social parallelism points, in his argument, toward a psychologist account based on collective intentionality [F12]. Yet the paper’s own conclusion leaves pressure on that proposal: the inability of a formal criterion to remain neutral under a strict historical demarcation does not by itself demonstrate that collective judgment supplies an adequate, nonrelativist replacement [F14].

### Open questions

- Whether the psychologist pattern’s reliance on collective intentionality fully escapes moral relativism is not settled by these conditional relations.
- The paper does not establish that social groups can never possess a sufficiently shared problem-solving goal; it argues that the scientific community’s relevant shared commitment is absent from social forms of life as such [F13].
- The examples do not determine how losses and gains across incommensurable experiential domains should be compared [F7] [F9] [F11].
- No retained relation supplies a biconditional definition of progress or regression.
