# Reading

## What kind of feedback account the paper supports

The paper’s most useful distinction is between **capital circulation**, **innovation diffusion**, and the broader **myth cycle**. These are related, but they are not one fully specified formal loop.

At the first level, capital is defined as money that must return with an increment [F1]. Profitability depends on labour being employed in a creatively destructive way [F2]. This establishes a growth-dependent circulation path—money expenditure → creative labour → surplus value → incremented return—but the paper does not separately specify all realization, demand, and reinvestment links needed to sign and close that path as a formal feedback loop.

At the second level, the paper supplies enough directional links to reconstruct a **conditionally reinforcing innovation-diffusion loop**. Successful communication of applications stimulates imitation and accelerates diffusion [F3]. Diffusion stimulates competition, and competition stimulates the invention of additional applications [F4]. If those new applications are successfully communicated, the path returns to imitation and diffusion. The return is conditional: the text preserves “successful communication,” so invention alone does not guarantee renewed diffusion.

At the third level, the text explicitly calls the development of economic myths a cycle of generation, diffusion, institutionalization, and decline [F5]. Generalization moves a technology toward institutionalization and is associated with an increasing flow of material and human capital investment [F6]. Institutionalization then erodes innovative aura, locks investment into selected options, shifts labour from creative-destructive to reproductive use, and removes the paradigm’s capacity to induce further growth and surplus value [F7]. As “sclerosis” approaches, profitability declines [F8], leading to crisis and a search for alternatives [F9]. Decline of the old myth becomes the starting point of a new one [F10], which redirects inherited structures and knowledge rather than simply repeating the old cycle [F11].

This broader sequence accounts for the paper’s alternation between high-uncertainty, high-profit-chance conditions and more calculable conditions with declining profits [F12]. It is a **source-described historical cycle**, not a calibrated periodic feedback model. The component effects do not all operate on one common variable, the new myth is not identical to the old one, and the source gives no cycle length, transition threshold, effect size, or numeric rate. Its claims of acceleration remain qualitative [F13].

## Feedback structures accounting for the described behavior

The paper’s growth phase is best accounted for by the interaction of two amplifying mechanisms. First, successful communication promotes imitation and diffusion [F3]. Second, diffusion intensifies competition and thereby stimulates the invention of more applications [F4]. Where those applications are communicated successfully, invention supplies new material for further diffusion. This is the clearest reinforcing structure in the paper, but its closure depends on communicative success.

The same diffusion process also produces limits to the profitability of a particular innovation. Successful innovators may gain temporary monopoly profit [F19], but competitors follow and the innovation’s spread reduces that profitability [F16]. This is a balancing pressure, although it is an open balancing path rather than a complete loop: the text does not specify a return from lower profitability to the original innovation success.

At the system level, diffusion and generalization lead toward institutionalization and concentrated investment [F6]. Institutionalization creates calculability, but it also generates the conditions of decline: aura loss, investment lock-in, blocked alternatives, reproductive labour, and reduced growth and surplus value [F7]. Sclerosis lowers profitability [F8], prompting crisis and search [F9]. Decline then starts a new myth [F10], drawing selectively on the old cycle’s knowledge, side effects, and blind spots [F11]. These relations account for the stated alternation between uncertain conditions with high risks and profit chances and more calculable conditions with declining profits [F12].

The broad cycle should not be mistaken for equilibrium oscillation. The new myth does not simply restore the previous state [F10] [F11]. Nor does the paper establish a quantitative tipping point at which institutionalization becomes sclerosis. The result is a qualitative account of endogenous renewal and decline, not a simulation-ready model.

## Counterforces and intervention questions

Cooperation and communicative framing are enabling conditions, not tested interventions. Entrepreneurs require cooperation from workers and surrounding actors [F17], while paradigms can induce cooperation, learning, and joint projects [F18]. It is therefore reasonable to ask whether stronger cross-boundary cooperation would support innovation communication and diffusion, but the paper does not report an intervention comparison or establish that cooperation alone would be sufficient.

The text presents market radicalism as a possible counterforce. It “tends to paralyse” the myth cycle by confronting entrepreneurs with unmanageable complexity, and entrepreneurs “may” withdraw toward financial markets, knowledge industries, or entrepreneurship rhetoric [F20]. These are interpretive and modal claims, not an observed controlled transition.

The depressive spiral is more explicitly hypothetical: declining wages and rising unemployment are projected if creative destruction were to stop [F21]. The proposed withdrawal from the pursuit of absolute wealth and extension of critique to money is likewise a possible future agenda, not an implemented or evaluated intervention [F22].

### Open questions

- What are the durations of generation, diffusion, institutionalization, decline, crisis, and renewal?
- At what degree of generalization does institutionalization become lock-in or “sclerosis”?
- What are the relative causal contributions of aura loss, investment lock-in, and reproductive labour to profitability decline?
- Under what conditions are newly invented applications communicated successfully enough to close the reinforcing diffusion loop?
- How do demand realization and reinvestment close—or fail to close—the capital-circulation path?
- Are the projected depressive spiral and critique of money observable under any comparative intervention design?
- How can the historically transformative myth cycle be translated into formal variables without assuming that each new myth reproduces the previous system state?
