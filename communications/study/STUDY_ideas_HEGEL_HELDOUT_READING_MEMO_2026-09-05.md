### Elling — substantive gains in qualifications, adoption, and the scope of practical demands

The [previous final](../../data/study/ideas_hegel_heldout_2026_09_05/43f051bdd4d89076/outputs/epistemological_method_detector__previous__elling.md) already treats the article as interpretive reconstruction and says absent interviews are not a defect for that aim. It distinguishes Hegel's statements from Elling's sharper taxonomy (**F4**), recognizes the bracketing of completed history (**F6**), and limits confidence when the reconstruction becomes a claim about actual subjects or comparative modern volatility (**F16/F17**). Its identification question is relevant to the programme, and **F26/F27** correctly separate acknowledged non-development of a remedy from the continued claim that philosophical reflexivity is needed.

Added **F30**, however, invents a professed/practised mismatch between “weakly critical” and a necessary threat. Necessary threat is precisely how the article defines weak criticism (E:631–634). **F19** similarly contrasts the modest label with “actively encourages,” rather than comparing the same claim's modality. The final question about observationally distinguishing unrealized necessary threat from a stably free habit also treats compatible conditions as alternatives. An informative test concerns the alleged structural basis and seriousness of risk, not simply whether free habitual practice occurs.

**F25** calls the content/uptake qualification an exemption, despite the prose admitting it is substantive. E:390–392 identifies compatible necessary conditions; it is not an exception Elling reserves for his own method. **F21/F24** also blur a proposed case-by-case practical demand with a method the article promises already to carry out. Missing worked application limits the practical programme's demonstrated usefulness; it does not alone establish inconsistency between the article's interpretive aim and practice.

The critic's **F3** usefully attributes the tooth-brushing example to Novakovic's reflective argument (E:158–162). But its rationale for withholding adoption—that Elling later says the reflective reading downplays the antinomy—is insufficient: E:172–180 and 673–683 explicitly retain its freeing/flexible-habit insight. Who supplies an argument and whether Elling selectively accepts it are separate questions. Added **F29** similarly turns Hegel's statements into “direct tutelage” and truth authority. The primary texts are authoritative for reconstructing Hegel, while Elling expressly reconstructs distinctions Hegel does not make and distances himself from historical completion. The body recognizes much of this; the added row's stronger characterization needs more than a quotation establishing Hegel's two textual directions.


The [revised final](../../data/study/ideas_hegel_heldout_2026_09_05/43f051bdd4d89076/outputs/epistemological_method_detector__revised__elling.md) improves several specific mistakes. **F27** expressly identifies the bad-habits qualification as a substantive distinction, and **F22/F23** plus the prose preserve selective adoption of both rival frameworks. The testimony section calls the engagement argumentative rather than testimonial dismissal. It attributes Novakovic's example while allowing its actual counterexample role (**F6**). The professed/practised prose explicitly says the modest label concerns the thesis's content rather than the strength of its warrant, avoiding the old added **F30**'s false contrast. These are clear interpretive gains, not just more cautious labels.

The practical-method distinction also becomes more discriminating. **F7/F28**, self-application, and the counter-evidence allow case-by-case inquiry to be a consequence for future normative assessment rather than a procedure the present interpretive article promises to complete. Empirical limits are generally scoped to claims read as assertions about actual societies (**F17/F18**). The final question now tests the inference from habit's structure to necessary threat; it no longer asks readers simply to distinguish latent risk from stable free practice as mutually exclusive conditions.

Some residual demands need the same scope discipline. **F16** and the all-possible-societies discussion describe the even-if argument as conceptually argued but not empirically tested. The latter is not by itself an evidential defect in a conditional conceptual argument. The appropriate test first concerns the premises connecting normative actualization with continued subjective uptake (E:635–642); empirical comparisons bear on actual historical generalizations. Likewise, formal irreducibility of subjectivity (E:687–689) does not itself promise first-person testimony. Such testimony could constrain claims about actual experience without being a condition of establishing the conceptual distinction. Finally, the open question about institutions reducing danger enough to challenge necessary risk should distinguish reduced practical significance from eliminating or defeating the premise of unavoidable residual vulnerability. Note 41 permits the former. These qualifications leave the revised reading's main improvements intact.
