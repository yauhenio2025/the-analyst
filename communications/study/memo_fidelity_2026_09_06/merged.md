# Reading

## Position map

The citing memo, **“Capitalism is a comparative problem, not a universal sequence”** (2026), synthesizes four studies by Hinnerk Bruhns into one position: Weber’s concepts serve a comparative history of distinct capitalist configurations rather than a universal developmental sequence. Its distinctive role is synthetic, joining methodological, historical, and institutional claims that the four studies distribute unevenly. Its qualification is source-specific: most pairings hold, but one citation asks a focused review essay to support more institutional breadth than it contains [F108].

**S1, “Max Weber’s Analysis of Capitalism”** (online 2019; print-publication metadata 2020), distinguishes capitalism as a widespread phenomenon from modern occidental rational enterprise capitalism. It supplies the most compact treatment of capital accounting, formally free labour, political and market orientations, legal calculability, and the state. Its qualification is that these variants do not form a finished classificatory system: Bruhns writes that Weber “never forced the many variants of capitalism into a classificatory schema” [F101].

**S2, “Weber’s Historical Studies on Economic Forms and Institutions”** (2024), connects the ancient economy, medieval city, and China as cases within theory-led comparative investigation. It provides the broadest support for the memo’s claims about ideal types, non-linear development, ancient divergence, urban institutions, and the conditions of modern capitalism. Its methodological qualification is that comparison works through ideal types [F102], which order evidence and guide hypothesis formation rather than themselves constituting a complete historical explanation [F112].

**S3, “Perspectives micro et macro sur l’économie antique”** (2008), is a critical review of scholarship on Polanyi, Weber, Finley, and ancient economies. Its distinctive addition is a historiographical correction: the same analytical logic applies to ancient and modern societies [F103], so *Haushalten* and *Erwerben* must be identified within each particular economic system. Its scope is narrower than that of the other studies; it does not independently reconstruct the full role of law, religion, urban associations, and coercion claimed in the memo’s opening synthesis [F108].

**S4, “Max Weber’s ‘Basic Concepts’ in the Context of his Studies in Economic History”** (2006), argues that Weber’s economic-historical research and sociological concept formation belong to a continuous undertaking. It expressly rejects the division between an early empirical-historical Weber and a later sociological Weber [F104]. Its qualification is that conceptual typification is not the final explanation of historical development: substantive economic circumstances must still supply explanatory content.

## Paired citation audit

### The comparative programme and the specificity of modern capitalism

Statement 1’s central comparison holds across S1, S2, and S4. S1 characterizes Weber’s work as an “inventory of differences” spanning ancient economy, the medieval city, China, and India, fairly supporting the memo’s contrast between occidental free-labour enterprise capitalism and other capitalist forms [F105]. S2 states most directly that the historical cases are organized around factors promoting or inhibiting particular economic developments, accurately supporting the claim of a continuous comparative programme [F106]. S4 independently supports continuity by emphasizing the “permanence and forcefulness” of economic-historical problems throughout Weber’s work [F107].

The S3 citation in the same statement requires narrowing. S3 supports the proposition that economic stages should be read as ideal types rather than chronology, but it does not support the whole accompanying enumeration of law, religion, urban associations, and coercion. The attribution is therefore selective, not false [F108].

Statement 2 accurately represents S1 and S2. S1 defines capitalism in terms of continuous rational business and distinguishes modern capitalism through accounting, market exchange, and formally free labour [F109]. S2 likewise identifies Weber’s object as “the modern rationally organized enterprise based on formally free labour” [F110]. S4 supports the broader differentiation between historical capitalist systems, but the memo’s full institutional bundle is more detailed than the supporting passage; that pair is fair rather than exact in every component [F111].

### Ideal types, historical inquiry, and stages

Statement 3 is accurate against all three cited works. S2 explains that ideal types order and weight historical material around a research problem and assist the formation of hypotheses; it also recounts Weber’s objection to Bücher’s linear sequence [F112]. S3 directly rejects the attribution to Weber of an oikos stage encompassing antiquity, calling that reading a misunderstanding [F113]. S4 states the methodological point directly: Weber construed Bücher’s stages as ideal types rather than “a determinate chronological sequence” [F14, unverified].

Statement 4 also holds. S2 opposes the older procedure of collecting facts before introducing theory and instead presents empirical inquiry, conceptual ordering, and hypothesis formation as interdependent [F115]. S4 supplies the continuity claim by rejecting a periodization that separates historical-empirical youth from theoretical-sociological maturity [F116]. The relation is not an identity of history and theory: historical material provides the objects and explanatory problems, while concepts and types organize inquiry.

Together, S2 and S4 establish continuity without establishing a chronological development between the two Bruhns publications. S4 foregrounds the history–theory relation; S2 reargues that relation through antiquity, the medieval city, and China. Publication dates show when these articles appeared, not an ordered transformation of Bruhns’s position.

### Ancient capitalism without an immanent modernity

Statement 5 is strongly supported. S1 provides the detailed inventory: restricted market production, unstable capital formation, limited calculation under slavery, precarious entrepreneurship, and political orientation distinguish ancient capitalism from modern enterprise capitalism [F117]. S2 states the decisive anti-teleological proposition directly: from Roman capitalism “there was no path, no immanent development leading to modern capitalism” [F118]. S4 independently says that ancient economy and ancient capitalism tend to diverge from, rather than converge upon, modern capitalism’s structural features [F119].

The continuity across these sources is argumentative rather than merely lexical. S1 differentiates forms through economic and institutional features; S2 converts that inventory into an explicit denial of developmental prehistory; S4 situates divergence within an argument against genetic stage classifications. None reduces ancient capitalism to non-capitalism, but none treats it as an incomplete modernity.

### One analytical logic across different systems

Statement 6 distributes its components across four sources. S1 clearly distinguishes market-oriented from politically oriented capitalism, although its full typology is not identical to the memo’s four-term constellation; the pairing is fair [F120]. S2 derives *homo politicus* and *homo oeconomicus* from different political and institutional situations, accurately supporting the claim that common analytical categories disclose differently organized systems [F121]. S3 supplies the most direct statement of common analytical logic: *Haushalten* and *Erwerben* must be identified within each particular system rather than assigned to separate civilizational essences [F122]. S4 reconstructs the ancient and medieval figures as an ideal-typical contrast generated by different corporate, political, and military conditions [F123].

The claim is therefore not that ancient and modern economies function identically. What persists is the applicability of common analytical distinctions; what changes is the historically specific combination of conduct, institutions, opportunities, and political structures. S3 adds a distinct historiographical route because it directly addresses primitivist and Polanyian interpretations, whereas S1, S2, and S4 primarily reconstruct Weber’s categories and comparisons.

### Political institutions and the medieval city

Statement 7 accurately captures S1’s threefold account of the state and capitalism: legal certainty can make calculation possible, bureaucratization can threaten capitalist organization, and state resources can serve particular capitalist interests [F124]. S2 likewise makes relations among political organizations, administration, and economy central, extending the analysis to urban law and associations [F125]. S4 supports the general proposition that regulatory corporate groups shape gainful transactions, but it does not carry every specific consequence compressed into the memo; its attribution is fair [F126].

Statement 8 is accurate against both cited works. S2 distinguishes the northern inland city from the southern commercial city and identifies political autonomy, city law, autocephaly, taxation, market regulation, and bourgeois association as relevant mechanisms. Yet it also says that medieval urban development was not the only precursor or carrier of modern capitalism [F127]. S4 supplies the same causal qualification: medieval urban development was critical but neither the sole preliminary stage nor the foundation of modern capitalism [F128].

The city argument therefore revises a birthplace narrative without replacing it with urban monocausality. The northern inland city matters as an institutional configuration that redirected acquisition toward rational economic means. It is critical within a larger comparison, not a sufficient cause standing alone.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the supplied collection, the memo’s governing proposition holds: capitalism is treated as a comparative problem involving historically variable systems and orientations, not as a universal progression from household through city to national economy. S1 differentiates capitalist forms; S2 integrates the principal historical cases; S3 contests claims of an alien ancient economic logic; and S4 explains why ideal-typical categories must not be mistaken for chronological stages.

The position is continuous but not uniform. S4’s route runs through the relation of economic history to conceptual sociology. S3 proceeds independently through criticism of primitivist and embeddedness-based historiography. S1 emphasizes capitalist types and contemporary institutional conditions. S2 offers the fullest integrated reconstruction. These are complementary argumentative routes, not evidence of a securely dated evolution among the texts.

Within the checked sample, all twenty-four eligible pairs were assessed and settled: eighteen are accurate, five fair, and one selective. None is stretched, misattributed, or unverifiable [F129]. The sole source-overextension is st1/S3. This conclusion applies only to the eight supplied memo statements and four held whole-text windows, not to Bruhns’s complete work, Weber’s entire reception, or a larger citation corpus.

## Read-first route and unsettled question

Read **S2 first** for the integrated comparison of antiquity, the medieval city, and China; **S4 second** for the methodological relation between economic history and basic concepts; **S1 third** for capitalist forms and institutional calculability; and **S3 last** for the focused intervention against primitivist and embeddedness-based readings.

The central unsettled question is how particular histories should assign relative causal weight to labour organization, legal calculability, political structure, urban association, religion, and technology. The supplied works formulate that comparative problem more securely than they resolve its weighting.

## What the comparison establishes

The collection’s strongest continuity is its rejection of a universal sequence of economic stages. S4 says that elements drawn from analytically differentiated stages can combine into a concrete historical whole [F209]. S2 makes the corresponding methodological claim directly: stage theory is not “a model of successive economic forms over time” but an ideal-typical instrument for analysing empirical systems [F207]. S3 carries this criticism into a dispute over Weber’s ancient economy by calling Nafissi’s placement of Weber among the primitivists a misunderstanding [F208].

These are mutually supporting but independently argued routes. S4 reconstructs the methodological relation between economic history and sociological concepts; S3 intervenes in ancient-economy historiography; S2 integrates the issue into comparisons of antiquity, medieval cities, and China. Publication dates establish that S4 appeared in 2006, S3 in 2008, and S2 in 2024, but the supplied passages do not establish that S3 inherited this argument from S4 or that S2 subsequently revised S3. The memo’s “from stages to ideal-typical combinations” is therefore substantively accurate but developmentally stronger than the evidence permits.

The same qualification applies to the corpus’s treatment of capitalism. S1 explicitly presents capitalism as a universal phenomenon while distinguishing modern rational enterprise capitalism from politically oriented forms [F210]. S2 independently describes ancient capitalism as genuinely capitalist but “not designed as a prehistory of capitalism” [F211]. S3 supplies a methodological complement rather than the whole differentiated-capitalism thesis: “la même logique d’analyse” applies to ancient and modern societies, requiring *Haushalten* and *Erwerben* to be identified within each particular system [F212].

The memo’s synthesis is consequently fair. It does not reduce ancient capitalism to an immature modernity, and it does not infer a universal path from political acquisition to rational enterprise. But its phrase “preserving capitalism’s universality” is supported most directly by S1 and S2. S3 supports the common analytical framework, not independently the entire claim about capitalism’s universal distribution.

## Cities as configurations, not single origins

The medieval-city argument rejects a simple birthplace narrative. S4 says medieval urban development was neither “the sole crucial preliminary stage” nor the foundation of modern capitalism, although it remained critical [F216]. S2 supplies the more differentiated institutional account: political autonomy, autonomous city law, taxation, association, and market regulation characterize the relevant city type, while the northern inland city is distinguished from the southern commercial city [F215].

The difference is one of argumentative granularity. S4 establishes that the city was important without being sufficient or singular; S2 identifies the city’s institutional components and the geographical contrast between city types. The memo’s statement that “S2 narrows” S4 is acceptable if “narrows” means greater specification. Nothing in the held passages demonstrates that S2 corrects or retracts the earlier article.

Nor do these sources establish institutional sufficiency. They identify a configuration that promoted rational acquisition and institutions absent in comparison cases. They do not show that autonomy, law, associations, taxation, and regulation would jointly produce modern capitalism whenever present. The corpus therefore replaces a singular origin with a comparative configuration, not with a new deterministic formula.

## China and the limits of material preconditions

China is the clearest test of the configurational argument. S1 reports that the conventional indicators invoked by economic historians—population, precious metals, money, trade, and acquisitive propensity—were in some respects more pronounced in China than in Europe [F201]. Yet it also states that China had no middle-class industrial capitalism capable of developing from small-capitalist beginnings [F217]. The comparison therefore directly supports the memo’s claim that material endowments did not by themselves generate modern rational enterprise capitalism.

S2 reinforces the negative comparison and specifies its urban-political side. It identifies the absence of the occidental city’s special political character [F202] and later notes that the Chinese city was not a corporate association with legal personality and had no city economic policy or system of guild privileges [F218]. Across the held China discussion, the chapter connects these absences to city law, bourgeois communal organization, enterprise law, and calculable administration.

The memo accurately moves from material indicators to institutional comparison. Its heading “institutional sufficiency,” however, should be read as shorthand for the insufficiency of material conditions and the relevance of institutions—not as proof that the listed institutions were themselves a sufficient causal set.

## Religion within reciprocal and multifactor explanation

S1 gives the strongest direct support for the treatment of religion. Its account culminates in “a whole bundle of factors,” with religion “essential but only one of many” [F203]. Religion contributes to rationalizing conduct but does not independently create capitalism.

S2 supports that interpretation from a different angle. It emphasizes that the *Protestant Ethic* pursued only “one side of the causal relationship,” while the later comparative studies considered the reverse relation and the economic conditions of occidental rationalism [F204]. This directly resists a monocausal reading, although the memo’s complete list of economic, legal, political, social, and technological conditions is assembled from the chapter more broadly rather than from that single passage.

S4 clearly rejects the familiar idealist interpretation of capitalism as the incarnation of Protestant mentality [F205]. Yet religion is not that article’s principal subject. Its main concern is the relation between economic history, theory, and sociological concept formation. The memo therefore uses S4 selectively but not falsely: S4 supports rejection of the idealist interpretation, while S1 and S2 carry the fuller multifactor account.

## Ancient-economy historiography as an independent route

S3 contributes a distinct historiographical argument. It reviews claims that reciprocity and redistribution could coexist, that redistribution may conceal extortion, and that isolated mechanisms must be situated in economic systems. Because these are often attributed to Renger, Testart, Nafissi, and other scholars before Bruhns evaluates them, the review’s voice chains must be preserved [F219].

Bruhns’s own concluding question asks whether a “disembedded economy” is any less nebulous or more operational than an “embedded economy,” and urges a different combination of classical rereading with recent archaeological, historical, and anthropological research [F206]. The memo’s statement 11 is accurate because it preserves the empirical-revision portion of this intervention. Its language of “systems, conduct, extortion, and institutions” synthesizes several portions of S3 rather than reproducing one uninterrupted assertion by Bruhns.

S3’s criticism of Nafissi is more direct. Bruhns says it is a misunderstanding to place Weber in the primitivist camp on the ground that antiquity occupies the oikos stage [F208]. He also insists that *Haushalten* and *Erwerben* provide a common analytical distinction for ancient and modern systems [F212]. This does not erase historical differences; it makes them objects of analysis within a shared conceptual framework.

## Economic history and concept formation

S4 and S2 converge on the relation between empirical inquiry and sociological concepts. S4 rejects the division of Weber’s work into an early historical-empirical phase and a later theoretical-sociological phase and derives conceptual systematization from substantive investigations [F214]. Concepts are therefore neither imposed after historical research nor substitutes for it.

S2 describes their operation more exactly. Historical facts are “ordered and weighted in the light of a particular research question or problem” [F213]. Ideal types guide hypothesis formation, but they are not themselves hypotheses and do not constitute substantive causal explanations.

The memo’s statement 14 is accordingly accurate. S4 establishes continuity between economic history and category formation; S2 clarifies the heuristic function of ideal types. Because both works reargue this relation, the evidence supports continuity and complementary emphasis. It does not establish a chronological progression in which S2 completes an unfinished methodological development begun by S4.

## Judgment across the collection

The memo’s central position holds: capitalism appears across the collection as a comparative problem involving historically divergent forms, not as a universal sequence culminating automatically in modern enterprise capitalism. The sources converge on three propositions: economic forms cannot be read as fixed chronological stages; modern capitalism must be distinguished from other genuine capitalisms; and causal explanation requires historically specific combinations of economic and extra-economic conditions.

The main limitation lies not in factual fidelity but in developmental phrasing. The sources repeatedly or independently argue related positions, and later works sometimes provide greater integration or detail. They do not, in the supplied passages, establish direct lines of inheritance, correction, or conceptual succession among Bruhns’s articles. Likewise, the collection identifies relevant configurations but does not determine the relative causal weight of religion, law, urban autonomy, political organization, technology, labour, or material endowments.

## Position map

The supplied collection contains five substantive documents and one metadata index.

The 2026 memo, **“Capitalism is a comparative problem, not a universal sequence”** (em:U3HITB25), synthesizes four studies by Hinnerk Bruhns. Its distinctive role is to formulate the eight attributions under audit: religion as one condition among many, ideal types against universal stages, a common analytical logic across ancient and modern economies, and criticism of embedded/disembedded binaries. Its qualification is evidentiary: it is the citing text, not an independent witness to the positions it attributes.

**“Max Weber’s Analysis of Capitalism”** (S1, em:RJRLLVLQ) differentiates modern rational enterprise capitalism from politically oriented and other capitalisms. For the indexed attribution, its decisive contribution is the claim that religion is “essential but only one of many” factors in the genesis of modern capitalism [F301]. The chapter therefore supports causal plurality rather than the exclusion of religion.

**“Weber’s Historical Studies on Economic Forms and Institutions”** (S2, em:YASGM27U) joins Weber’s studies of antiquity, the medieval city, China, and religion within theory-led comparative economic history. For the checked claims, it qualifies religious causation [F302], rejects a universally linear sequence [F307], and describes Bücher’s regression to a sequence running from household to town and national economy [F310]. Its important qualification is methodological: ideal types organize inquiry rather than themselves supplying a complete historical explanation.

**“Perspectives micro et macro sur l’économie antique”** (S3, em:WGY2P4N5) is a critical review of work on Polanyi, Weber, Finley, and Nafissi. Its distinctive role is historiographical: it challenges fundamental ancient alterity [F5–F6], reports Nafissi’s Athens thesis [F311], and questions the operational value of “disembedded economy” [F312]. Several propositions in the article are nested reports of contributors to the reviewed works, so the memo’s synthetic wording must not be mistaken for Bruhns’s uninterrupted statement [F304].

**“Max Weber’s ‘Basic Concepts’ in the Context of his Studies in Economic History”** (S4, em:NQZYBP6Y) connects Weber’s basic concepts to continuing economic-historical investigation. Within this audit it rejects an idealist interpretation of capitalism [F303] and most clearly supports the claim that elements abstracted into distinct stages may combine in concrete historical configurations [F309]. It does not, however, name Rodbertus’s followers in the checked discussion of genetic classification [F314].

## From religious cause to comparative conditions

The memo’s first movement is supported across S1, S2, and S4, but the sources perform different argumentative tasks.

S1 supplies the positive multifactorial formulation. Against the memo’s statement that religious ethics enter the rationalization of conduct while monocausal explanation is subordinated, Bruhns writes that Weber’s account ends with “a whole bundle of factors” in which religion is “essential but only one of many” [F301]. The object is the genesis of modern capitalism; the scope is causal explanation; the passage’s inferential role is to retain religion while denying its sufficiency.

S2 reaches the same qualification through the scope of *The Protestant Ethic*. It says that Weber pursued only “one side of the causal relationship,” namely the effect of religious beliefs on economic mentality or ethos [F302]. Here the object is a particular study rather than the entire causal account. The passage shows that the religious inquiry was deliberately partial and was later placed beside inquiry into economic conditions.

S4 takes the negative route. Bruhns calls the univocal idealist interpretation “entirely mistaken” [F303]. This supports the memo’s rejection of capitalism as merely an expression of Protestant mentality, but does not make religion causally irrelevant.

The three works therefore converge on qualified religious causation: S1 states the bundle, S2 restricts the scope of the religious study, and S4 rejects reduction to mentality. This is continuity among the supplied publications, not demonstrated development in Bruhns’s position. Their publication dates—2006, 2019, and 2024—do not by themselves establish that one formulation revises another.

## From modes and binaries to economic systems

S3 provides an independent route through ancient-economic historiography. The memo’s statement 18 combines several reported propositions: coexistence of reciprocity and redistribution, extortion concealed by redistribution, uncertainty about embeddedness, and a redirection toward systems. The checked anchor directly supports only the final move: Bruhns asks how inquiry can pass from partial elements or “different mechanisms” to an understanding of precapitalist economic systems [F304]. Because the earlier components belong to different contributors discussed in the review, the attribution is fair as synthesis but compressed in voice and distribution.

The more direct methodological claim appears in statement 19. Bruhns writes that “la même logique d’analyse s’applique aux sociétés anciennes et modernes” and requires analysis within each particular economic system [F305]. The objects are ancient and modern societies; the scope is analytical rather than a claim that their economies are substantively identical. Its inferential role is to reject the idea that antiquity requires a fundamentally alien economic logic while preserving historically specific configurations.

Statement 20 is correspondingly accurate. S3 favorably presents Bresson’s challenge to “la doctrine de l’altérité fondamentale de l’économie antique” and his criticism of schematic embeddedness concepts [F306]. This does not establish that every Polanyian analysis is rejected. It supports the narrower claim that fundamental alterity and schematic application of embeddedness are inadequate substitutes for analysis.

## Ideal types against universal stages

The anti-stage argument is distributed unevenly across S2, S3, and S4.

S2 explicitly rejects a universal linear succession. It says Weber did not employ stage theory as a model of successive forms over time, and the surrounding discussion explains that an oikos economy could appear at the end rather than the beginning of a development [F307]. S2 also gives the clearest formulation of the position being rejected: Bücher had returned to “a sequence of stages that all peoples go through in the same direction,” from household through town to national economy [F310].

S3 supports a narrower historical correction. Bruhns reports that Weber defended Bücher by explaining that the *Wirtschaftsstufen* should be understood as *Idealtypen* [F308]. This supports the ideal-type component of the memo’s statement, but S3 does not independently carry the full “combinable analytical instruments” formulation in the checked passage.

S4 most directly supplies that combinatory predicate. It states that elements taken from conceptually differentiated stages “often combined into one distinct, specifically-formed concrete whole” [F309]. Thus the forms are analytical differentiations whose elements may recur in different combinations, not obligatory epochs through which every society passes.

The three-source attribution in statement 21 is consequently sound if read distributively: S2 rejects universal chronological succession, S3 supplies the ideal-type correction, and S4 gives the strongest account of recombination. The claim is repeated and reargued across the works; the supplied passages do not establish that a later work merely presupposes an earlier one.

Statement 22 requires a sharper source distinction. S2 supports the attack on Bücher’s linear model and discusses Rodbertus-linked treatment elsewhere in the held window [F310]. S4 supports the opposition between ideal-typical construction and genetic classification, but it does not name Rodbertus’s followers [F314]. Applied to S4 alone, the memo’s collective association is therefore selective rather than false: its anti-genetic conclusion holds, while one named lineage comes from another source.

## Athens and “disembedded economy”

The two Athens statements preserve the necessary nested attribution. S3 reports Nafissi’s proposition that Athens was simultaneously the birthplace of political and economic modernity [F311]. This is Nafissi’s thesis as represented by Bruhns, not Bruhns’s own conclusion.

Bruhns’s response is separately supported. He asks whether “économie désencastrée,” applied to Athens, is really less nebulous or more operational than “économie encastrée” [F312]. Elsewhere in the article, he calls the placement of Weber among the primitivists a misunderstanding [F313]. Statement 24 combines these two criticisms fairly, but they occur in noncontiguous passages and should not be rendered as one continuous quotation.

The conceptual objection does not establish that S3 rejects every inquiry into economic embeddedness. Its force is methodological: reversing the label from embedded to disembedded does not become explanatory unless it clarifies institutions, conduct, allocation, and the historically specific economic system.

## The medieval city: a specified configuration, not a singular birthplace

Statement 25’s concrete mechanism holds against S2. The memo attributes special consequence to the northern inland city because its political and institutional configuration oriented burghers toward rational acquisition. S2 explains that, because the highest military force did not reside in these cities, bourgeois acquisition depended on “the development of rational economic means” [F401]. The verdict is fair rather than strictly accurate because “especially consequential” compresses an ideal-typical contrast into something resembling a measured ranking.

Statement 26 preserves the resulting qualification. S2 explicitly displaces the conventional southern-European birthplace thesis: the medieval city played an essential role, but the relevant type was “the industrial inland city north of the Alps” rather than the southern commercial city [F402]. Its object is therefore not every medieval city but a politically and institutionally specified configuration.

S4 reaches the anti-monocausal conclusion at a more general level. It calls medieval urban development a critical factor while denying that it was either the sole preliminary stage or the foundation of modern capitalism [F403]. S4 does not foreground the southern commercial city at this locus, so the complete compound attribution is fair rather than exact against S4 alone.

Across S2 and S4, the city’s importance persists, but the inferential roles differ. S2 identifies the northern inland mechanism; S4 limits any attempt to turn medieval urban development into an exclusive or sufficient cause [F402] [F403].

## History and economic theory: neither fact accumulation nor detached abstraction

Statement 27 accurately captures S2’s procedure. Historical material is not theoretically neutral: it is “ordered and weighted” in light of a particular research question [F404]. Ideal types consequently guide inquiry rather than reproduce empirical reality. The historical evidence supplies the material, while conceptual construction determines how it is selected and compared.

S4 supports the conjunction through disciplinary reconstruction. Its quotation from the Sombart–Weber declaration assigns “the rational construction of concepts, typologies and systems” a central place in economics [F405]. This attribution is fair because S4 develops the complementary role of historical substance across its methodological discussion rather than in a single sentence identical to the memo’s formulation.

Statement 28 identifies the alternatives being rejected. S2 opposes remaining with “the mere collection of facts” and also denies that economics can be an abstract, purely theoretical science [F406]. S4 rejects the entrenched alternative between “‘historical’ or ‘theoretical’ economics” [F407]. These works therefore offer independent arguments for a conjunction of theory and history: S2 demonstrates its operation in historical comparison, while S4 reconstructs the disciplinary dispute in which the conjunction was formulated.

No development in Weber’s thought can be inferred merely from S4’s 2006 publication preceding S2’s 2024 publication. Those are dates of Bruhns’s texts, not dates of Weber’s underlying works. The supplied collection establishes continuity in Bruhns’s reconstruction, with S2 providing the more case-specific account.

## The comparative object: modern capitalism among capitalisms

Statement 29 holds against all three cited works, though each defines the object differently. S1 asks directly what constitutes modern capitalism, how it differs from other forms, and which factors explain its origins [F408]. Its wider typology includes politically oriented, speculative, agrarian, colonial, and other capitalisms.

S2 gives the explanandum a narrower formulation: “the capitalism of the modern rationally organized enterprise based on formally free labour” [F409]. The existence of other capitalist forms therefore does not indicate incomplete progress along one sequence. It creates the comparative problem of why this particular configuration emerged under some conditions and not others.

S4 reaches the same problem through the concept of an economic system and Weber’s studies of antiquity in relation to modern capitalism’s origins [F410]. Its support is fair because the memo’s complete formulation combines propositions distributed across S4’s discussion.

Taken together, the three works distinguish capitalism as a historically variable field from modern rational enterprise capitalism as a particular object of causal explanation [F408] [F409] [F410].

## Ancient capitalism without immanent modernity

Statement 30 is one of the strongest attributions in the sample. S1 says that ancient political and social structures prevented a highly developed capitalist economy from developing toward modern middle-class enterprise capitalism [F411]. The source thus preserves both elements of the memo’s formulation: antiquity could have a capitalist economy, but modern capitalism was not its intrinsic destination.

S2 states the discontinuity categorically: from the capitalism of the late Roman Republic and early Empire, “there was no path, no immanent development leading to modern capitalism” [F412]. Its immediate object is that defined Roman configuration rather than every period or place conventionally grouped under antiquity.

S4 formulates the same relation as divergence: ancient economy and ancient capitalism tend not to converge on, but to diverge from, modern capitalism’s structural features [F413]. “Genuine capitalism” therefore establishes a valid classification without collapsing ancient and modern systems into one form or arranging them as stages.

S3, outside the indexed citation sample, adds an independent methodological point: Bruhns states that the same analytical logic applies to ancient and modern societies [F419]. This does not retroactively support any pair in which S3 was not cited. It instead shows an independent route beyond an absolute opposition between ancient and modern economic rationality.

S3 also challenges Nafissi’s description of Athens as a “disembedded economy.” It asks whether that concept is any less nebulous or more operative than “embedded economy” [F422]. The addition redirects analysis away from assigning an entire society to one categorical logic and toward more discriminating historical inquiry.

## Ideal types against chronological stages

Statement 31 accurately represents both S2 and S4. S2 says Weber did not use stage theory as a model of successive economic forms over time but as an ideal-type model for analysing empirical systems [F414]. Its treatment of the oikos is decisive: a form assigned by linear theory to the beginning of development may instead appear at its end.

S4 likewise reports that Weber defended Bücher’s stages as ideal types “not in the sense of a determinate chronological sequence” [F15, unverified]. Across the two documents, the persistent prohibition is against turning analytical constructions into empirical epochs or universal succession. S2 reargues this prohibition through historical cases rather than merely presupposing S4’s earlier publication.

The same distinction limits the city thesis. The northern inland city is an ideal-typical configuration used to explain an orientation toward rational acquisition. It is neither a universal stage through which all economies must pass nor the sufficient foundation of modern capitalism [F401] [F402] [F403].

## Economic history and sociological concept formation

Statement 32 is accurate against S4. S4 calls economic history a foundation underpinning Weber’s entire body of writing and rejects a division between an early historical-empirical phase and a later sociological phase [F417]. Its scope is Weber’s work as reconstructed in that article, not every possible interpretation of Weber’s complete corpus.

S2 supports the statement more indirectly. It says that the results of Weber’s historical investigations were incorporated into the “Basic Sociological Categories of Economic Action” [F416]. This establishes movement from substantive investigation into conceptual systematization. Because “foundational throughout” is more explicitly S4’s formulation, the S2 verdict is fair.

Continuity must not be converted into immobility. S4 also identifies “a process of development” in the conception of economic-historical questions [F421]. What persists is economic history’s foundational and empirical role; what changes is its integration into broader cultural, sociological, and conceptual problems. The supplied texts therefore reject a clean rupture between history and sociology without denying argumentative development within Weber’s work.

## Judgment across the collection

Within the checked sample, the memo’s central position holds: modern rational enterprise capitalism is treated as a comparative problem, not as the endpoint of a universal sequence. The principal continuities are the differentiation of capitalist forms, denial of an immanent ancient-to-modern path, use of ideal types against chronological stages, and conjunction of economic history with concept formation [F412] [F414] [F417].

The collection also imposes three limits. First, cross-period comparison need not assume fundamentally different analytical logics [F419], but neither does it erase historically specific configurations. Second, the medieval-city thesis concerns a defined northern inland type and remains anti-monocausal [F401] [F402] [F403]. Third, continuity between history and sociology permits development in the formulation of economic-historical problems [F421].

For the 17 checked pairs, ten verdicts are accurate and seven fair. This conclusion applies only to statements 25–32 and the three works they cite, not to the memo’s complete citation practice, Bruhns’s complete work, or Weber’s wider reception.

Read **S2 first** for the substantive movement from antiquity through the medieval city to China. # Citation-fidelity reading

## Position map

The supplied collection comprises one citing memo and four cited studies by Hinnerk Bruhns.

The 2026 memo, **“Capitalism is a comparative problem, not a universal sequence”** (`em:U3HITB25`), consolidates the studies into three settled propositions and five open questions. Its distinctive role is synthetic: it translates historical and methodological arguments into a comparative research program. The main qualification is that some “still open” questions are the memo’s forward extrapolations rather than questions explicitly posed by the cited studies [F12–F14].

Bruhns’s **“Max Weber’s Analysis of Capitalism”** (`em:RJRLLVLQ`, online 2019; print 2020) distinguishes modern rational enterprise capitalism from other capitalist forms and explains its emergence through multiple conditions. Religion is “essential but only one of many” [F501], and material endowments require political, legal, technological, and cultural support [F504]. Its important qualification is contemporary: Weber’s concepts remain useful only if they are used flexibly and reconstructed where necessary [F507].

**“Weber’s Historical Studies on Economic Forms and Institutions”** (`em:YASGM27U`, 2024) connects Weber’s studies of antiquity, medieval cities, and China as theory-led comparisons. Its distinctive contribution is methodological: historical evidence is ordered and weighted in relation to particular questions [F511]. Its city ideal types consequently remain question-dependent instruments; the chapter does not demonstrate their reach beyond its ancient, medieval European, and Chinese comparisons [F512].

**“Perspectives micro et macro sur l’économie antique”** (`em:WGY2P4N5`, 2008) reviews disputes concerning Polanyi, Weber, Finley, and Nafissi. It challenges the operational sufficiency of embedded/disembedded economy [F506] and redirects attention from isolated mechanisms toward particular economic systems [F8–F9]. Its important qualification is programmatic: it identifies the problem of integrating partial evidence and system-level explanation rather than completing that integration.

Finally, **“Max Weber’s ‘Basic Concepts’ in the Context of his Studies in Economic History”** (`em:NQZYBP6Y`, 2006) places Weber’s concepts within his continuing economic-historical investigations. For the indexed citations, its relevant roles are to reject a mentality-only explanation of capitalism [F503] and to identify historically bounded city ideal types as comparative instruments [F513]. It does not itself test those instruments against additional urban cases.

The citation index supplies routing, pair identifiers, and retrieval metadata; it is not an independent substantive source. All four cited works were held and assessed. No document was unassessable, no work fell outside the indexed sample, and no unchecked or linked-name windows were reported.

## What the settled attributions establish

The memo’s three settled propositions hold against their cited works.

Statement 33 attributes a multifactorial explanation of modern capitalism to S1, S2, and S4. S1 provides the strongest direct support: Bruhns writes that Weber’s account ends with “a whole bundle of factors,” religion being “essential but only one of many” [F501]. The attribution is therefore **accurate** for S1.

S2 supports the same conclusion by restricting *The Protestant Ethic* to “one side of the causal relationship,” namely religious influence on economic mentality [F502]. Because this passage establishes the partial scope of that study rather than reproducing the memo’s “one among many” formula, the attribution is **fair** rather than exact.

S4 approaches the point polemically. It rejects as mistaken an interpretation that makes capitalism the incarnation of a particular mentality [F503]. That supports the memo’s denial of religious sufficiency, although it does not independently enumerate religion among a bundle of causes. The S4 attribution is therefore also **fair**.

Statement 34 condenses the Chinese comparison. S1 expressly juxtaposes conventional material explanations—population, precious metals, money, and trade—with additional prerequisites including a predictable state, technology, science, and rational conduct [F504]. The memo’s claim that material-commercial factors are insufficient without supporting configurations is **accurate** for S1.

S2 reaches a compatible conclusion through urban institutions. It reports that the Chinese city could not conduct city economic policy and lacked the legal foundations of a freely regulated commercial constitution [F505]. This supports the political, legal, and associational parts of the memo’s formulation. Because “cultural configurations” is broader than the immediate supporting passage, the attribution to S2 is **fair**.

Statement 35 is a particularly close match. S3 asks whether “disembedded economy,” as applied to Athens, is any less nebulous or more operative than “embedded economy” [F506]. The memo correctly converts this rhetorical question into the proposition that neither category alone supplies an operative analysis. The attribution is **accurate**. It should not, however, be inflated into a claim that S3 denies every heuristic use of Polanyian categories.

## How the open questions relate to their sources

The memo’s open questions vary between direct reformulations and constructive extensions.

Statement 36 follows S1 closely. S1 says Weber’s concepts may remain useful today if they are “used flexibly and possibly altered and reconstructed” [F507]. Turning this recommendation into a question about reconstruction and empirical supplementation for contemporary capitalism is **accurate**.

Statement 37 converts S3’s movement from partial mechanisms to economic systems into a problem of integrating micro- and macro-historical scales [F508]. The source explicitly identifies the difficulty of moving from partial descriptions to an understanding of precapitalist systems. The added phrase “without reducing one scale to the other” belongs to the memo’s formulation, so the attribution is **fair**, not literal.

Statement 38 is likewise a synthesis. S3 says that Weber’s distinction between *Haushalten* and *Erwerben* must be determined within each particular economic system, while its surrounding discussion treats reciprocity, redistribution, exchange, coexistence, and extortion [F509]. The six-part combination is constructed by the memo from that discussion rather than stated as a single source-side list. The attribution remains **fair** because its analytical direction and constituent terms are present.

Statement 39 asks how to establish relative causal weights among urban institutions, calculable law, free labour, religion, technology, and political organization. S1 expressly frames Weber’s problem as identifying the factors responsible for modern capitalism’s origins [F510]. S2 explains that evidence is ordered and weighted in light of a research question [F511]. Both attributions are **fair**: the sources support multifactorial, question-led causal inquiry, but neither offers the memo’s exact formula or a completed weighting procedure.

Statement 40 is more selective. S2 says that when the question changes, the construction of the ideal type also changes [F512]. This licenses reconstruction for new comparisons, but the chapter does not itself ask how city ideal types travel beyond ancient, medieval European, and Chinese cases. S4 identifies medieval/ancient, northern/southern, and occidental/oriental city types as instruments for analyzing the development of modern capitalism [F513]. It likewise establishes the existing comparative apparatus without testing additional cases. Both pairings are therefore **selective**: the memo proposes a reasonable next question, but that question is its addition.

## Position across the collection

Within the indexed material, the four studies support the memo’s central comparative orientation through distinct routes. S1 supplies a multifactorial causal account; S2 explains how question-led comparison orders and weights historical evidence; S3 insists that categories and mechanisms be situated within particular economic systems; and S4 links conceptual construction to historically bounded comparisons. These are complementary arguments, not merely instances of shared vocabulary.

The supplied dates—2006, 2008, 2019/2020, and 2024—do not by themselves prove a linear development in Bruhns’s position. The checked passages instead establish continuity in method and divergence in argumentative task. S4 reconstructs the relation between concepts and economic history; S3 applies system-level criticism to ancient-economic historiography; S1 differentiates capitalist forms and causal conditions; S2 integrates several historical comparisons. The later texts sometimes reargue earlier methodological commitments, but the indexed evidence does not establish that every difference is a chronological revision.

The resulting position is consistent across this collection: capitalism should be explained through historically specific combinations of economic conduct, political organization, law, association, culture, and coercion rather than through a universal sequence or a single sufficient cause. The remaining difficulty is methodological and empirical—how to compare configurations without turning ideal types into stages or treating historically bounded contrasts as universally portable explanations.

## How the recommendations relate to the sources

The memo’s central move is to convert source arguments into research instructions. That conversion is strongest where a source already poses essentially the same next step. S3 asks whether rereading foundational texts should be combined with recent archaeological, historical, and anthropological work; the memo changes the question into an imperative without changing its objects or evidentiary scope. The attribution is accurate [F606].

S1 similarly supports the recommendation to reconstruct Weber’s concepts for contemporary capitalism, but the memo combines two neighboring claims: concepts may be used flexibly and altered, while empirical precision remains indispensable. Because the combined instruction is not a single source formulation, the attribution is fair rather than exact [F607]. This qualification should govern the memo’s agenda generally: conceptual portability is conditional on reconstruction and empirical investigation [F602].

The same distinction between source claim and programmatic synthesis governs the institutional recommendations. S2 explicitly rejects an unspecified relation between economy and law in favor of inquiry into urban policy and its determining factors; st43 accurately turns that contrast into a direction for concrete research [F608]. S4 directly grounds st44’s call to reconnect basic categories with economic history because it presents the conceptual system as developed through substantive investigation [F609]. Yet S4 also limits what such reconnection can accomplish: conceptual clarification does not by itself explain historical development [F605].

The memo’s most composite recommendation is st45. S2 supports comparison through the interaction of economic forms and political structures but does not independently state the memo’s five-part list of acquisition, provisioning, coercion, regulation, and legal guarantees [F610]. S3 supports movement from multiple mechanisms toward system-level understanding, including attention to redistribution and coercive extraction, but again not the entire formula [F611]. S4 shows that gainful transactions depend on forms of regulation and the groups carrying them, without supplying the exact enumerated bundle [F612]. Each attribution is therefore fair: the three sources support the configurational method, while the memo adds the consolidated vocabulary.

The reading recommendations are more directly grounded. S2 treats Weber’s studies of antiquity, the medieval city, China, religion, and modern capitalism as continuations within one inquiry [F613]. S4 independently groups *The Protestant Ethic*, *Agrarian Relationships in Antiquity*, *The City*, and *The Economic Ethic of World Religions* as a connected complex [F614]. This is continuity rather than demonstrated chronological development: the documents establish an argumentative relationship among the works, not a complete sequence of revisions between their composition dates.

The recommendation concerning *Economy and Society* is unevenly supported across its two citations. S1 establishes that *Economy and Society* was only one part of the larger *Outline of Social Economics*, warranting resistance to a self-sufficient reading; the memo’s exact instruction to place it beside *General Economic History* and all substantive studies is its own specification, so the attribution is fair [F615]. S4 more directly juxtaposes the categories in *Economy and Society* with the conceptual preface to *General Economic History* and traces both to historical inquiry; that attribution is accurate [F616].

The Bücher–Meyer recommendation receives the broadest support. S1 identifies the controversy as an occasion for Weber’s conceptual clarification, although its retrieved locus does not state the ideal-type-versus-chronology distinction as directly as the other sources; its support is fair [F617]. S2 explicitly contrasts Bücher’s linear model with Weber’s expectation that economic stages be handled as ideal types [F618]. S3 says that Weber defended Bücher by treating *Wirtschaftsstufen* as *Idealtypen*, while criticizing the chronological interpretation [F619]. S4 states the decisive point most clearly: Weber construed the stages as ideal types rather than a determinate chronological sequence [F620]. The continuity across S2–S4 is substantive, not merely lexical: each assigns ideal types an analytical rather than universally sequential role.

Its two audited statements recommend reading Polanyi through Bresson, Testart, Renger, and Bruhns and comparing Weber and Sombart across capitalism, economic systems, historical theory, and the spirit of capitalism [F701] [F702]. - **Hinnerk Bruhns, _Max Weber’s Analysis of Capitalism_ (S1; em:RJRLLVLQ)** makes capitalism an organizing problem of Weber’s work and explicitly places Weber in tension with Sombart. - **Hinnerk Bruhns, _Weber’s Historical Studies on Economic Forms and Institutions_ (S2; em:YASGM27U)** reconstructs Weber’s studies of antiquity, the medieval city, and China as theory-led comparisons bearing on modern capitalism. ## Polanyi through differentiated criticism

Statement 49 instructs the reader to approach “Polanyi’s reciprocity, redistribution, exchange, and embeddedness through their critiques by Bresson, Testart, Renger, and Bruhns” [F701]. S3 substantiates that route, although “critiques” must encompass different argumentative operations rather than one collective objection.

Renger revises a sequential Polanyian account by presenting reciprocity and redistribution as forms that “coexistaient et interagissaient.” Bruhns subsequently asks how inquiry can move from descriptions of partial mechanisms toward comprehension of economic systems [F703]. Between those positions, Testart criticizes redistribution for neglecting extortion, while Bresson challenges schematic uses of embeddedness and the asserted fundamental alterity of ancient economy.

The recommendation therefore identifies both the relevant categories and the named participants. Its necessary qualification is internal plurality: Renger revises Polanyi, Testart attacks a category, Bresson challenges a broader methodological opposition, and Bruhns assesses what these interventions permit at the level of systems. Because the statement recommends a route through these criticisms rather than attributing one thesis to all four, **st49/S3 is accurate** [F708].

## Weber and Sombart: one comparison, unequal source breadth

Statement 50 recommends comparing Weber and Sombart on “capitalism, economic systems, historical theory, and the spirit of capitalism” [F702]. The four cited works sustain related but not interchangeable routes. S1 and S2 directly develop the comparison; S4 provides strong methodological common ground with uneven topical emphasis; S3 supplies only one substantial component.

### S1: explicit dispute, agreement, and partial acceptance

S1 makes the Weber–Sombart relation part of its direct account of capitalism. Bruhns says that Weber disputed Sombart “in relation to the ‘spirit’ of capitalism and the role of religion,” agreed with him on many other points, and accepted his developmental-historical approach only partly [F705]. The relation is thus neither straightforward inheritance nor complete opposition.

This directly covers capitalism, historical development, and spirit, while S1’s wider discussion of Weber’s conceptual distinctions supplies the economic-systems axis. The recommendation does not suppress disagreement or attribute a shared doctrine. **The st50/S1 pair is accurate** [F709].

### S2: the broadest integrated route

S2 develops the relationship at greater methodological length. It distinguishes Sombart’s periodization from Weber’s project: Sombart constructs a development from early through high to late capitalism, whereas Weber begins from a narrower definition of modern capitalism [F706]. The chapter also expressly presents _The Protestant Ethic_ as a reaction to Sombart’s _Der moderne Kapitalismus_ [F710].

The contrast is inferentially important. Sombart’s periodization supplies one historical construction; Weber’s comparisons ask why a particular capitalist configuration developed in one setting and not in apparently favorable alternatives. Economic systems and historical theory therefore differentiate the projects rather than merely providing shared vocabulary. Because the chapter also connects their accounts of capitalism to the spirit question, **st50/S2 is accurate** and offers the broadest single route through the four topics [F710].

### S3: economic systems without a four-part comparison

S3’s principal objects are Polanyi, Weber, Finley, Nafissi, ancient economy, and the relation between mechanisms and systems. Its explicit Sombart route identifies him as the originator of the economic-system concept [F704]. That reference supports comparison at the systems level and connects Sombart to the analysis of capitalism, but it does not provide a sustained Weber–Sombart comparison across historical theory and the spirit of capitalism.

Because each citation pair is assessed independently, S1, S2, and S4 cannot compensate for this limitation. S3 remains relevant to the recommendation, but only for a narrower part of it. **The st50/S3 pair is selective** [F711].

### S4: methodological common ground with uneven emphasis

S4 directly gives Weber and Sombart a common commitment to theory understood as “the rational construction of concepts, typologies and systems” [F707]. This shared position responds to the separation of historical and theoretical economics: concepts and systems become instruments of historically grounded inquiry rather than substitutes for it.

S4 consequently supports the historical-theory, systems, and capitalism axes strongly. Its discussion also places their overlapping work around modern capitalism, economy, and religion, but it does not develop a symmetrical comparison of their respective concepts of the spirit of capitalism. **The st50/S4 pair is fair**: the comparison is warranted, while its four topics receive unequal treatment [F712].

## Judgment on the checked position

The Polanyi recommendation is strongest when understood as a deliberately plural route through revision, objection, methodological criticism, and systems-level synthesis. The Weber–Sombart recommendation likewise identifies a legitimate comparative problem, but its omnibus citation conceals substantial differences in source breadth.

Across the five checked pairs, three are accurate, one fair, and one selective. S1, S2, and S4 relate the historical study of capitalism to conceptual construction, but they make the Weber–Sombart relation visible in different ways: explicit disagreement and partial agreement in S1, contrasting historical constructions in S2, and shared methodological commitments in S4. S3 independently reaches the systems question through criticism of Polanyian mechanisms, but it should not be treated as equivalent evidence for every Weber–Sombart topic [F713].

This judgment applies only to statements 49–50 and their five indexed pairs. It does not establish the fidelity of the rest of the memo, Bruhns’s complete scholarship, or the wider reception of Weber, Sombart, Polanyi, and Finley.
