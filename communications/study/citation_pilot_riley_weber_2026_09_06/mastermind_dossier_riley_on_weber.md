# Riley's Weber: Modular Borrowing, Selective Fidelity, Gramscian Frame

*A philological and reception audit of twelve citing acts across two decades, with a verdict table for future users*

06 September 2026 · researcher edition · medium depth · job dossier-bdc5eb48c407

## Summary

Across twelve citing acts from 2003 to 2026, Riley treats Weber as a modular interlocutor rather than a systematic reference point. The early period borrows Weber's differentiation of classes, status groups, and parties to license conceptual space for political society; the middle period puts Weber's patrimonial, charismatic, and legal-rational typologies to direct diagnostic work on Judt's ethics and Trump's rule; the late period progressively subordinates Weber's angle of vision to a Gramscian-Marxian frame, explicitly enclosing Weber's firm-centred standpoint within a Lukácsian synthesis. The through-line is political capitalism and the class/estate distinction, which together carry more citation weight than the five other routes combined, and which are where the most consequential tensions within Riley's corpus arise: his 2018 rejection of estate concepts as a category error for advanced capitalism is not reconciled with his 2025 application of those same concepts to political capitalism's structural reinforcement of estate-type politics.
The fidelity record, checked across twenty-eight indexed citation pairs against five routed Weber witnesses, yields a stable pattern: narrow quotations and declared adaptations are generally accurate or fair. The 2013 imperialism prognosis is verbatim-matched at Economy and Society p. 919; the 2018 patrimonial-office quotation is confirmed at both predicates at pp. 1028–31; the 2024 power definition is word-for-word at p. 53; the 2020 adaptation of Roman imperialist capitalism is announced as adaptation and the cited p. 917 supports the retained mechanism. The gap opens when a local passage is promoted into a system-level argument: the political-society formula extends Weber's differentiation beyond what 'Class, Status, Party' states; the capitalism-suffrage thesis adds a broader affinity proposition the cited parliamentary-function pages do not establish; the broad political-capitalism definition is Weberian in substance but wider than pp. 965–66 support; and the census article's state definition substitutes bureaucratic-infrastructural capacity for Weber's held definition by monopoly of legitimate physical force — the one citation the audit classifies as stretched rather than merely selective. Twelve pairs remain unverifiable at the cited locations, meaning the check cannot settle those attributions in either direction.
Among the supplied independent readers — Wright, Ganev, Ktenas, Bendix, Love, Rundell, and Castoriadis — Riley is analytically consonant on categorical distinctions and revisionist on their explanatory uses. Wright is the closest neighbour on estates and closure but holds, unlike Riley's 2025 thesis, that capitalism systematically erodes the salience of traditional status groups. Ganev's Weberian political capitalism presupposes state weakness and de-bureaucratisation; Riley's mature account requires durable fiscal and monetary agencies that engineer asset-price support — a structural divergence on mechanism despite categorical consonance. Love's philological finding that Weber's use of 'capitalism' was not internally consistent and that tax-farming does not satisfy a market-exchange definition complicates the stable Weberian genealogy Riley and Brenner invoke. On the fact-value problem, Ktenas and Bendix defend Wertfreiheit as a genuine methodological achievement; Riley historicises it as a targeted political move against specific enemies, retaining objective possibilities and the ethic of responsibility while discarding value-pluralism — a narrowing none of the supplied readers explicitly contests but none endorses.

## 1. How Riley uses Weber: six routes, one modular logic

Across the supplied collection Riley never gives Weber a fixed address. In the 2003 Lombardy article he places the argument explicitly in a "Gramscian and Weberian tradition" and uses "Class, Status, Party" to establish that political society is irreducible to civil-society interests or the state. [^1] That is genealogy and permission at once: Weber licenses the conceptual space; Gramsci and the historical data fill it. In 2005 Weber enters only as a parenthetical authority for reconstructing suppressed historical alternatives embedded in historical sequences. [^2] The Weber citation is methodological scaffolding, not load-bearing wall.

Table 1 maps twelve major citing acts from 2003 to 2026 and makes the modular pattern visible: object cited, citation move, stance, and the analytical job Weber is asked to do in each text.

*Early texts (2003–2005) place Weber alongside Gramsci as methodological or genealogical authority; middle texts (2011–2020) apply typologies directly (responsibility, patrimonialism, political capitalism) and mark adaptations explicitly; late texts (2022–2026) either systematise (estate politics, tax-farming genealogy) or contain Weber within a Lukácsian standpoint critique, with the Gramscian-Marxian framework consistently carrying the explanatory core.*

Reading down the Stance column, "Builds on" dominates the early period, giving way to "Adopts" and then to "Builds on and contains" in the late texts — a slow arc from collaboration toward epistemological circumscription.

The middle period — 2011 to 2020 — is where Weber does the most direct argumentative work. In the Judt essay Riley invokes "Politics as a Vocation" to correct Judt's detached moralism: Weber's actual position treats conviction and responsibility as supplements rather than opposites, a reading Riley pairs with Sartre to argue that both thinkers accepted the tension between ethics and politics rather than dissolving it. In the 2018 Trump essay Riley applies Weber's three forms of rule — the charismatic, the patrimonial, and the bureaucratic — to diagnose Trump as a patrimonial misfit inserted into a legal-rational state, a genuinely qualified adoption that refuses to import the category without reservation. [^5] And in 2020 Faultlines Riley marks his most consequential adaptation with a careful hedge: "Adapting Weber's concept of Roman 'imperialist capitalism', this could perhaps be termed 'political capitalism'." [^6] The hedge matters: it distinguishes a declared conceptual transfer from an identity claim.

The late texts — 2022 to 2026 — perform a different operation. Marx approached capitalism from the standpoint of wage labour, Weber from that of the owners of capital. [^11] This 2026 formulation, the most metatheoretically explicit in the collection, credits Weber's firm-centred angle of vision as "highly revealing" while subordinating it to a Lukácsian synthesis. By this point Weber is no longer primarily a source of distinctions to borrow; he is a bourgeois angle of vision to enclose. The 2025 estate-politics chapter preserves real content from Weber — workers who organise through race, ethnicity, or credentials form what Weber called an "estate" rather than a class [^9] — but inserts that category into a Marxian theory of exploitation and market interests that Weber's own framework does not supply.

The six routes identified in the analysis — political-society genealogy, historical-sociology methodology, political ethics, imperialism and political capitalism, state and bureaucracy, positivism and intellectuals — are not parallel channels of equal weight. Political capitalism and the class/estate distinction together constitute the through-line that runs from 2003 to 2025 and is where most of the citation work happens. The Gramscian-Marxian framework is not one option among the six; it is the frame within which all six routes operate. Weber supplies distinctions; the framework assigns them explanatory duties.

## 2. Political capitalism: from material corrective to estate-politics synthesis, 2013–2025

The concept of political capitalism is the longest unbroken thread in Riley's Weber engagement. It passes through three analytically distinct stages, and the tensions between them are not resolved in the supplied texts.

The first stage is a corrective move against Michael Mann. Writing in 2013, Riley reconstructs Weber's theory of imperialism against Mann's predominantly ideological account: it is true that Weber insisted that inter-state competition was saturated with conflicts over prestige, but he also argued that there was a strong material dynamic to imperialism. [^4] Riley presents Weber here as evidence in a debate, not as the source of a new category. The reconstruction is accurate — the verbatim prognosis at Economy and Society p. 919 matches the held Weber text exactly — but the broader claim that Weber held a Lenin-Hobson-style declining-profit theory is broader than the cited passage alone can establish.

The second stage is the explicit label-transfer of 2020. In Faultlines Riley adapts Weber's concept of Roman imperialist capitalism, applying it to contemporary profit-oriented activity whose returns derive from direct political power. [^6] What is significant here is the epistemological self-awareness: Riley writes "could perhaps be termed" rather than asserting an identity. The Weber passage at p. 917 supports the adaptation fairly — it identifies expansionary political power as the basis of profit for tax-farmers, state creditors, and colonial capitalists — but the contemporary economic mechanism Riley has in mind (asset-price inflation, bailouts, state guarantees) is already his own addition.

The third stage arrives in 2025 and the undated Thesis text. Riley and Brenner trace the origins of the term political capitalism to Weber, Gabriel Kolko, and Murray Rothbard, and identify tax-farming as its archetypal historical manifestation. [^10] The undated essay then attributes to Weber a broader definition: economic activity that is profit-oriented but relies predominantly on political means rather than on productivity-enhancing investment. [^13] The Weber pages at 965–66 support the tax-farming mechanism; they do not themselves use the phrase political capitalism or establish Weber's terminological priority. The genealogical claim is Weberian in substance but selective relative to the cited locus — a gap the fidelity audit makes precise.

Running alongside this trajectory is the class/estate revision, which constitutes an unresolved internal tension. The 2025 estate chapter argues that political capitalism structurally reinforces estate-type politics rather than class politics, making the Weberian category load-bearing for a contemporary diagnosis. But Riley's 2018 Bourdieu critique had explicitly rejected applying the conceptual apparatus of estate-type groups to advanced capitalism as a category error. The two positions are not reconciled in the supplied texts. They could be reconciled — 2018 may be rejecting estates as a general class model, while 2025 applies them to specific fractions under a particular regime — but the later text performs no such reconciliation. The reader who wants to use Riley's estate thesis should treat the 2018 reservation as an open question, not a superseded one.

*Direct quotations and narrow definitions (RW0016, RW0042, RW0050, RW0064, RW0076, RW0084) consistently hold; declared adaptations (RW0045, RW0085) remain fair when the transformation is explicit; selective enlargement occurs when a local passage is made to carry a wider synthesis (RW0001, RW0029, RW0082, RW0088, RW0096); the census article's state definition (RW0023) is the sole stretched verdict; twelve pairs remain unverifiable due to retrieval failure, edition mismatch, or absent predicate.*

Six pairs earn the verdict accurate, four fair, five selective, one stretched, and twelve unverifiable. Read across the Reason column and a single distinction structures the record: whether the citation asks Weber's page to do the job of the page, or the job of an argument that lives several steps beyond it.

The clearest accurate results are at the narrowest citation acts. Riley's 2013 quotation of Weber's imperialism prognosis — "For the predictable future, the prognosis will have to be made in its favor" — matches the held Economy and Society p. 919 word for word. [^4] The 2018 Trump essay quotes Weber on patrimonial office — the absence of separation between the private and the official sphere, and staff commitment as personal loyalty rather than impersonal rule — and both predicates are confirmed at pp. 1028–31. The 2024 relational-power chapter reproduces Weber's definition of power as the probability an actor carries out its will despite resistance, which the held p. 53 confirms verbatim. These are text-to-text matches.

The declared adaptations hold for a different reason. In Faultlines Riley explicitly writes that he is adapting the Roman concept, and the held p. 917 confirms that Weber identifies the direct exploitation of expansionary political power as the profit basis for the relevant actors. In the 2025 estate chapter Riley compresses Weber's criteria — special esteem, possible monopolies, and the life-conduct requirements of Stand — but preserves class/estate nonidentity and the possibility of a material foundation. Compression of a fairly stated criterion is different from substituting a wrong one.

The selective verdicts mark a coherent practice of local enlargement. The 2003 political-society formula asks Weber's differentiation of classes, status groups, and parties to carry the broader claim that political society is reducible neither to civil-society interests nor to the state; Weber's passage differentiates the spheres without stating Riley's full mediating-domain formula. The capitalism-suffrage thesis in the 2015 Bourdieu article asks the parliamentary-function pages (pp. 1407–10) to establish the affinity of industrial capitalism, mass suffrage, and a strengthened representative state; those pages defend parliament through consent, accountability, and leadership selection, which supports one component but not the full affinity proposition. The 2024 information-dependence claim redirects Weber's genuine insight about bureaucratic information into a general elite-dependence-on-subordinates thesis; Weber's immediate object is bureaucracy's advantage over parliamentary supervisors, not a theory of downward organisational dependence. The tax-farming genealogy and the broad political-capitalism definition are selective for a related reason: Economy and Society pp. 965–66 establishes the mechanism but does not itself use the term political capitalism or state Weber's priority in deploying it.

The sole stretched verdict — the 2015 census article's state definition — is materially different in kind. Riley attributes to Weber a definition of the state in terms of organisational means, specifically its bureaucratic and infrastructural capacities. [^16] The held Weber pages at pp. 77–78 define the modern state through its successful claim to monopoly of legitimate physical force within a territory. Infrastructural capacity is not the defining predicate at the cited location; this is a substitution, not an enlargement. Twelve pairs remain unverifiable because retrieval reached editorial matter, a different German collection, or an unheld work. Unverifiable does not mean wrong; it means the check cannot settle the question.

The overall pattern confirms the thesis's fidelity claim with precision: narrow quotations and declared adaptations are generally accurate or fair; the gap opens when a local passage is promoted into a system-level argument.

## 4. Riley among Weber's readers: consonances, contrasts, and the two fault lines

The supplied independent readers — Wright, Ganev, Ktenas, Bendix, Love, Rundell, Castoriadis, and Swedberg — do not form a school, and none of them cites Riley. What the comparison yields is not a record of influence but a map of analytical consonances and contrasts that allows the researcher to place Riley's Weber moves in a wider interpretive field.

*On estate politics the three-way tension among Riley 2003, Wright 2002, and Riley 2025 is unresolved — the later reinforcement of estates contradicts both Wright's erosion prediction and Riley's own 2018 rejection of estate concepts for advanced capitalism; on political capitalism Ganev and Riley are consonant on categorical distinctiveness but divergent on causal mechanism (disarray vs. systematic state support); on fact–value Riley's historicist critique contrasts with Ktenas's and Bendix's defences of Wertfreiheit as compatible with situated commitment.*

The table makes two structural features visible: on categorical distinctions Riley is generally consonant with the supplied readers; on the explanatory uses of those distinctions he is often revisionist, sometimes in ways that contradict his own earlier positions.

On estates and social closure, Wright is the closest analytic neighbour. He preserves a strict class/status distinction, treats status honour as materially consequential through coercive exclusion mechanisms, and recognises the economic rationality of racial closure — all points Riley's 2025 chapter endorses. [^29] But Wright also argues that both Marx and Weber saw capitalism as systematically eroding the salience of traditional status groups. [^30] Riley's 2025 thesis runs in the opposite direction: political capitalism structurally reinforces estate-type politics rather than class politics. [^29] The divergence is not Wright's criticism of Riley; it is an analytical contrast between two independent research trajectories. The sharper internal tension is within Riley's own corpus. His 2018 Bourdieu critique rejected applying estate-type concepts to advanced capitalism as a category error; his 2025 chapter applies those concepts directly to advanced political capitalism. The supplied texts do not reconcile these two positions.

On political capitalism, Ganev provides the most sustained independent Weberian reconstruction. He sharply distinguishes rational acquisition of profits from political capitalism as contrasting analytical categories, and locates political capitalism in social and institutional disarray — de-bureaucratisation, unmonitorable discretion, and the collapse of legal-administrative routines. [^32] Riley's mature account moves in a different direction: contemporary political capitalism depends on durable, capable fiscal and monetary agencies that systematically engineer asset-price support. Ganev's postcommunist political capitalism presupposes state weakness; Riley's requires state effectiveness. The two are consonant on the categorical point — market acquisition and political extraction are analytically distinct — and divergent on the empirical mechanism. Love adds a further complication: he shows that Weber's own use of the term capitalism was not consistent, and that tax-farming does not satisfy a market-exchange definition of capitalism. This challenges the stable Weberian anchor that Riley and Brenner invoke for their tax-farming genealogy.

On the fact-value problem, Ktenas defends Wertfreiheit as a genuine methodological achievement: the person who inquires from a value-laden standpoint plays an active and creative role without being able to present just anything as valid. [^36] Bendix presents Weber's exclusion of political value judgments from academic teaching as compatible with passionate commitment to impartiality outside the classroom. Riley's 2023 Brown review takes a harder line: Weber's sundering of fact and value was a political move aimed at specific enemies — Marxist scientific socialism and German nationalism — and he illicitly extended his problem to values in general. [^7] Riley retains the concept of objective possibilities from Weber and keeps the ethic of responsibility, but the broader value-pluralism is historicised away. Ktenas and Bendix defend a position Riley narrows; none of the supplied texts argues explicitly against Riley's reconstruction.

Rundell offers a consonance on institutional pluralism. His medieval autonomous city exists outside the orbit of the patrimonial state, and groups figurate the political from positions of closure and circumscription — a structural logic parallel to Riley's argument that political organisation independently constrains class formation. The substantive objects differ entirely — medieval Italian city-states versus contemporary Trumpian rule or Lombard patricians — and no demonstrated engagement connects the two readings. The consonance is analytical, not intellectual-historical.

## 5. Decision table: which Riley–Weber moves to trust, track, or check independently

The fidelity and reception evidence together resolve into three categories that a researcher should treat differently. They are not a hierarchy of quality; they are a map of epistemic risk.

The first category — secure moves — contains narrow quotations and declared adaptations where the Weber text establishes what Riley claims. The 2013 imperialism prognosis is verbatim-matched. [^4] The 2018 patrimonial office quotation is confirmed at both predicates. The 2024 power definition is word-for-word. The ethic of responsibility reconstruction is fair, supported by Weber's requirement that an actor give an account of the foreseeable results of one's action. The 2020 adaptation of Roman imperialist capitalism is declared as adaptation, not identity, and the cited p. 917 supports the retained mechanism. The estate application preserves class/estate nonidentity with a Weberian foundation even under compression. A researcher can cite these Riley moves with confidence, though always with the qualification that application to contemporary objects is Riley's own work, not Weber's.

The second category — productive but selective moves — contains attributions where the cited locus supports a genuine Weberian component but not the full claim Riley builds on it. The political-society formula extends Weber's differentiation beyond what "Class, Status, Party" states. The capitalism-suffrage thesis adds to Weber's parliamentary-function argument a wider affinity proposition the cited pages do not establish. The information-dependence claim redirects a genuine bureaucratic insight into a general theory of elite dependence on subordinates. The tax-farming genealogy confirms the mechanism but not terminological priority. The broad political-capitalism definition is Weberian in substance but wider than pp. 965–66 establish. Riley's Weber is doing real work in all these moves; it simply does not do all the work the argument assigns it. A researcher who uses these moves should track the Weberian component separately from Riley's synthesis, verify what the cited pages actually contain, and treat the larger claim as Riley's contribution with Weberian materials — a productive adaptation, not a textually licensed assertion.

The third category — moves requiring independent retrieval — contains the stretched state definition and the twelve unverifiable pairs. The census article attributes to Weber a definition of the state in terms of bureaucratic and infrastructural capacities, but the held pages define the state through monopoly of legitimate physical force. [^16] That substitution is material: a researcher who builds on Riley's state-centred argument should retrieve Weber's pp. 77–78 before citing. Weber's own claim that there are no general laws of history is itself a theory of history — and is contradicted by his own theory of rationalization: [^43] this attribution is used as a foil but has no routed primary locus in the supplied materials. The four Relational Power Theory polarities (formal/informal, cooperative/conflictual, people-as-power-holders, and the managerial-direction-for-profit claim) are unverifiable at their cited pages. Riley and Brenner's genealogy of political capitalism names Weber as the term's origin, but the cited pages establish tax-farming as Historically its archetypal manifestation without using the term or establishing priority. [^44] For all of these, the rule is simple: go to Weber before citing Riley's attribution.

Two open questions frame the limits of this audit. First, does another Weber locus state the broad political-capitalism definition — profit through political rather than productivity-enhancing means — more directly than pp. 965–66? The supplied witnesses do not settle it. Second, does Riley's 2025 estate-politics thesis reconcile with his 2018 rejection of estate concepts for advanced capitalism, or does it constitute a revision without acknowledgement? The supplied texts establish the tension; they do not dissolve it. A future reader who engages Riley's political-capitalism and estate-politics theses together should treat the 2018 reservation as an active methodological question, not a closed one.
