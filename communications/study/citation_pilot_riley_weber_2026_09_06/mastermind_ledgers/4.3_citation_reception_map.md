## Position among the readers

### Estates, class and social closure

Riley’s early and late uses of status differ sharply. In 2003, political privilege blocks the conversion of capitalist position into class action: “This system did not allow class interests to be translated into political claims.” By 2025, status organization is no longer chiefly an inherited obstruction; it is a contemporary strategy by which workers defend differentiated market advantages: “When workers organise in this second way they do not form a class, they form what Max Weber called an ‘estate’” [F1]. The later claim is not merely a repetition of the first. The earlier text reargues institutional mediation through municipal privilege; the later text presupposes class/status nonidentity and applies it to race, credentials, and citizenship under political capitalism. The supplied texts do not establish what caused this revision.

Wright supplies the closest direct theoretical comparator. He preserves a strict distinction between class and status, treating status honour as materially consequential because it “underwrite[s] various coercive mechanisms” [F2]. Riley’s later account is consonant with that proposition: racial closure has “an obvious economic rationality” as a source of income gains [F2]. But Wright also argues that capitalism “systematically” erodes traditional status groups, whereas Riley’s 2025 thesis predicts the reinforcement of estate politics under political capitalism [F3]. This is an analytical contrast, not Wright’s criticism of Riley: Wright’s article does not cite him.

Rundell supplies a different consonance. His Weberian city is structured through “closure and circumscription” or political opening [F4], while Riley’s 2003 Lombard argument makes municipal privileges the institutional filter that blocks class formation. Both make organization and closure causally consequential. Rundell, however, emphasizes the circulation and limitation of political power; Riley emphasizes the translation of economic interests into political claims.

The internal chronology matters. Riley’s 2018 Bourdieu critique says the Bourdieusian programme wrongly applies “the idea of a society of estate-type groups” to advanced capitalism; the 2025 chapter says political capitalism reinforces “estate-type politics rather than class politics” [F5]. This is explicit opposition between Riley’s supplied formulations. It may be reconcilable—2018 rejects estates as a general class model, whereas 2025 applies them to specific worker fractions—but the later text does not explicitly perform that reconciliation. It should therefore be described as a revision or unresolved tension, not as a seamless development.

### Political capitalism: three distinct routes

Riley’s political-capitalism argument develops through three distinguishable stages. The 2013 article reads Weber’s imperialism as jointly material and status-laden: interstate conflict is “saturated with conflicts over prestige” but also has “a strong material dynamic” [F6]. The 2020 text then explicitly marks a conceptual transfer: “Adapting Weber’s concept of Roman ‘imperialist capitalism’” to contemporary US returns secured by political power [F7]. The 2025 Riley–Brenner reply takes a different Weberian route, through tax-farming, and distinguishes its modern mechanism from the old one: contemporary rent rests less on “direct privatization of the state’s fiscal functions” than on politically supported asset prices [F8]. The undated *Thesis* systematizes this as M–A–M′, but it cannot be placed after the 2025 text merely because it appears last in the supplied list; its date is unknown.

Ganev is the strongest supplied interlocutor on this theme. He sharply distinguishes “rational acquisition of profits” from “political capitalism” [F9], consonant with Riley’s insistence that politically secured returns are analytically distinct from productive accumulation. Yet their causal objects diverge. For Ganev, political capitalism “emerges from social and institutional disarray” [F9]: broken administrative routines, porous state boundaries, discretionary control, and privilege replace coherent policy. Riley’s later version instead makes politically engineered support systematic—central-bank policy, debt, subsidies, bailouts, and asset appreciation. Ganev explicitly rejects interpretations based on coherent state tutelage. The result is not direct opposition, because neither text addresses the other, but two independent Weberian routes: **dysfunctional appropriation through fragmented state capacity** versus **systematic upward redistribution through effective state intervention**.

Love opens a second fault line. He concludes that “Weber’s use of the term capitalism is not consistent” because tax-farming does not fit a definition centred on production for market exchange [F10]. Riley and Brenner respond to essentially the same conceptual difficulty by treating tax-farming as an historical archetype while distinguishing the contemporary asset-price mechanism [F8]. They therefore preserve the analogy that Love treats as evidence of conceptual equivocation. Again, Love’s 1991 text cannot be criticism of Riley.

Riley’s position is consequently heterodox only in a qualified sense. His direct quotation of Weber’s imperialism prognosis and his 2020 adaptation are close to the retrieved Weber texts. His broader genealogy and M–A–M′ theory are his own synthesis. Among the supplied readers, Ganev confirms that “political capitalism” can be a serious Weberian category, but not Riley’s specific contemporary definition; Love demonstrates that the category’s application to antiquity and tax-farming is contested.

### Patrimonialism, bureaucracy and political organization

Riley’s 2018 Trump essay applies Weber’s tripartite typology directly: “the charismatic, the patrimonial and the bureaucratic” [F11]. Its central innovation within Riley’s supplied corpus is not the definition of patrimonial office, which tracks Weber closely, but the hybrid application: charismatic leadership and personal loyalty operating inside a legal-rational state. The later *Faultlines* largely presupposes this reading, describing Trump’s “‘patrimonial’ style of rule” without rearguing the whole typology.

Rundell is consonant at the level of institutional differentiation. He treats the medieval city as autonomous “outside the orbit of the patrimonial state” [F12], and political forms as movements between closure and opening [F4]. Riley likewise refuses to reduce political organization to a single axis: his Trump is a “patrimonial misfit,” while the 2024 relational-power chapter distinguishes domination from mere resource possession. Yet Rundell’s substantive concern is autonomous urban politics and democratic fragility, not contemporary executive personalism.

Ganev diverges more substantially. His political capitalism is a problem of de-bureaucratization: “Corrupt practices are not a style of governing—they are an assault on the infrastructure of governance” [F13]. Riley’s contemporary political-capitalism argument, by contrast, generally presupposes capable agencies that sustain returns through fiscal and monetary intervention. These routes could coexist in different cases, but the supplied corpus does not provide a synthesis.

### Marx, Weber, meaning and history

Riley’s latest formulation places Weber and Marx in contrasting “angles of vision”: “Marx approached capitalism from the standpoint of wage labour, Weber from that of the owners of capital” [F14]. Weber’s strength, in this account, is the firm-centred analysis of calculability; Marx’s is the ability to mediate immediate capitalist experience through class structure and history. This is a sharper and more epistemological distinction than the 2006 “productive return” to Marx and Weber or the 2013 comparison over imperialism.

Wright partly supports and partly challenges that placement. His Weber also centres market exchange and life chances, while Marx adds production and exploitation: “the Weberian account revolves exclusively around market transactions” [F15]. That is strongly consonant with Riley’s owner/firm standpoint. Wright, however, makes the normative consequence explicit: Weber’s treatment of labour effort centres efficiency and rationalization, thereby marginalizing workers’ capacity for resistance. Riley’s 2026 essay calls Weber’s bourgeois standpoint highly revealing but does not develop Wright’s exploitation critique there.

Ktenas offers the major complementary route omitted by Riley’s late standpoint essay: Weber as theorist of meaning. For Ktenas, “the intended meaning of each individual action is always social” [F16]; value-related inquiry is situated but cannot “present just anything as valid” [F17]. Riley’s 2026 standpoint—an achieved perspective that discloses by limiting vision—is consonant with this situated, non-arbitrary account. No influence is demonstrated: Ktenas does not cite Riley.

There is sharper divergence over historical theory. Ktenas defends Weber’s anti-speculative stance: Weber “traced tendencies and chances but not strict determinisms” [F18]. Riley’s 2018 and 2023 texts argue that Weber’s rejection of general historical laws is internally unstable and that Weberian confidence in partial theories is “a hidden dogmatism” [F19]. This is genuine analyst-level opposition between Riley and a supplied reader, but it is not explicit textual controversy: neither cites the other.

Castoriadis and Bendix reinforce the side of Weber that Riley’s late standpoint contrast brackets. Castoriadis makes “social meaning” constitutive of social-scientific material [F20]. Bendix argues that Weber’s empirical studies, not isolated methodological formulations, contain the “basic concepts and central problems” of the later work [F21]. Riley is consonant with both whenever he insists on historical specificity and rejects abstract definitions detached from concrete institutions. But his increasingly Marxian interpretation of standpoint and exploitation gives Weber a more limited critical horizon than either reader does.

### Science, values and responsibility

Riley’s 2011 reconstruction of responsibility is comparatively orthodox: conviction and responsibility are “not ‘absolute contrasts, but rather supplements’” [F22]. His 2023 Brown review revises the emphasis by historicizing the lectures. Weber’s fact–value distinction becomes a political intervention shaped by German nationalism, and political values may have a cognitive component because they arise from diagnosis and programme [F23].

Ktenas is the nearest supplied counter-position. He defends value-relation as constitutive of inquiry while maintaining evidentiary constraint [F17]. Bendix similarly presents Weber’s exclusion of political value judgments from teaching as compatible with passionate “partisanship for impartiality” [F24]. Riley’s 2023 reading is consonant with them in rejecting value-free indifference, but more critical: he interprets Weber’s generalized fact–value divide as an illicit extension of the specific difficulty of justifying nationalism.

No supplied independent reader directly addresses Riley’s reconstruction of Weberian responsibility. Accordingly, it cannot be called orthodox or heterodox *among the supplied readers* beyond these analytical comparisons.

## Reception and citation scope

No supplied scholarly citation-count table exists. The citation index counts twenty-seven Riley or coauthored citing texts, ninety-eight candidate Weber-name windows, and twenty-eight checked pair candidates, but those are local routing counts—not citation-impact measures. Therefore:

- no “most-cited reader” ranking can be made;
- no count-based list of important readers whom Riley failed to cite can be made;
- inferred affinities—Riley with Wright on material closure, with Ktenas on situated standpoint, or with Rundell on political organization—must remain separate from documented citation.

Among the independent supplied readers, Wright’s full 2002 article and Ganev’s full 2009 article contain no demonstrated citation of Riley. Ktenas’s 2021 full article and Rundell’s 2014 held chapter also do not cite Riley. Swedberg, Bendix, and Love are represented by bounded excerpts; only absence within those excerpts is established, and Bendix and Love predate all or almost all relevant supplied Riley texts. Castoriadis’s visible extract is incomplete, so Riley engagement is unresolved.

The principal unsettled question is whether Riley’s later political-capitalism and estate theses form one coherent revision of his earlier Weber reading. The supplied texts show a movement from political mediation, through imperialist capitalism, to contemporary asset rents and estate politics; they do not establish when or why Riley came to regard estate concepts—rejected as a general model of advanced capitalism in 2018—as appropriate to the political capitalism of 2025.

## Judgment

Riley sits neither simply inside nor outside an orthodox Weberian reception. He is closest to the supplied readers when he preserves analytical distinctions: class from status, market acquisition from political extraction, patrimonial from bureaucratic organization, and standpoint from biography. His narrow quotations and definitions tend to be conventional and often accurate. His distinctiveness lies in what he makes those distinctions do.

Compared with Wright, Riley expands status from a material supplement to class analysis into a diagnosis of contemporary estate politics. Compared with Ganev, he shifts political capitalism from fragmented, privilege-centred state failure toward systematic state-supported asset appreciation. Compared with Ktenas and Castoriadis, he narrows Weber’s horizon by treating owner-centred calculability and nationalist value conflict as the limits of Weber’s standpoint. Compared with Rundell, he shares an institutional account of political organization but directs it toward class formation and contemporary executive rule rather than democratic autonomy.

The best characterization is therefore **selectively consonant but theoretically revisionist**. Riley is orthodox in many local uses of Weber, heterodox in the synthetic burdens he places on them, and largely unengaged by the supplied peers. No supplied evidence demonstrates that the consonances result from influence or that the contrasts formed an explicit controversy.
