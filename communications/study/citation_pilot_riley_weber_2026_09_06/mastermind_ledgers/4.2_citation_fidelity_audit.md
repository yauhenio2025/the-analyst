# Citation fidelity audit: Dylan Riley’s indexed Weber attributions

## Position map and scope

The supplied collection contains **27 Riley or coauthored citing documents**, five routed Weber witnesses, and a duplicated citation index. The index provides routing, loci, edition warnings, candidate counts, and duplicate links; its repeated quotations are metadata rather than an independent source.

The citing documents occupy distinct argumentative roles:

- **Privilege and Property** (2003) explains failed Lombard class formation through political institutions; Weber supplies a qualified class/status/party precedent.
- **Civic Associations and Authoritarian Regimes** (2005) explains divergent Italian and Spanish authoritarian regimes through associational strength; Weber appears only in its method of reconstructing historical alternatives.
- **Waves of Historical Sociology** (2006) periodizes historical sociology through changing relations between Marx and Weber; its assessable pair concerns Gorski’s reported reading, not Riley’s direct adoption.
- **Tony Judt: A Cooler Look** (2011) criticizes Judt’s historical and political moralism; Weber supplies a corrective account of political responsibility.
- **Routes or Rivals?** (2013) challenges Michael Mann’s separation of capitalism and war; Weber provides an imperialism prognosis, although Riley’s broader declining-profit reconstruction exceeds this pair.
- **Back to Weber!** (2014) assesses Biernacki’s critique of coding and appeal to ideal types; no specific Weber locus is routed for paired assessment.
- **The Third Reich as Rogue Regime** (2014) criticizes Tooze’s separation of ideological ends from rational tactics; “Weberian” is an interpretive label without a routed primary locus.
- **The Racialization of Legal Categories in the First U.S. Census** (2015, Rebecca Jean Emigh, Dylan Riley, and Patricia Ahmed) advances an interactive state–society account of census categories; its Weberian state definition is directly assessable.
- **The New Durkheim: Bourdieu and the State** (2015) criticizes Bourdieu’s neglect of democracy and repression; its appeal to Weber on parliament is assessable but broader than the supplied pages.
- **Politics as Theatre?** (2016) criticizes Runciman’s reduction of politics to style; its Weber reference is a brief, unrouted gloss.
- **BOURDIEU’S CLASS THEORY** (2017) argues that Bourdieu lacks an adequate macrosociology; its Weber references identify concepts but no primary locus.
- **What Is Trump?** (2018) replaces a simple fascism analogy with a qualified patrimonial–charismatic–bureaucratic analysis; its patrimonial quotations are assessable.
- **Science and Politics: A Response to Burawoy, Heilbron, and Steinmetz** (2018) disputes the idea that sociology can avoid a theory of history; its Weber attribution has no routed primary work.
- **Faultlines** (2020) explains US politics through political capitalism, redistributive coalitions, and personalized leadership; it explicitly adapts Weber’s Roman imperialist capitalism.
- **Real Utopia or Abstract Empiricism?** (2020) argues for comparative study of noncapitalist societies; Weber is invoked generally, without a routed locus.
- **The Social Foundations of Positivism** (2021, Riley, Emigh, and Ahmed) distinguishes methodological from ontological positivism; three Weber pairs test rationalization and the life-conduct question.
- **Notes on the Curriculum** (2021) criticizes curricular accounts that displace analysis of ideas; Weber is a canonical example rather than a paired primary citation.
- **Seven Theses on American Politics** (2022, Riley and Robert Brenner) distinguishes status-group competition from class politics; no specific Weber work is routed.
- **Hidden Dogmatism** (2023) challenges Weberian confidence in partial theories of history; the reference is to a tradition without a primary locus.
- **Science and Politics: Reflections on Wendy Brown’s Nihilistic Times** (2023) historicizes Weber’s fact/value distinction and political responsibility; only its citation of the Machiavellian example is paired here.
- **The Sociology of Sociological Interventions** (2024, Patricia Ahmed, Rebecca Jean Emigh, Riley, and Nancy Wang Yuen) reports an earlier Weber–Gramsci synthesis; it has no eligible primary-text pair.
- **Introduction: Relational Power Theory** (2024 metadata; printed copyright 2025, Emigh and Riley) constructs a multidimensional theory of power; ten Weber attributions are routed, with sharply varying fidelity.
- **The Rise of Political Capitalism** (2025) relates political capitalism to estate-type worker politics; its estate citation is assessable at the edition-sensitive marginal A180.
- **The Long Downturn and Its Political Results** (2025, Riley and Brenner) answers critics through a theory of stagnation and political capitalism; Weber’s tax-farming discussion supplies a historical mechanism, not the complete genealogy claimed.
- **Angles of Vision** (2026) contrasts Marx’s wage-labour standpoint with Weber’s owner/firm standpoint; no primary Weber locus is identified.
- **First Principles** (2026) treats differentiated market positions as the material basis of working-class fracture; its Weber attribution is unrouted.
- **The Thesis of Political Capitalism** (date unknown) defines political capitalism as value expansion through political means; the same tax-farming pages only partially support its broad definition.

## What the paired evidence establishes

### Exact quotation and narrow definition

The clearest correspondences are quotations or tightly bounded definitions.

Riley reproduces Weber’s imperialism prognosis exactly: both texts state, “For the predictable future, the prognosis will have to be made in its favor” [F7]. Weber’s surrounding argument connects imperialist capitalism to public contracts, concessions, government loans, monopolized opportunities, and politically backed expansion. Riley’s quotation is accurate, although this pair does not independently establish his broader reconstruction of declining investment returns.

The patrimonial citation is likewise secure. Riley quotes the absence of separation between the private and official spheres, and Weber states that predicate directly [F10]. The neighbouring cited pages also distinguish impersonal office commitment from “a servant’s loyalty” to the ruler. Riley’s application to Trump is his adaptation; the quoted Weberian criteria themselves are accurate.

Three other narrow attributions hold. Emigh and Riley reproduce Weber’s definition of power as the probability that an actor can carry out a will despite resistance [F17]. Their statement that authority depends “at least in part” on belief in legitimacy preserves Weber’s qualification that legitimacy operates alongside custom, affect, and material advantage [F25]. The positivism article accurately quotes the question, “What shall we do, and, how shall we arrange our lives?”, while Weber’s surrounding passage identifies it as a question science cannot answer [F14].

The Brown review’s Machiavellian example also holds. Riley presents Weber’s called politician through Florentine citizens who place civic greatness above salvation; Weber invokes that example while discussing the tension between political means and salvation ethics [F15].

Finally, Riley’s reconstruction of responsibility in the Judt essay is fair. The held window supports responsibility for foreseeable consequences and morally dangerous political means [F6]. The exact claim that conviction and responsibility are “supplements” occurs later in the held primary work, outside the routed search window, so the pair is fair rather than fully settled in every quoted detail.

### Declared adaptation

A declared adaptation need not reproduce Weber literally. In *Faultlines*, Riley explicitly writes, “Adapting Weber’s concept of Roman ‘imperialist capitalism’” [F11]. Weber’s cited page identifies tax-farmers, state creditors, suppliers, privileged traders, and colonial capitalists whose profits rest on direct exploitation of expansionary political power. Riley retains that mechanism while transferring it to contemporary political economy. The attribution is fair because the transformation is transparent.

The estate citation works similarly. Riley applies *Stand* to workers organized through racial, ethnic, or credential distinctions rather than through class politics [F26]. Weber defines an estate through effectively claimed special esteem and possible monopolies, states that status may rest on class without being identical to it, and links estate formation to appropriated privileges. Riley preserves nonidentity between class and estate and the possibility of a material foundation, but compresses Weber’s criteria of life conduct, esteem, and effective monopoly.

Emigh and Riley’s organizational-domination claim is also fair. Weber says that major economic organizations exhibit structures of domination and that bureaucracy is technically superior for complex, continuous administration [F22]. The chapter’s sharper contrast between command through organizational position and ownership is its synthesis rather than Weber’s exact formulation.

### Selective enlargement

The 2003 citation makes Weber’s differentiation bear a larger claim. Weber distinguishes the economic location of classes, the social location of status groups, and the power-oriented activity of parties. Riley develops that into political society as reducible neither to civil-society interests nor to the state [F1]. Weber’s “house of power” supports differentiation, but not Riley’s full mediating-domain formula.

In *The New Durkheim*, the supplied Weber pages defend strong parliament through consent, accountability, leadership selection, and positive participation in government. Riley turns this into a wider claim about the affinity of industrial capitalism, mass suffrage, and a strengthened representative state [F9]. The parliamentary component is supported; the complete capitalism–suffrage proposition is not stated in the window.

The organizational-information citation redirects a genuine asymmetry. Weber says bureaucrats possess technical and official information unavailable to parliamentary supervisors. Emigh and Riley restate this as leadership dependence on information embedded at lower organizational levels [F23]. The inference is intelligible, but Weber’s immediate object is bureaucracy’s advantage over parliament, not a general theory of elite dependence on subordinates.

The later political-capitalism citations are selective for a different reason. Weber’s pages 965–66 explain tax-farming: the ruler receives a fixed amount, the farmer retains the surplus, and the arrangement may encourage ruthless exploitation [F27]. They do not themselves name political capitalism or establish Weber’s priority for the term. Riley and Brenner’s mechanism is Weberian; their genealogy is broader than the cited locus.

The undated *Thesis of Political Capitalism* generalizes still further. Riley attributes to Weber a contrast between profit through political means and productivity-enhancing investment [F28]. The held work elsewhere distinguishes market opportunities from acquisition based on power relations, but the cited pages primarily concern office- and tax-farming. The broad definition is Weberian in substance yet selective relative to pp. 965–66.

### A stretched state definition

The census article says Weber defines the state through “bureaucratic and infrastructural capacities” [F8]. At the cited pp. 77–78, Weber defines the modern state through its successful claim to monopolize legitimate physical force within a territory. The wider essay later treats administration and bureaucracy, but “infrastructural capacities” is not the defining predicate at the cited location. This is the only checked pair where the held text supplies a materially different definition.

### Retrieval and edition limits

Twelve pairs remain unverifiable. This does not mean that Weber rejects the claims.

The two *Methodology of the Social Sciences* citations cannot be tested because the retrieved German MWG windows are from a different collection and either consist of editorial material or discuss Roscher rather than suppressed alternatives [F2, unverified] [F3, unverified]. Gorski’s post-Axial reading has no cited page connecting it to the retrieved discussion of religious virtuosi [F4]. Judt’s allusion is routed to Gerth and Mills’s biographical introduction rather than Weber’s lecture [F5, unverified]. The analogy between Mosca’s political formula and Weberian legitimacy is unresolved because the cited German 1921 page is unheld and the supplied English search pages are editorial [F16, unverified]. The material-resources citation likewise spans several unresolved German works, while its supplied search window concerns monetary policy [F21, unverified].

The positivism pairs also remain unresolved. Weber’s cited economic-action pages discuss calculability, capital accounting, and formal and substantive rationality, but they do not assert that capitalism generates positivism [F12] [F13]. RW0049 additionally cites an unheld *General Economic History* locus, so the combined proposition cannot be settled from the held *Economy and Society* pages.

Four propositions in *Relational Power Theory* are not carried by their retrieved pages. Those pages do not state that power “can be held by people” [F18], formulate the authors’ formal/informal polarity [F19], define consensuality as a cooperative/conflictual continuum [F20], or give the complete claim that managers direct subordinates for profit [F24]. None of these absences constitutes Weber’s silence beyond the supplied windows.

## Practice within the checked sample

A stable distinction appears within these 28 pairs. Direct quotation and narrow definition usually hold: imperialism [F7], patrimonial office [F10], life conduct [F14], political vocation [F15], power [F17], and legitimacy [F25]. Declared adaptations can remain fair when the transformation is explicit, as in *Faultlines* [F11] and the estate application [F26].

Problems arise chiefly when Riley or his coauthors make a local passage carry a larger synthesis. The political-society formula [F1], capitalism–suffrage thesis [F9], lower-level information dependence [F23], and tax-farming genealogy and definition [F27] [F28] retain Weberian components but exceed the retrieved loci. The census article’s state definition is more serious because it substitutes bureaucratic and infrastructural capacity for the cited legitimate-force predicate [F8].

No checked pair warrants “misattributed.” Where the retrieved pages are editorial, irrelevant, or silent on the attributed predicate, the correct result is unverifiable rather than adverse. This judgment applies only to the 28 indexed pairs, not to Riley’s complete citation practice.
