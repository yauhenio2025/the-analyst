# Dylan Riley reading Max Weber across the supplied texts

## Position map

The collection contains twenty-seven citing texts plus a duplicated citation index. The index is routing metadata only: its 98 candidate name windows, 28 checked pair candidates, unresolved windows, page calibrations, and duplicate links do not constitute additional citations or prove absence elsewhere.

Riley’s supplied texts fall into six argumentative routes:

1. **Political society, class formation, and authoritarian organization.** *Privilege and Property* (2003) argues that municipal privileges prevented economically capitalist Lombard nobles from forming politically as a class; Weber helps distinguish political society and status organization, though Gramsci carries more of the institutional argument. *Civic Associations and Authoritarian Regimes* (2005) explains divergent Italian and Spanish authoritarianisms through associational organization; Weber enters only as methodological support for studying suppressed alternatives. *The Civic Foundations* is not supplied here as a separate text, so no claims about it are made.

2. **Historical sociology, interpretation, and social theory.** *Waves of Historical Sociology* (2006) periodizes the field through changing relations between Marx and Weber, then argues that intellectual advance occurs through “productive return.” *Back to Weber!* (2014) assesses Biernacki’s proposal to replace coding with Weberian ideal types, finding the critique of coding powerful but the procedures of interpretation underdeveloped. *Bourdieu and the State* (2015), *Bourdieu’s Class Theory* (2017), and *Science and Politics* (2018) use Weber selectively against Bourdieu: for representative government, reflexive labour, and the unavoidable presence of historical theory. *Real Utopia or Abstract Empiricism?* (2020) invokes Weber’s comparative sociology to make capitalism’s historical peculiarity visible. *Notes on the Curriculum* (2021) treats Weber chiefly as a canonical test case: institutional canonization cannot substitute for reconstructing ideas. *Hidden Dogmatism* (2023) turns the Weberian preference for partial histories into a foil. *First Principles* (2026) returns positively to Weber’s account of differentiated market positions within the working class. *Angles of Vision* (2026) offers the collection’s most explicit metatheoretical reading: Weber sees capitalism from the firm and owner’s standpoint, Marx from wage labour.

3. **Political ethics and value.** *Tony Judt: A Cooler Look* (2011) uses Weber’s ethic of responsibility to correct Judt’s moralism and places Weber in dialogue with Sartre. *Politics as Theatre?* (2016) uses “conviction and responsibility” only as a compressed gloss on representative politics. *Science and Politics: Reflections on Wendy Brown’s Nihilistic Times* (2023) gives the sustained treatment: Riley historicizes Weber’s fact/value distinction as a response to German nationalism and revises it through “objective possibilities.”

4. **Imperialism, war, and political capitalism.** *Routes or Rivals?* (2013) recruits Weber against Michael Mann’s predominantly ideological account of imperialism: prestige conflict remains, but so does a material dynamic. *The Third Reich as Rogue Regime* (2014) calls Tooze’s split between ideological ends and instrumentally rational means “deeply Weberian,” but uses that description critically. *Faultlines* (2020) explicitly adapts Weber’s Roman “imperialist capitalism” into contemporary “political capitalism.” *The Rise of Political Capitalism* (2025) primarily develops estate politics, while *The Long Downturn and Its Political Results* (2025, with Robert Brenner) returns to Weber’s tax-farming analogue. *The Thesis of Political Capitalism* (year unknown) supplies the most explicit current definition: political means enter the circuit of value expansion.

5. **State, bureaucracy, patrimonialism, and power.** *The Racialization of Legal Categories* (2015, with Rebecca Jean Emigh and Patricia Ahmed) treats Weber both as a root of society-centred sociology and as the source appropriated by neo-Weberian state-centred analysis; its own answer makes state autonomy historically variable. *What Is Trump?* (2018) applies and qualifies Weber’s charismatic, patrimonial, and bureaucratic types to explain Trump’s hybrid rule. *The Sociology of Sociological Interventions* (2024, with Ahmed, Emigh, and Nancy Wang Yuen) only reports an earlier Weber–Gramsci synthesis; it makes no direct citation move with Weber. *Relational Power Theory* (2024, with Emigh) systematically mines Weber for power, domination, bureaucracy, legitimacy, information dependence, and formal versus informal authority, while subordinating these materials to a broader relational theory.

6. **Positivism and intellectuals.** *The Social Foundations of Positivism* (2021, with Emigh and Ahmed) treats Weberian rationalization as one component of the externalist theory linking capitalism and positivism, but limits that account to methodological positivism; Weber’s question about how to live helps identify Italian positivists as a teleocratic intellectual estate.

The remaining supplied texts—*Science and Politics: A Response to Burawoy, Heilbron, and Steinmetz*, *Seven Theses on American Politics*, and the shorter Sidecar essays—are important because they show Weber appearing inside Riley’s independent Marxian arguments rather than dictating them. Across the collection, Weber is therefore neither master theorist nor routine foil. He is a modular interlocutor: Riley borrows distinctions, tests them historically, and often relocates them inside a Marxian account of exploitation, accumulation, and class formation.

## The main line: from class and status to estate politics

The earliest supplied substantive use is *Privilege and Property*. Riley explicitly places the paper in a “Gramscian and Weberian tradition” and uses Weber’s “Class, Status, Party” to establish that political society cannot be reduced either to civil-society interests or to the state [F1]. Weber here supplies genealogy and conceptual permission; the empirical mechanism—municipal institutions transforming ownership interests into claims of local privilege—is Riley’s. This matters because the article does **not** collapse market position and status honour. Its argument is precisely that an economically convergent agrarian-capitalist group could remain politically organized through patrician privileges.

That distinction is reformulated in the 2022 and 2025 political writings. In *Seven Theses*, Riley and Brenner call worker fractions organized to protect the value of labour “status groups”: the term names political-ideological management of competition rather than a class relation [F2]. In *The Rise of Political Capitalism*, Riley sharpens the contrast: workers who organize through race, ethnicity, citizenship, or credentials form what Weber called an “estate,” not a class [F3]. He immediately adds the decisive reader’s addition: estates may be culturally marked but have a material foundation. Weber’s category is thus inserted into a Marxian distinction between market interests and interests arising from exploitation.

There is continuity from 2003 to 2025, but not a simple repeated thesis. In 2003 status organization explains why a capitalist nobility fails to become a political class; in 2022–25 estate organization explains why wage earners fail to act collectively against capital. The later texts generalize the earlier problem from a historical landed elite to contemporary labour. What persists is the proposition that common economic location does not automatically generate class politics. What changes is the explanatory setting: from institutions translating interests into municipal privilege to political capitalism producing defensive closure among worker fractions [F4].

The supplied texts therefore do not support a claim that Riley fuses closure, status honour, estate, and class. Rather, he uses Weber to keep apart: class position; market-based sectional advantage; culturally marked closure; and politically constituted class formation. *First Principles* states the point compactly: the working class is fractured by material interests arising from its component market positions [F5]. Its proposed strategy is not to deny these interests but to “suture” them to class-wide interests rooted in wage labour.

## Imperialism and the construction of political capitalism

*Routes or Rivals?* is the crucial starting point for this route. Against Mann’s reduction of imperialism to militarism, Riley reconstructs Weber as combining prestige conflict with a “strong material dynamic” [F6]. He further claims—explicitly and provisionally—that Weber’s theory lies near Hobson and Lenin: declining returns on ordinary investment make imperial ventures, armaments, and state loans comparatively attractive. Weber is evidence, foil, and dialogue partner at once. Riley acknowledges the status dimension but denies that it exhausts imperialism.

*Faultlines* changes the role of the citation. Weber no longer merely corrects another theorist; Riley explicitly adapts Roman “imperialist capitalism” into “political capitalism,” cautiously defined as profit-oriented activity whose returns derive largely from direct political power [F7]. The “could perhaps be termed” matters: it marks adaptation rather than an assertion that contemporary America reproduces ancient Rome.

The 2025 coauthored reply shifts again. Riley and Brenner name Weber as one origin of “political capitalism” and identify tax-farming as its archetypal form [F8]. The mechanism is specific: entrepreneurs advance money for the right to collect taxes and pocket the difference between the advance and politically enforced extraction. They then analogize this to contemporary fiscal redistribution through debt. The later argument is not simply the 2013 prognosis repeated. The sequence is:

- **2013:** Weber supplies a material account of imperialism alongside prestige.
- **2020:** Riley adapts a Weberian label to contemporary profit through political power.
- **2025:** Riley and Brenner reconstruct a historical extraction mechanism—tax-farming—to clarify politically constituted rent.
- **Year unknown:** *The Thesis of Political Capitalism* defines Riley’s own M–A–M′ circuit, while retaining Weber’s initial contrast between political means and productivity-enhancing investment [F9].

This is development in argumentative use, though not a demonstrated change in Riley’s opinion about Weber. The citation moves from corrective evidence, through explicit adaptation, to genealogy and historical analogy [F10]. The later theory—asset-price inflation, state guarantees, debt, and investment in political influence—is Riley’s addition, not something attributed to Weber.

## Patrimonialism, bureaucracy, and relational power

*What Is Trump?* gives Weber’s domination types their most concentrated political application. Riley recalls charismatic, patrimonial, and bureaucratic rule, then identifies Trump’s mode of administration as patrimonial because it turns public office into personal rule and staff commitment into personal loyalty [F11]. But the application is carefully qualified. Patrimonialism was historically fitted to premodern orders; Trump inserts it “like a foreign body” into a legal-rational capitalist bureaucracy. His legitimacy, moreover, cannot rest on tradition and must instead be charismatic. Fox, Twitter, and right-wing media become postmodern mechanisms transmitting that charisma.

The claim is thus not “Trump is a patrimonial ruler” without remainder. It is that Trump’s rule is an unstable compound: patrimonial administration, media-mediated charisma, and a resistant legal-rational state. This hybrid diagnosis supports Riley’s rejection of fascism as a sufficient classification. In *Faultlines*, the same distinction is largely presupposed rather than reargued: Trump’s “patrimonial” style and aversion to bureaucratic impersonality are used to explain conflict with the professional civil service [F12].

The 2024 coauthored *Relational Power Theory* broadens the institutional use of Weber. The chapter begins with Weber’s actor-centred definition of power but labels such definitions too narrow for a theory encompassing nonagentive power [F13]. It nonetheless adopts several Weberian propositions: organizations work through domination rather than resource possession alone; leaders depend on information held below them; rules and spheres of competence stabilize uncertain cooperation; and authority depends partly on belief in legitimacy [F14]. Weber now supplies components of a synthetic framework, not its architecture. Marx provides exploitation, Gramsci ideational power and hegemony, while Weber contributes domination, organization, and legitimacy.

This is a substantial enlargement relative to *What Is Trump?*: the types of legitimate domination used diagnostically in 2018 become, by 2024, elements in a general relational theory of elite and nonelite power. Because the latter text is coauthored, this should be stated as a development across Riley’s supplied texts, not as Riley’s solitary theoretical evolution.

## Science, value, responsibility, and historical standpoint

In *Tony Judt: A Cooler Look*, Weber corrects Judt’s moralized “ethic of responsibility.” Riley insists that political responsibility means accepting responsibility for legitimate violence and its consequences; conviction and responsibility are supplements rather than simple opposites [F15]. Placing Weber beside Sartre, he argues that both reject the comfortable position of the moralizer. This is direct adoption and dialogue, although the primary Weber passage was not independently certified here.

The 2023 Brown review revises the problem more radically. Riley historicizes Weber’s fact/value distinction as a political response to Germany’s specific predicament—rapid capitalist development, socialist opposition, geopolitical pressure, and nationalist great-power aspiration [F16]. He argues that Weber illicitly generalized the difficulty of justifying nationalism into a difficulty applying to values as such. Values differ in their relation to knowledge: nationalism and socialism do not pose the same science–politics problem.

Yet Riley does not simply reject Weber. He praises Weber’s ability to embrace German nationalism while recognizing its contingency, retains the idea of responsibility, and borrows “objective possibilities” to reconnect political values with historical diagnosis [F17]. The move is selective reconstruction: Weber’s separation of science and politics is narrowed, while a Weberian concept helps Riley formulate an alternative in which political values possess a cognitive component.

The methodological criticism extends into *Science and Politics* (2018) and *Hidden Dogmatism* (2023). The first argues that Weber’s denial of general historical laws is itself a theory of history, strained by his theory of rationalization [F18]. The second challenges the broader Weberian preference for partial histories without a grand narrative. By *Angles of Vision* (2026), however, the criticism is more dialogical. Riley reads Weber’s firm-centred standpoint as illuminating: wage labour matters because it renders labour costs calculable. But this standpoint sees capitalism from its owners’ side, while Marx begins from wage labour and property relations [F19]. Standpoints here are constructed politico-epistemological achievements, not biographies.

## Evidence, foil, and dialogue

Across these texts Weber has three distinguishable roles:

- **Evidence:** *Routes or Rivals?* on imperialism; *What Is Trump?* on patrimonial administration; *Relational Power Theory* on bureaucracy, information, and legitimacy.
- **Foil:** Mann’s militarism; Tooze’s separation of ideological ends and rational means; Bourdieu’s neglect of representative government; Weberian scepticism about historical totality; the generalized fact/value divide.
- **Dialogue:** the Weber–Sartre pairing on responsibility; Biernacki’s return to ideal types; the Brown review’s selective reconstruction; the Marx–Weber standpoint comparison.

These roles can coexist locally. The 2023 Brown essay, for example, treats Weber’s nationalism as historical evidence, his generalized value pluralism as foil, and “objective possibilities” as a resource for dialogue.

## Scope and qualifications

The authoritative scope assessments are overwhelmingly **inconclusive**, even where they retain positive findings. That outcome is methodological, not evidence of silence. For most documents the complete supplied text was inspected, but the corpus is not Riley’s complete work, some primary Weber passages were not checked independently, several citations are mediated through readers such as Gorski, Biernacki, Brown, or Lukács, and several works are coauthored.

The principal exceptions recorded as **findings present** are: the cited object in *Routes or Rivals?*; the cited objects in *Bourdieu and the State* and *Bourdieu’s Class Theory*; the Weberian foil in *Hidden Dogmatism*; the place and stances in the Brown review; the reported combined framework in *Sociological Interventions*; and the cited-object result in the 2025 Riley–Brenner reply. These positive outcomes remain document-specific.

For *Sociological Interventions*, citation move is explicitly outside positive scope: the text reports that earlier work combined Weber and Gramsci, but does not itself perform one of the defined argumentative moves with a direct Weber citation. This scoped negative is not a claim that Weber is absent from the chapter or from the authors’ work.

The earliest 2003 assessments remain inconclusive because their review state is unchecked and the retained evidence is partial or limited to the supplied article. Similar caution applies to *Waves*, *Tony Judt*, *Back to Weber!*, *The Third Reich*, *Politics as Theatre?*, *Science and Politics*, *Faultlines*, *Real Utopia*, *Social Foundations*, *Notes on the Curriculum*, *Seven Theses*, *Relational Power Theory*, *Rise of Political Capitalism*, *Angles of Vision*, *First Principles*, and the undated *Thesis*: substantive passages are present, but they cannot establish exhaustive citation practice.

Edition limits matter. The citation Weber 1922, p. 180 in *The Rise of Political Capitalism* corresponds to marginal **A180** at MWG I/23, pp. 598–99, not printed MWG p. 180. The Gerth–Mills copy held is a 1948 impression, while citing texts sometimes give 1946 or 1958. Search matches involving different German and English collections do not establish edition equivalence.

## Read first

1. **“Routes or Rivals?”, pp. 491–92** — opens the imperialism route and shows Riley distinguishing prestige from material capitalist dynamics [F6].
2. **“What Is Trump?”, pp. 25–28** — the clearest applied Weberian typology and the best example of Riley qualifying rather than simply importing a category [F11].
3. **“Science and Politics: Reflections on Wendy Brown,” pp. 449–53** — the fullest sustained reconstruction and critique of Weber’s fact/value problem [F16–F17].
4. **“The Rise of Political Capitalism,” pp. 97–102** — shows estate as materially grounded closure and clarifies its difference from class [F3].
5. **“Angles of Vision,” complete two-page essay** — states most directly Riley’s final supplied judgment about Weber’s standpoint, powers, and limits [F19].

## Overall judgment

Across this supplied collection, Riley reads Weber as indispensable but insufficient. Weber provides distinctions that resist reduction—class and estate, economy and political profit, charisma and bureaucracy, facts and values, subjective meaning and historical explanation. Riley’s characteristic move is then to historicize those distinctions and insert them into arguments about exploitation, accumulation, institutions, and collective agency. The trajectory is not from rejection to adoption. It is from selective borrowing toward increasingly explicit metatheoretical containment: by 2026 Weber’s owner-centred angle of vision is credited with reaching very far, but Marx’s standpoint is assigned the stronger capacity to disclose capitalism as a historically specific class structure.

The unsettled question is whether Riley’s later “estate-group world” and political-capitalism thesis can be integrated without making status organization merely an effect of capitalist stagnation. The supplied texts establish the bridge—material market interests, closure, and politically constituted returns—but do not yet provide a fully worked account of how estate solidarities become, or fail to become, class politics.

### Counter-evidence

- *Privilege and Property* does not support saying that Weber supplies the detailed Lombardy mechanism; the Weber citation establishes a conceptual lineage, while Riley’s institutional history does the explanatory work.
- *The Third Reich as Rogue Regime* calls Tooze’s structure “deeply Weberian,” but this is a critical characterization of Tooze, not an endorsement of that structure.
- *Back to Weber!* reports Biernacki’s Weberian proposal and then faults its lack of interpretive procedure.
- *The Social Foundations of Positivism* retains the capitalism–rationalization account only within a delimited scope; the Italian case is expressly anomalous for that framework.
- *Sociological Interventions* reports a Weber–Gramsci synthesis but performs no direct citation move with Weber.
