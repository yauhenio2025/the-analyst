# The passages — Riley on Weber: conceptual machinery borrowed, misreadings corrected, and political categories remade

*Riley on Weber: every passage in the held texts that cites or names Weber, in the order of the texts; each read in its document by anthropic/claude-sonnet-5; the quotes verified against the renditions by code.*

## The Thesis of Political Capitalism (n.d.) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:GEFERFPB)

*Argument.* Riley argues that neither the 'return to normal capitalism' thesis nor the platform-rents thesis adequately explains post-2008 low growth, high inequality, and political fragmentation. Instead he proposes the thesis of political capitalism, a new M–A–M' circuit combining political power and asset ownership, drawing the term from Weber but redefining it for his own purposes.

*The place of Weber.* Weber is invoked as the originator of the term 'political capitalism,' giving genealogical and conceptual authority to Riley's key category. Riley immediately notes that the term has since been used in contradictory ways by different schools, and stakes out his own specific definition distinct from Weber's original and from subsequent uses—so Weber functions as a source to be cited for terminological pedigree before being superseded by Riley's own analytic reworking.

**PDF p. 2** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AGEFERFPB%2Fopen#page=2&search=Weber) · genealogy · builds on · cites Weber's coinage of 'political capitalism' in Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft

Riley credits Weber with originating the term political capitalism to describe profit-oriented activity relying on political rather than productive means, using this as the historical anchor for his own theoretical apparatus. He then immediately distinguishes his own usage—a specific property relationship where political power enters value expansion—from Weber's and from later, divergent appropriations by neoliberals and leftists.

> "The term was originally deployed by Max Weber to describe economic activity that is profit-oriented but relies predominantly on political means rather than on the reaping of productivity gains"

## Privilege and Property: The Political Foundations of Failed Class Formation in Eighteenth-Century Austrian Lombardy (2003) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:BEFGGK6M)

*Argument.* Riley draws on Weber's essay 'Class, Status, Party' alongside Gramsci to establish that political society is conceptually irreducible to either civil society or the state. This theoretical foundation, labeled 'Gramscian and Weberian,' underwrites the paper's central claim that political society has autonomous effects on class formation.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions as a co-founding authority for the paper's core theoretical distinction between state, civil society, and political society, paired with Gramsci to establish the conceptual framework the whole essay builds on. Weber is not engaged with in detail or disputed; he is cited briefly for a single essay to anchor the 'Weberian tradition' that Riley claims to follow, then largely recedes as Gramsci's concepts (hegemony, political society) do the sustained theoretical work.

Riley pairs Weber with Gramsci as joint authorities for the claim that political society cannot be reduced to civil-society interests or state structures. Weber's essay is cited alongside Gramsci's Prison Notebooks to legitimate the paper's foundational conceptual move, establishing the 'Gramscian and Weberian tradition' the paper claims to extend.

> "the German sociologist Max Weber is his essay on class, status and parties, political society is reducible neither to interests in civil society, nor to the state"

## Civic Associations and Authoritarian Regimes in Interwar Europe: Italy and Spain in Comparative Perspective (2005) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:AKVCVFYG)

*Argument.* Riley argues that civic associationism can enable rather than obstruct hegemonic authoritarianism, using Italy and Spain as a Millian comparison: strong associationism in Italy fed the fascist party and produced a hegemonic regime, while weak, fragmented associationism in Spain prevented party formation and produced an economic-corporate dictatorship. He develops this against Tocquevillian expectations and in favor of a Gramscian account of associations as transmission belts of political power.

*The place of Weber.* Weber is a minor methodological reference cited alongside Moore for the idea of analyzing 'suppressed alternatives' embedded within historical sequences. Weber is not engaged substantively on his sociology of authority, bureaucracy, or the state, but invoked briefly as a methodological authority to support Riley's comparative-historical strategy, which combines a Millian method of difference with attention to historical sequence and counterfactual possibility.

**PDF p. 3** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AAKVCVFYG%2Fopen#page=3&search=Weber) · authority · mentions · cites Methodology of the Social Sciences, on suppressed historical alternatives

Riley cites Weber (alongside Moore) merely to back the methodological point that a historically-sequenced analysis should attend to alternatives that were suppressed by actual outcomes, not to engage any substantive Weberian thesis about authority or the state. The citation recurs twice in the passage, both times in parenthetical support of the same methodological claim about sequences and suppressed possibilities.

> "an analysis of suppressed alternatives embedded in historical sequences (Moore 1978:385–91; Weber 1949:172)"

## Waves of Historical Sociology (2006) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:BWDEURC8)

*Argument.* Riley reviews claims about a third wave of historical sociology, arguing that supposed breaks with second-wave concerns (epochal transitions, class-based agency) are better understood as returns to earlier problems. He uses Weber as the anchor for one such 'return' claim about epochal thinking.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions here primarily as a textual resource being reinterpreted by Gorski, whose 'creative reading' of Weber's essay on religious rejections of the world Riley cites as evidence that epochal, transition-scale thinking has not disappeared from historical sociology but returned in new form. Weber himself is treated with mild skepticism—Riley notes Gorski's periodizing ambition would make Marx and Durkheim blush, 'although probably not Weber himself'—suggesting Weber's own thought already contained resources for grand epochal claims, making Gorski's extension less novel than it appears. Weber is not directly argued with; he is the original source whose ideas are creatively repurposed by a contemporary scholar, and Riley uses this repurposing as a data point in his larger thesis about historical sociology advancing by returning to its origins.

**PDF p. 7** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3ABWDEURC8%2Fopen#page=7&search=Weber) · evidence · mentions · cites Weber's essay 'Religious Rejections of the World' (Zwischenbetrachtung)

Riley cites Gorski's 'creative reading' of Weber's essay to support his claim that epochal transition-thinking persists in third-wave historical sociology rather than giving way to an 'even cadence.' He notes that Weber himself, unlike Marx or Durkheim, would probably not blush at Gorski's ambitious periodizing scheme, implying Weber's own work already anticipated large-scale civilizational transitions (the 'post-Axial' shift). Weber is thus positioned as the deep source whose ideas Gorski extends rather than a direct interlocutor of Riley's.

> "Gorski develops this argument through a creative reading of Weber’s essay on religious rejections of the world."

## Tony Judt: A Cooler Look (2011) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:H6GGBV9I)

*Argument.* Riley uses Weber's actual argument in 'Politics as a Vocation' to expose Judt's superficial and self-serving deployment of the concept of 'responsibility.' This single citation is deployed as a foil demonstrating Judt's broader pattern of intellectual carelessness: invoking major thinkers' terminology while evacuating their actual content.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions as a foil against which Judt's conceptual sloppiness is measured. Riley reconstructs Weber's genuine position on the ethic of responsibility—tied to legitimate violence and the tragic choice between political means—to show that Judt's 'Weberian calculus' bears little resemblance to it, and that Judt evades the affinity between Weber's view and Sartre's, which would undercut his moralizing dichotomies.

**p. 47** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AH6GGBV9I%2Fopen#page=17&search=Weber) · foil · disputes · cites Weber's ethic of responsibility, 'Politics as a Vocation'

Riley contrasts Judt's vague gloss of the 'Weberian calculus' with Weber's actual argument that responsibility is bound up with the use of legitimate violence and the tragic incommensurability of political ends and means. He uses this reconstruction to show Judt's citation is decorative rather than substantive, and notes Judt avoids the inconvenient proximity of Weber's view to Sartre's.

> "Weber's actual view was rather different. For the great sociologist, the ethic of responsibility was embodied by the political actor who took responsibility for the use of ‘legitimate violence’."

## Routes or Rivals? Social Citizenship, Capitalism, and War in the Twentieth Century (2013) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:PSYPHAPS)

*Argument.* Riley argues that Mann's structuralist separation of capitalist and militarist dynamics is untenable, and that Mann's own use of Weberian 'status/militarism' as an ideological explanation for imperialism and war departs from Weber's actual, more materialist account. Riley uses Weber's own text on political capitalism to show that Weber himself held a view closer to Lenin/Hobson than to Mann's ideology-of-militarism thesis, thereby undercutting Mann's claim to Weberian authority.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions here as a source of evidence deployed against Mann: Riley reconstructs Weber's actual theory of imperialism (political capitalism, declining rates of profit, material dynamics behind status competition) to show that it diverges sharply from the version of 'Weberian' militarism Mann invokes. Weber is thus a resource for correcting Mann's genealogy of his own concepts, not a figure Riley endorses in his own right—Weber's status-conflict language is treated as compatible with a materialist reading that Mann suppresses.

**PDF p. 9** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3APSYPHAPS%2Fopen#page=9&search=Weber) · evidence · qualifies · cites Weber's theory of imperialism/political capitalism in Economy and Society

Riley cites Weber's actual argument about 'political capitalism' and declining rates of profit to show that Weber's account of imperialism, despite the prestige/status language Mann borrows, was materially grounded and close to the Lenin/Hobson thesis Mann rejects. This is used to reveal a contrast between Mann's invocation of 'Weberian' status and Weber's own more structural analysis, undermining Mann's appeal to Weber as ideological support for militarism.

> "The universal revival of ‘imperialist’ capitalism, which has always been the normal form in which capitalist interests have influenced politics, and the revival of political drives for expansion are thus not accidental."

## The New Durkheim: Bourdieu and the State (2015) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:EBSFFE5N)

*Argument.* Riley argues Bourdieu's neglect of democracy and repression in his theory of the state stems partly from the classical sociological tradition (Durkheim, Weber, Marx) that Bourdieu draws on, which marginalized mass democracy as an object of explanation. Weber is singled out as the exception within this tradition who understood the affinity between capitalism and mass suffrage and the strengthening effect of representative government on the state, yet this exception does not fully explain Bourdieu's blind spot, which Riley traces instead to Bourdieu's top-down field-of-power framework.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions as a comparative reference point within the genealogy of classical social theory that shaped Bourdieu's intellectual formation. He is invoked briefly to complicate a monocausal explanation of Bourdieu's neglect of democracy and repression: Weber, unlike Durkheim and Marx, actually grasped the link between capitalism and mass suffrage and the repressive capacities of modern states, which means the 'classical tradition' explanation for Bourdieu's blind spots only partially works.

**PDF p. 10** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AEBSFFE5N%2Fopen#page=10&search=Weber) · genealogy · qualifies · cites Weber's 'Parliament and Government in a Reconstructed Germany' (Economy and Society)

Riley uses Weber to test and ultimately limit an explanation for Bourdieu's neglect of democracy: unlike Durkheim and Marx, Weber understood the affinity between capitalism and mass suffrage and that representative government strengthens the state, and was clear about state repression too. This means the 'trapped by classical tradition' explanation for Bourdieu's omissions is undermined, since Weber (part of that same tradition) got it right, pushing Riley toward a structural explanation rooted in Bourdieu's field-of-power theory instead.

> "Only Weber understood the deep affinity between modern industrial capitalism and mass suffrage, and only he pointed out consistently that representative government strengthens, rather than weakens the state."

## Antecedents of Censuses from Medieval to Nation States: How Societies and States Count (2016) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:UWEWLCPF)

*Argument.* Riley reads Weber's theory of bureaucracy and legal-rational authority as originally dialectical, positing mutual dependence between bureaucracy, capitalism, and democracy, but argues that neo-Weberians have flattened this into a state-centered account emphasizing only bureaucratic domination. Riley recovers the dialectical elements in Weber's own texts (elective affinities, the interdependence of parliament and bureaucracy) to ground his own interactive state-society model. Weber thus functions both as the genealogical origin of the dominant state-centered paradigm the book critiques and as a resource for the dialectical alternative Riley proposes.

*The place of Weber.* Weber is simultaneously a foil and a framework: Riley criticizes the standard 'neo-Weberian' reading of Weber's bureaucracy theory as overly state-centered, but recovers a different, more dialectical Weber—one who linked bureaucracy to capitalism and democracy through 'elective affinities' and mutual dependence—to build his own society-state interactive model. Weber's discussions of legal-rational authority, elections, interest groups, and parliamentary accountability are mined extensively as building blocks for Riley's 'macro-Weberian' theory of how democratic institutions in society sustain and check bureaucratic power. Weber is treated less as a target than as an under-read ally whose original nuance has been lost by his interpreters.

**p. 7** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AUWEWLCPF%2Fopen#page=18&search=Weber) · genealogy · qualifies · cites Weber's theory of bureaucracy and elective affinity between capitalism/bureaucracy and democracy/bureaucracy

Riley argues Weber's original formulation already contained a dialectical relationship between bureaucracy, capitalism, and democracy, but that neo-Weberians strip this nuance to produce a purely state-centered account. He uses Weber's own textual claims (legal-rational authority, elective affinities) to show the interpretive distortion, distinguishing Weber's checks on bureaucratic power from how he is usually read.

> "Weber (1978:220–226, 974) argued that there was an elective affinity between capitalism and bureaucracy based on legal rational authority"

**p. 7** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AUWEWLCPF%2Fopen#page=18&search=Weber) · evidence · builds on · cites Wirtschaftsgeschichte on calculability and bureaucratic rationality enabling profit-oriented economic action

Riley cites Weber's claim that bureaucratic calculability is a precondition for profit-oriented economic action as evidence for the bidirectional relationship between capitalism and bureaucracy, supporting his broader dialectical reading against one-sided state-centered interpretations.

> "Without the calculability that bureaucratic rationality provides, economic action could not be oriented toward profit"

**p. 28** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AUWEWLCPF%2Fopen#page=39&search=Weber) · authority · adopts · cites Politik als Beruf on elections, interest groups, and parliament-bureaucracy accountability

Riley draws directly on Weber's account of how elections and interest groups prevent bureaucracies from becoming closed status groups, and especially Weber's English-Parliament example of accountability between legislatures and bureaucracies, to construct his own 'macro-Weberian' dialectic of bureaucracy and democracy as mutually dependent state-society structures.

> "elections undermine the solidification of a status group and consequently help maintain legal-rational authority"

## Bourdieu’s Class Theory (2017) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:JD34AWGB)

*Argument.* Riley argues that Bourdieu's sociology fails as macrosociology and that its popularity among elite US academics stems from resonance with academic lived experience, ersatz political identity, and defense of academic autonomy rather than explanatory power. He develops an analogy between Bourdieusian sociology and Weberian Protestant ethic to explain its appeal as a form of self-transformation, and situates Bourdieu's political vision within a Weberian tradition of pluralist elite theory.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions in this text primarily as an analogical framework and a genealogical marker: Riley uses the Protestant ethic to explain the psychological/cultural appeal of Bourdieusian reflexivity as a secularized religious practice, and separately places Bourdieu's underlying political vision within a lineage of elite pluralism running through Weber. Weber is not engaged as an interlocutor whose theory is tested or disputed, but invoked twice as a comparative touchstone to characterize Bourdieu's sociology from the outside.

**PDF p. 22** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3AJD34AWGB%2Fopen#page=22&search=Weber) · illustration · adopts · cites Protestant ethic/Calvinism; mixed constitution/pluralist elite theory

Riley uses Weber's account of the Calvinist ethic as an analogy to explain why Bourdieusian 'reflexivity' functions like a secularized religious discipline of constant self-examination, accounting for the appeal of Bourdieu's sociology as self-transformation rather than explanation. Later in the same passage's section, Riley also places Bourdieu's political vision of elite pluralism within a tradition 'from Aristotle to Weber,' using Weber as a genealogical marker for the mixed-constitution/pluralist-elitism strand Bourdieu's work ultimately recapitulates.

> "Like the Calvinist ethic Weber described, Bourdieu’s sociology requires a constant examination of the self, a process glossed under the term “reflexivity.”"

## What Is Trump? (2018) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:3YLGA6BQ)

*Argument.* Riley argues that fascism, authoritarianism, and populism are inadequate categories for Trump, and instead proposes reading his rule through Weber's typology of domination, specifically as a patrimonial form of legitimation lodged incongruously within a modern legal-rational, capitalist bureaucratic state. This mismatch—patrimonial household rule grafted onto bureaucratic America—generates the structural instability and dysfunction characteristic of the Trump administration.

*The place of Weber.* Weber supplies the core conceptual framework of the essay: his tripartite typology of charismatic, patrimonial, and bureaucratic domination is adopted wholesale to characterize Trump's mode of rule, displacing the fascism/populism/authoritarianism analogies Riley rejects. Weber is not a foil or evidence to be tested but the theoretical apparatus through which Riley builds his positive account of Trump as a patrimonial ruler operating paradoxically atop a legal-rational bureaucratic state.

**p. 25** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3A3YLGA6BQ%2Fopen#page=21&search=Weber) · authority · adopts · cites Weber's typology of patrimonial, charismatic, bureaucratic domination; definition of patrimonial office

Riley uses Weber's definition of patrimonialism—rule as household, no public/private distinction, personal loyalty rather than office-based commitment—to characterize Trump's staffing and governing style. He quotes Weber directly to ground the claim that Trump's relationship to aides is 'a servant's loyalty' rather than bureaucratic commitment, then extends this to diagnose the 'inherently paradoxical' insertion of a patrimonial logic into a modern legal-rational capitalist state.

> "it may be useful to recall Weber’s three forms of rule, each with its own apparatus of domination and logic of legitimation: the charismatic, the patrimonial and the bureaucratic."

Weber is not engaged substantively or disputed; he supplies a standard sociological definition (legitimacy as an answer to why men obey) that Riley uses as a stepping stone toward his own distinction between democracy and liberalism as separable institutional forms.

## Faultlines: Political Logics of the US Party System (2020) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:CX58SV9M)

*Argument.* Riley argues that US party politics since 1980 has been reshaped by 'political capitalism'—a mode of profit extraction dependent on direct political power rather than accumulation—which underlies the redistributive, zero-sum conflict between the two coalitions. This concept, adapted from Weber, frames his analysis of elite and mass party coalitions and their competing logics.

*The place of Weber.* Weber is a source of a single conceptual term—'political capitalism,' originally used for Roman 'imperialist capitalism'—which Riley adapts and repurposes to name the contemporary US political-economic conjuncture. Weber functions as a terminological/theoretical anchor providing legitimacy and precision for Riley's own coinage, rather than as an interlocutor whose broader sociology is engaged.

**p. 9** [PDF ↗](http://127.0.0.1:8765/static/pdfjs/web/viewer.html?file=%2Fapi%2Fitems%2Fem%3ACX58SV9M%2Fopen#page=2&search=Weber) · authority · builds on · cites Weber's concept of Roman 'imperialist capitalism' (Economy and Society)

Riley borrows Weber's category of 'imperialist capitalism' from ancient Rome and adapts it to coin 'political capitalism,' a term for a contemporary form of profit-making dependent on direct exercise of political power rather than investment and accumulation. The citation to Economy and Society supplies scholarly grounding for this analogical move, which becomes central to Riley's periodization of US politics after 1980.

> "Adapting Weber’s concept of Roman ‘imperialist capitalism’, this could perhaps be termed ‘political capitalism’"

## The Social Foundations of Positivism: The Case of Late-Nineteenth-Century Italy (2021) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:89QVNXT8)

*Argument.* Riley et al. argue positivism's form depends on the social structure of the intelligentsia, distinguishing methodological positivism (industrial capitalist societies with organic intellectuals) from ontological positivism (backward societies like Italy with traditional intellectuals). Weber is grouped with the externalist theorists who link positivism to capitalism and rationalization, a position the article ultimately qualifies as insufficient to explain the Italian case.

*The place of Weber.* Weber functions as a representative externalist theorist whose account of rationalization is cited alongside Marx and Simmel as part of the general claim that capitalist modernity produces positivist/scientistic worldviews. He is treated as evidence for the externalist position that the article seeks to synthesize with internalism (Bourdieu) via a Gramscian theory of intellectuals, and specify the conditions of validity of rather than simply endorse or refute. In one later passage, Weber's famous formulation of the practical question of value ('What shall we do...') is used illustratively to characterize the teleological orientation of Italian traditional intellectuals, detached from any specific Weberian theoretical claim about capitalism.

## Science and Politics: Reflections on Wendy Brown’s Nihilistic Times - Wendy Brown, Nihilistic Times: Thinking with Max Weber (Cambridge, MA, Harvard University Press, 2023, 132 p.) (2023) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:SBN4ZVXW)

*Argument.* Riley argues that Brown's reading of Weber's vocation lectures treats fact/value and value pluralism as general, ahistorical problems, when in fact Weber's own concern was the specific reconciliation of science with German nationalism amid geopolitical and class threats. He contends that Brown inherits this Weberian abstraction, failing to historicize either the fact/value distinction or contemporary value pluralism, which Riley attributes instead to the absence of popular political subjectivity.

*The place of Weber.* Weber is the central textual object mediated through Brown's book: Riley reconstructs Weber's vocation lectures historically to show that Brown's generalized reading of fact/value and value pluralism obscures Weber's specific concern with German nationalism versus socialism and Anglo-French cosmopolitanism. Weber functions both as a figure whose work Riley re-historicizes against Brown's presentation, and as a foil whose 'irreducible plurality' of values Riley disputes as itself historically specific rather than a permanent condition.

Riley summarizes Brown's chapter-by-chapter treatment of Weber's attempt to insulate facticity from value, noting Brown's own critique that this attempt is paradoxically nihilistic; Riley uses this summary to set up his objection that Brown's and Weber's shared assumption of general value/knowledge relations is ahistorical.

> "Wendy Brown’s beautifully written and warmly personal set of meditations on Weber’s vocation lectures invites its readers to think anew the relationship between science (Wissenschaft) and politics"

Riley historicizes the timing of Weber's first lecture (a month after the Bolshevik Revolution) to argue that the essay was not about values in general but the specific value of German national greatness against socialist and Anglo-French threats, contra Brown's thin contextualization.

*Argument.* Riley and Brenner defend and refine their 'political capitalism' thesis against critics who charge it is inconsistent with Brenner's earlier work and imprecise historically, by tracing the term's genealogy and specifying its mechanism (asset-price rents, M-A-M' circuit) as distinct from technofeudalism.

## The Rise of Political Capitalism, the Crisis of Democracy, and the Strategic Tasks of the Left (2025) — [the record](http://127.0.0.1:8765/#/item/em:QKSXS3VW)

*Argument.* Riley argues that political capitalism has produced a new pattern of social conflict—estate-type rather than class-type politics—because stagnant profitability forces proletarians into defensive struggles over fixed shares rather than collective struggles over the surplus. He uses Weber's concept of the 'estate' to theorize this shift, showing that identity-based closures (education, race) have a material rationality that rival descriptive accounts (Lind, Karp) fail to explain theoretically.

*The place of Weber.* Weber is a conceptual source providing the key analytical category—the 'estate'—that Riley deploys to explain the material basis of non-class forms of worker organization (around race, credentials, ethnicity). Weber's distinction between class and estate is adopted wholesale and integrated into a Marxian framework to explain why political capitalism generates 'neo-estate politics' rather than class politics, filling a theoretical gap left by merely descriptive accounts of 'class de-alignment.'
