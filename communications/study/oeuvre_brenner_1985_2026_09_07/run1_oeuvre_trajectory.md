# Reading: property relations, capitalist dynamics, and working-class agency

The supplied collection pursues a remarkably stable explanatory wager across changing objects: markets, technologies, institutions, and political opportunities do not carry determinate effects by themselves. Their consequences depend on historically formed social-property relations, class capacities, and rules of reproduction. The oeuvre applies that wager along four main routes: the transition from feudalism to capitalism; merchant and revolutionary history; working-class politics and reformism; and the profitability-centered history of modern capitalism. A fifth, smaller route extends the same framework to bureaucratic collectivism and capitalist restoration.

The focal text belongs centrally to the first agenda. It does not inaugurate Brenner’s property-relations account—the decisive formulations are already explicit in 1976–78—but reconstructs its conceptual genealogy by dividing Marx’s work into two incompatible models. It thereby turns a position previously argued against demographic, commercialization, and world-system theories into an interpretation of Marx’s own development. Later texts repeatedly presuppose and extend that reconstruction.

## The focal text’s place: **middle / theoretical reorientation within an established agenda**

*Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism* sits in the **middle** of the transition agenda but performs a **theoretical reorientation** within it. The substantive position precedes it: in 1977 Brenner had already argued that “The rise of trade is not at the origin of a dynamic of development because trade cannot determine the transformation of class relations of production” [F3]. The focal text then historicizes that position by asserting that Marx offered “two Marxian models” which “represent two fundamentally different historical materialisms” [F4]. Its distinctive role is therefore not to discover social-property relations as the explanatory key, but to explain why one Marxian tradition repeatedly reproduces the commercial model that Brenner had criticized.

The article’s decisive move is methodological. It subjects Marx’s early works to concepts drawn from the later ones—explicitly acknowledged in note 5 as “based on ‘hindsight’”—and argues that the early causal chain assumes capitalist mechanisms in order to explain capitalism. Its positive alternative is only sketched: transition becomes the problem of how feudal actors, trying to reproduce themselves within feudal relations, unintentionally transform those relations [F5]. The 1986 *Social Basis of Economic Development* immediately presents this as a general theory of property relations and rules of reproduction [F6], while the 1989 *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism* returns to the focal distinction and applies it to the English Civil War [F7]. Later transition texts generally presuppose rather than reargue the distinction: *The Low Countries* says capitalist relations had to emerge from feudal actors’ reproductive strategies, and *Property and Progress* gives the resulting causal chain from social-property relations to rules of reproduction and aggregate development [F23–F24].

Thus the focal text is best read as the oeuvre’s conceptual hinge between an already-established critique and its systematic later formulation. It also imposes an important qualification on the collection’s frequent invocations of “Marx”: the oeuvre does not treat Marx as supplying a single consistent transition theory.

### 1972–89

- **The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion, 1550–1650** (1972) records merchant organization as constitutive of opportunity; role: actor-centered commercial history; qualification: it rejects complete Pirennean replacement of old capitalists by “new men” [F1].
- **Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development** (1976) records class power as determining the effects of demography and trade; role: foundational comparative statement; qualification: it does not make class outcomes automatic [F2].
- **Origins of Capitalist Development** (1977) records free wage labor and producer separation as capitalism’s historical problem; role: critique of Smith, Sweezy, Frank, and Wallerstein; qualification: trade remains consequential but not determinant [F3].
- **Reply to Sweezy** (1978) records “pre-capitalist commodity production” as underspecified; role: clarification through polemic; qualification: it grants Sweezy’s analytical distinction but denies that it solves transition.
- **A New Social Democracy?** (1980) records reformist officialdom as a barrier to self-activity; role: foundational strategic polemic; qualification: social democracy itself depends historically on mass struggle [F9].
- **Reagan, the Right and the Working Class** and **The Right Wing and the Working Class: A Response** (both 1981, unordered) record rightward movement as a practical response to crisis and organizational weakness; roles: thesis and defense; qualification: neither claims uniform ideological conversion [F20].
- **Again on Reform or Revolution in Poland** (1983) records bureaucratic control as incompatible with workers’ democracy; role: revolutionary-strategy intervention; qualification: revolutionary strategy is presented primarily as guidance for daily politics, not insurrectionary scheduling.
- **Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?** (1984) records electoral logic as distinct from movement-building logic; role: categorical anti-electoralist polemic; qualification: the target is electoralism as strategy, not every possible vote [F10].
- **Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism** (1985) argues that Marx’s early and later transition models embody incompatible historical materialisms; role: Marxological reconstruction of an existing Brenner thesis; qualification: Part I primarily criticizes the early model, while the later model remains a task rather than a solved history [F4–F5].
- **The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism** (1985) records a comparative defense of social-property analysis; role: response to the Brenner Debate; qualification: demographic trends matter through class relations.
- **The Paradox of Social Democracy** (1985) records reformist officialdom as undermining the movements on which it depends; role: synthesis of the political agenda; qualification: reformism’s mass constituency is distinguished from its officials [F11].
- **Austerity & Interventionism**, **The Deep Roots of U.S. Economic Decline**, **The Elections and the Left**, and **The Social Basis of Economic Development** (all 1986, unordered) respectively analyze military Keynesianism, structural manufacturing decline, conditional electoral work, and market dependence; their distinct roles are conjunctural policy analysis, economic diagnosis, programmatic qualification, and abstract theory. None claims that state policy, elections, or markets are irrelevant [F6, F13].
- **Notes on the Crash and Crisis** (1988) records the 1987 crash within a profitability crisis; role: first compact crisis statement; qualification: debt postpones depression rather than having no effect.
- **Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism** and **Untitled Contribution on the Class Nature of the Soviet Union** (both 1989, unordered) apply property-relations theory respectively to English revolution historiography and bureaucratic collectivism; qualifications: English capitalism arises “within a landlord shell,” while the Soviet system is neither capitalist nor socialist [F7, F21].

### 1991–2007

- **Soviet Union & Eastern Europe, Parts I and II**, **The Regulation Approach**, and **Why Is the United States at War with Iraq?** (all 1991, unordered) extend the framework to bureaucratic crisis, failed marketization, capitalist institutions, and imperial action. Their qualifications are respectively that planning does not fail intrinsically, restoration need not bring development, institutions matter within capitalist constraints, and imperial actions can be counterproductive [F15, F22].
- **Failing to Bring the State Back In**, **Merchants and Revolution**, and **The Problem of Reformism** (all 1993, unordered) analyze finance’s obstruction of public investment, merchant blocs in the English Revolution, and reformism’s institutional basis. Their roles are conjunctural polemic, major archival synthesis, and strategic restatement; qualifications: state intervention remains capitalist, the merchant community is internally divided, and programs alone do not identify reformism [F8].
- **Clinton’s Failure & the Politics of U.S. Decline** (1995) records a “politics of decline” generated by profitability crisis and altered class forces; qualification: finance is decisive partly because no counterforce supports industrial reconstruction.
- **A View of the Teamster Tragedy**, **The Economics of Global Turbulence**, and **The Looming Crisis of World Capitalism** (1998, unordered) address autonomous rank-and-file organization, the long-downturn synthesis, and the 1998 crisis. Their qualifications are that progressive officials can be allies, competition both develops and destabilizes production, and neoliberalism aggravates rather than originates the crisis [F14].
- **Competition and Class** and **Reply to Critics** (1999, unordered) defend the competition/overcapacity account; qualification: exploitation and class struggle remain consequential even when intercapitalist competition is the immediate crisis mechanism.
- **The Boom and the Bubble** (2000) distinguishes a real manufacturing recovery from its bubble-driven prolongation; qualification: the boom is not wholly fictitious [F17].
- **The Low Countries in the Transition to Capitalism** and **The World Economy at the Turn of the Millennium** (2001, unordered) apply the two principal historical-economic agendas to Dutch agrarian development and a fragile US-led boom; qualifications: Dutch commerce was indispensable but insufficient, and the US revival was real but structurally vulnerable [F23].
- **An Economy of Two, Three Many Enrons**, **Competition and Profitability**, **England’s Divergence from China’s Yangzi Delta**, **The Boom, the Bubble, and the Future**, **The Economy After the Boom**, and **The Trajectory of the Manufacturing Profit Rate** (all 2002, unordered) test the framework against corporate fraud, theoretical and measurement criticism, global divergence, and the post-bubble recession. Their shared qualification is methodological: no input order establishes development among these same-year texts.
- **Elections & the Democrats** and **New Boom or New Bubble?** (2004, unordered) respectively permit tactical Democratic support and diagnose debt-driven recovery; neither abandons the strategic independence or profitability-centered positions [F12, F17].
- **The Capitalist Economy, 1945–2000** (2005) answers agency- and hegemony-centered critics; qualification: structural constraints condition rather than mechanically dictate action.
- **The Economics of Global Turbulence**, **The Origins of Capitalism**, and **What Is, and What Is Not, Imperialism?** (2006, unordered) consolidate the long-downturn account, restate transition as unintended property transformation, and refine imperialism through the multiple-state system. Their qualifications are that capitalism remains technologically dynamic, commercialization is indispensable though insufficient, and states can generate collectively counterproductive outcomes [F16].
- **Property and Progress** and **Structure vs Conjuncture** (2007, unordered) systematize the social-property causal chain and distinguish the 2006 electoral conjuncture from the structural rightward shift; qualifications: Smith identifies real capitalist mechanisms but universalizes them, and Democratic victory is not equivalent to progressive realignment [F24].

### 2009–25

- **What Is Good for Goldman Sachs Is Good for America** (2009) embeds the financial crisis in long-run weak accumulation and asset-price Keynesianism; qualification: finance amplifies rather than independently creates the crisis [F18].
- **The Political Economy of the Rank-and-File Rebellion** (2010) links labor militancy to employer attack and bureaucratic failure; qualification: rebellion is more effect than cause of falling profitability.
- **Feudalism** and **The Pre-history of Core–Periphery** (2011, unordered) synthesize feudal political economy and global nondevelopment; qualifications: legal, political, and economic definitions of feudalism are complementary, and commercialization can coexist with nondevelopment.
- **Introducing Catalyst** (2017) identifies neoliberalism with austerity and upward redistribution and calls for durable organization; qualification: autonomous movements and electoralism are each insufficient alone.
- **Escalating Plunder** (2020) records pandemic rescue as state-backed corporate predation; qualification: the argument assigns bipartisan rather than solely Republican responsibility.
- **From Theory to History** (2021) criticizes Mann’s “European Dynamic” through the merger of class and state under feudalism; qualification: it opposes both holistic evolutionism and a merely autonomous-state account.
- **Seven Theses on American Politics** (2022) proposes political capitalism and fiscal coalitions; qualification: it calls this a low-growth transformation within capitalism, not a new mode of production [F19].
- **The Long Downturn and Its Political Results** (2025) connects persistent low profitability to the emerging M–A–M′ circuit of state-supported asset returns; qualification: political capitalism exists alongside, rather than replacing, productive capitalism [F25].

All 58 supplied texts can be assessed at least at profile level; only the focal text is supplied in full. The held sample does **not** cover Brenner’s entire oeuvre. It omits, among other items visible in the profiles’ self-citations, *Dobb on the Transition*, *The Rises and Declines of Serfdom*, and *Property Relations and the Growth of Agricultural Productivity*. It also does not supply many cited interlocutor texts. Consequently, claims about “first,” “last,” or disappearance apply only to this collection.

## Judgment

Across the collection, Brenner’s dominant position is not simply “class before markets.” It is that historically specific relations of reproduction determine what counts as rational action, while the aggregate consequences of those rational actions are often unintended. Under feudalism, peasant subsistence and lordly political accumulation reproduce stagnation; under capitalism, competitive cost-cutting produces both unprecedented productivity and recurrent overcapacity; under reformism, officials preserve organizations while weakening the activity that gives them force; under political capitalism, firms rationally seek state-supported asset returns while productive dynamism deteriorates. The focal text supplies the Marxological architecture for this general position. It is neither an outlier nor the origin of the agenda, but the text that most clearly explains why Brenner believes a property-relations historical materialism must break with another, equally Marxian-looking, theory of automatic development.

### Counter-evidence

- The focal text’s binary division of Marx may be too sharp. It acknowledges that Marx’s second model “was never fully worked out,” while the first contains insights that anticipate the later critique. The article itself notes a passage where early Marx recognizes that generalized competition depends on the world market.
- The 1998 claim that “the logic of competition, not class struggle” rules capitalism’s deeper rhythms [F14] diverges in emphasis from the transition and political writings, where class conflict is indispensable. The collection reconciles these by assigning competition the immediate role within established capitalism and class conflict the constitutive role in forming or transforming property relations, but that division remains contestable.
- The 2004 defense of voting Democratic qualifies the apparently categorical 1984 case against electoral engagement. It is best understood as a tactical revision, though the collection does not eliminate the tension.
- *Merchants and Revolution* gives trade, merchant initiative, institutional privilege, and political alliance substantial causal weight. It therefore guards against reducing Brenner’s history to agrarian relations alone.
- “Political capitalism” may strain the earlier insistence that capitalist market dependence governs reproduction. The 2025 text answers this by presenting M–A–M′ as an emerging circuit alongside M–C–M′, not as its replacement.
