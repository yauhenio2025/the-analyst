# Reading

## The end of the road visible here

Read as the end of the supplied road, *Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism* is a qualified culmination of one pre-1985 line of argument, not of everything Brenner had written. The route that reaches it begins with *The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion, 1550–1650* (1972), which makes merchants’ social constitution mediate commercial opportunity while allowing opportunity to matter [F1]; continues through *Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe* (1976), which makes class power condition demographic and commercial effects [F2]; and reaches explicit transition theory in *The Origins of Capitalist Development* (1977), which distinguishes capitalism from mere market production [F3, unverified], and *Reply to Sweezy* (1978), which identifies the unexplained place of class formation and conflict in trade-centred transition theory [F4]. A separate political route follows. *A New Social Democracy?* (1980) grounds class consciousness in workers’ self-constitution through struggle; the focal text likewise requires class formation and struggle, but does not import the political essay’s account of organization or reformism [F5, unverified]. *Reagan, the Right and the Working Class* (1981) explains political conduct through perceived material interests and makes renewed struggle the possible reversal; the focal text shares the causal emphasis on socially situated action but addresses feudal reproduction rather than contemporary consciousness [F6, unverified]. The same-year *The Right Wing and the Working Class: A Response* makes actual conditions fundamental to consciousness while rejecting survey reduction; the focal article instead makes property relations structure reproduction, so the continuity remains methodological rather than a demonstrated inheritance [F7]. *Again on Reform or Revolution in Poland* (1983) treats revolutionary strategy as a guide to everyday practice, whereas the focal text invokes class formation and struggle without developing an analogous political strategy [F8, unverified]. *Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?* (1984) distinguishes electoral aggregation from movement power; the focal text’s corresponding concern is not electoral organization but whether class struggle has independent causal efficacy [F9]. The focal article turns the transition route back upon Marx: the earlier appeal to Marxian class categories becomes a reconstruction of “two Marxian models” that are “entirely incompatible” [F10]. This resolves the central negative problem by showing that the competitive mechanism works systematically only after capitalist market dependence exists, so the first model must “assume the transition in order to explain the transition” [F11]. It also identifies the alternative starting point in the conflicted reproduction of lord–peasant property relations, but leaves unexplained how feudal reproduction dissolves those relations [F12, unverified]. The result is a **culmination of the negative and conceptual transition agenda, but not a completed theory of transition** [F19]. The supplied collection contains the focal text and nine earlier texts, not the complete oeuvre; omitted writings between 1978 and 1985 may alter the chronology, and nothing written after the focal text enters this judgment.

The transition inheritances involve more than repeated vocabulary. In 1972, the object was the socially variable response of merchant groups to commercial possibilities; in the focal article, the object is the capacity of exchange to transform entire property systems [F1]. In 1976, class structure conditioned the effects of population and commerce; the focal article strengthens that proposition by defining social-property relations as constitutive of reproduction itself [F2]. In 1977, the distinction between capitalism and market activity served a direct critique of neo-Smithian Marxism; in 1985 it becomes the retrospective standpoint from which early Marx’s causal sequence is interrogated [F3, unverified]. Finally, the 1978 demand for an autonomous account of class formation and conflict is no longer directed only at Sweezy: it becomes the basis for separating two incompatible historical materialisms within Marx [F4].

The most consequential transformation concerns Marx. The 1977 text had used Marxian class categories to oppose free-wage-labour capitalism to pre-capitalist class systems. The focal article instead announces that Marx’s writings contain two incompatible models [F10]. Marx is therefore neither simply retained as authority nor rejected. The text distinguishes an early causal chain running from productive forces through the division of labour to class and property from a later account in which property relations antecedently organize reproduction.

Smith’s changed role makes that reconstruction possible. The earlier argument denied that trade could transform class relations; the focal article now says that early Marx “mirrors rather closely Adam Smith’s classic account” [F13]. Smith thus becomes an instrument of immanent criticism: the text uses him to oppose one Marx to another. Sweezy and Wallerstein remain inherited opponents, but their place changes accordingly. Instead of being answered primarily through comparative history, they are presented as Marxists who adopted the Smithian strategy found in Marx’s first model [F14] [F15].

The five political writings do not form direct steps in this transition reconstruction. They nevertheless prevent an overbroad culmination claim. Their objects are working-class consciousness, reformism, bureaucracy, revolutionary strategy, and electoral organization. Each gives social position and collective practice causal force [F5, unverified] [F6, unverified] [F7] [F8, unverified] [F9]. The focal article shares that orientation when it calls for a deepened account of class formation and class struggle, but it neither cites those analyses nor translates their institutional categories into feudal history. The relation is therefore parallel continuity across distinct objects, not demonstrated inheritance of specific claims.

## What the focal text resolves

| earlier tension | focal answer | left open | finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| The 1977 critique established that trade cannot transform class relations but did not fully expose the circularity of trade-led transition models. | Competitive pressure reliably compels innovation and selection only after producers have become dependent on the market for reproduction. Using that mechanism to explain capitalism therefore assumes capitalist conditions. | Whether Cohen’s functionalist alternative can connect productive forces to fitting property relations without the same circularity. | [F11] [F17] |
| The 1978 reply demanded the conditions under which primitive accumulation succeeds in some places and fails in others. | Transition is redefined as the transformation of relations between peasants possessing their means of reproduction and lords extracting surplus through extra-economic compulsion. | The article does not explain how attempts to reproduce these feudal positions bring about their dissolution. | [F12, unverified] |
| The earlier class-conflict explanation sat uneasily beside a productive-forces theory in which property forms adapt to technical development. | The focal text assigns these propositions to incompatible models: the first deprives class struggle of independent causal significance, while the second requires class formation and struggle. | No completed historical account identifies which conflicts and outcomes create capitalist property relations. | [F18] |

The focal text’s strongest resolution is negative. The earlier claim that trade cannot determine the transformation of class relations becomes an explanation of why: competitive production compels generalized innovation only when actors already lack direct access to their means of reproduction. The developmental mechanism attributed to trade is therefore not transhistorical. It is an effect of the capitalist relations whose emergence it is being asked to explain [F11].

This also clarifies the variable effects observed in the earlier historical studies. Where producers retain their means of reproduction, exchange need not be compulsory; failure to innovate need not threaten survival; and existing property relations may constrain productive forces rather than automatically adapting to them. The focal article thus supplies a theoretical ground for the 1972 and 1976 findings that apparently similar economic pressures can produce different outcomes [F1] [F2]. The persistent claim is that economic opportunities do not explain their own realization; the new inferential step identifies compulsory market dependence as the condition making competitive selection systematic.

The second resolution is deliberately incomplete. The 1978 reply asked what, other than market expansion, made primitive accumulation possible in some places and unsuccessful in others. The focal text now identifies the proper object: feudalism consists in the conflicted reproduction of possessing peasants and surplus-extracting lords, and transition means the breakup of those constitutive relations [F12, unverified]. This resolves the problem’s formulation, not its history. The article says that Marx “barely posed and never solved” the resulting problem and calls for a deeper account of class formation and struggle.

The division between Marx’s models also resolves a theoretical inconsistency. If productive forces necessarily call forth suitable property relations, class struggle can only mediate or accelerate an independently determined succession. If property relations instead structure how classes reproduce themselves, conflict can affect both the persistence and transformation of the system. By declaring the models incompatible, the focal text gives conceptual coherence to the earlier proposition that divergent outcomes of class conflict lie at the heart of transition [F18]. It does not yet connect particular conflicts to the creation of capitalist relations.

## Verdict — **qualified culmination**

The focal text is a **qualified culmination**, provided its object is stated narrowly: the 1972–78 critique of automatic commercial causation and the search for a class-relational account of the transition to capitalism [F19]. It gathers four earlier achievements into one theoretical architecture. From 1972 it inherits the social mediation of economic opportunity [F1]; from 1976, the priority of class and property relations in conditioning economic effects [F2]; from 1977, the distinction between market activity and capitalism [F3, unverified]; and from 1978, the demand that class formation, conflict, and primitive accumulation receive an autonomous explanation [F4]. It resolves their central negative problem by showing why the first model must “assume the transition in order to explain the transition” [F11], and it establishes reproduction through social-property relations as the alternative point of departure [F2] [F12, unverified].

It does not culminate the independent political route represented by the 1980–84 writings, whose analyses of reformism, consciousness, bureaucracy, organization, and electoral action remain outside the focal argument [F5, unverified] [F6, unverified] [F7] [F8, unverified] [F9]. Nor is it a completed positive theory. Cohen’s alternative is mentioned rather than answered [F17]; North and Thomas appear only as an analogue for mechanical property-rights adjustment [F16, unverified]; Marx’s second model is described as unfinished; and the decisive historical mechanism remains unresolved [F12, unverified]. The proper judgment is therefore: **conceptual completion of the critique, programmatic opening of the solution** [F19].
