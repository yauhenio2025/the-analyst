# Reading

## Position map

The 58-document supplied collection divides into five argumentative routes. The **merchant-history route**—*The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion* (1972) and *Merchants and Revolution* (1993)—argues that commercial opportunities acquire effects through merchant organization, investment, privilege, and political alliance; its qualification is that commerce remains causally important without acting automatically. The **transition route**—*Agrarian Class Structure* (1976), *Origins of Capitalist Development* (1977), *Reply to Sweezy* (1978), the focal *Marx’s First Model* (1985), *Agrarian Roots* (1985), *Social Basis of Economic Development* (1986), *Bourgeois Revolution* (1989), *Low Countries* (2001), *England’s Divergence* (2002), *Origins of Capitalism* (2006), *Property and Progress* (2007), *Feudalism* (2011), *Pre-history of Core–Periphery* (2011), and *From Theory to History* (2021)—makes social-property relations and actors’ rules for reproduction determine whether trade produces development or non-development; its qualification is that trade, demography, and technology are mediated, not irrelevant.

The **working-class politics route**—*A New Social Democracy?* (1980), the two 1981 right-wing essays, the Poland essay (1983), *Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?* (1984), *Paradox of Social Democracy* (1985), *Elections and the Left* (1986), *Problem of Reformism* (1993), *Teamster Tragedy* (1998), *Elections & the Democrats* (2004), *Rank-and-File Rebellion* (2010), *Introducing Catalyst* (2017), and *Seven Theses* (2022)—locates political capacity in independent collective activity rather than officials or elections alone; its qualification is that later texts permit electoral tactics and alliances without treating them as substitutes for organization.

The **profitability and political-economy route**—*Austerity & Interventionism* and *Deep Roots* (1986), *Crash and Crisis* (1988), *Regulation Approach* and the Iraq essay (1991), *Failing to Bring the State Back In* (1993), *Clinton’s Failure* (1995), *Global Turbulence* and *Looming Crisis* (1998), both 1999 replies, *Boom and Bubble* (2000), *World Economy* (2001), the five economic texts of 2002, *New Boom or New Bubble?* (2004), *Capitalist Economy* (2005), the 2006 *Economics of Global Turbulence* and imperialism essay, *Structure vs Conjuncture* (2007), *Goldman Sachs* (2009), *Escalating Plunder* (2020), *Seven Theses* (2022), and *Long Downturn* (2025)—explains stagnation through competition, fixed capital, overcapacity, and unrecovered profitability; its qualification is that institutions, class struggle, finance, and state policy remain effective within those constraints. Finally, the **bureaucratic-society route**—the 1989 Soviet contribution and the two 1991 Soviet/Eastern European essays—derives bureaucratic crisis from coercive surplus extraction and weak innovative compulsion; its qualification is that neither planning’s failure nor capitalist restoration is automatic.

All documents can be assessed at profile level, while only the focal text is available here in full. Same-year texts—including the focal article and the two other 1985 texts—cannot be developmentally ordered. The held collection is not Brenner’s complete oeuvre and omits several self-cited works and much of the interlocutor literature.

The pattern is not a simple replacement of one theory by another. The focal text **carries** Marx, Smith, *Capital*, the *Grundrisse*, and North–Thomas from the earlier transition debate, but changes their argumentative function. The 1976–78 essays used them to dispute demographic, commercialization, and world-system explanations directly; the focal article uses them to reconstruct why a commercialization account can appear Marxian. At the same time, it **drops the empirical archive**—Postan, Hilton, Wallerstein, Dobb, Pirenne, and most medieval agrarian evidence—that had supported the earlier comparative argument. Its first citations consequently form a strikingly concentrated **Marxological cluster**, not a new empirical literature.

For a reader choosing what to read next, the priorities differ. *The German Ideology* is essential because almost the entire negative reconstruction is built from it. *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations* is equally important because it supplies the positive contrast, yet it is not held. Meek is the most useful contextual secondary source because the focal text uses him to locate Marx and Smith within eighteenth-century four-stages history. Cohen is the clearest contemporary theoretical foil, but the focal text cites him only briefly. The *Manifesto*, *Poverty of Philosophy*, “Moralizing Criticism,” and “Guizot…” should be read as corroborating texts rather than as independent foundations of the argument.

## The focal shift in argumentative form

The substantive claim predates 1985. In 1977 the problem was stated directly: “The rise of trade is not at the origin of a dynamic of development because trade cannot determine the transformation of class relations of production” [F12]. The focal article reformulates this as a problem within Marx, asserting that the early model “must, at every strategic juncture, assume the transition in order to explain the transition” [F11]. The object changes—from rival transition theories and comparative outcomes to the internal architecture of Marx’s texts—but the inferential role persists: market mechanisms cannot explain the social conditions that make those mechanisms compulsory.

The focal article then gives the later Marx a different role from that found in the earlier essays. The 1976 essay had already said that property relations impose “rather specific long-term patterns” [F13]. Here, *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations* supports the stronger textual claim that pre-capitalist property relations reproduce actors as possessors and community members, so production cannot be assumed to be competitive production for exchange [F8]. This is partly a reargument and partly a genealogy of an established position—not an innovation in the substantive theory.

The article explicitly qualifies its own procedure: its critique of early Marx is “based on ‘hindsight’” [F9]. It reads the early texts through distinctions achieved in the later ones. That methodological asymmetry matters for deciding what to read: the early texts establish the object criticized, but the later-Marx materials supply the standards by which they are criticized.

## First citations: the Marxological dossier

The focal text’s major first citation is *The German Ideology*. It is not merely named: the article says that the first transition theory “was presented in Marx's earlier works from the German Ideology (1845-6)” and later that the theory “is laid out in its essentials in the German Ideology” [F1]. The next held text to reuse it is *The Social Basis of Economic Development* (1986), where the use shifts from reconstruction of early Marx to criticism of productive-force determinism.

*The Poverty of Philosophy* and the French-title record *Misère de la philosophie* support the same early-model dossier. The article uses the work’s proposition that historically fixed divisions of labour become bases of social organization [F3]. Because the packet splits the English and French records, only the French-title item is formally unheld; scholars should treat this as a bibliographic duplication, not as two independent arguments.

The *Communist Manifesto* is assigned a synthetic role: it “magistrally sketched” the early model [F4]. Yet the packet marks it unheld and records no later citation. It is therefore an action item, although the focal text’s detailed analysis depends much more heavily on *The German Ideology*.

Meek’s *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage* is the key first-cited contextual study. It is used for Smith’s four-stages theory and for the instability of commerce as both a separate stage and an activity within earlier stages [F5]. Meek is not in the Referee and the book is not held. Since the 1989 bourgeois-revolution essay returns to it, this is the highest-priority unexamined secondary citation.

“Moralizing Criticism and Critical Morality” and “Guizot…” connect the early economic model to its political superstructure. The former is cited among the later-1840s works that give more attention to political forms [F6]; the latter supports the proposed provenance in French conservative historiography [F7]. “Guizot…” never returns in the later supplied profiles, making it the most text-specific member of the cluster.

*Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations* is the central positive first citation. The focal article uses it to define pre-capitalist actors as reproducing themselves through possession and community rather than competitive exchange [F8]. Because it is not held and returns in the 1989 essay, acquiring it should be a priority comparable to Meek.

Cohen’s *Karl Marx’s Theory of History* is cited as a functionalist attempt to solve the problem of how productive forces bring about corresponding property relations [F9]. The citation is consequential but narrow: Cohen is not the article’s principal opponent in the way Smith is. The work is held, although Cohen is not in the Referee, and the 1986 theoretical essay returns to him.

Finally, the focal article cites Brenner’s 1977 “Origins of Capitalism” as the antecedent critique of Engels, Sweezy, and Wallerstein’s Smithian strategies [F10]. This self-citation makes explicit that the Marxological reconstruction builds on, rather than inaugurates, the earlier anti-commercialization argument.

## What is dropped

The principal disappearance is disciplinary. The focal article largely suspends the agrarian-historical and comparative literature that dominated the 1976–78 argument. Postan and Hilton had supplied opposing or supporting accounts of demographic cycles, serfdom, and peasant resistance; the focal article instead asks how Marx’s conceptual apparatus can pose transition coherently [F16]. Their absence is not abandonment: both return repeatedly in later transition studies.

Wallerstein is a more pointed drop. In 1977, world-system theory was the major target, and the essay opposed world-market incorporation to transformation of class relations [F17]. In 1985 the same causal objection is redirected toward early Marx’s assumption that exchange induces capitalist behaviour. Wallerstein returns only much later in the supplied transition profile, in *The Low Countries* (2001).

Dobb and the transition-debate collection also disappear despite their importance in 1977–78 [F18]. Their absence allows the article to frame the basic theoretical division as internal to Marx rather than as a contest among twentieth-century Marxists. Dobb returns in 1989.

Pirenne and the merchant-history literature disappear for a related reason [F19]. Earlier texts tested commercial explanations against social and regional evidence. The focal text instead traces the intellectual grammar of commercial explanation through Smith and early Marx. Pirenne reappears in 1986 and later in *Feudalism*.

## What remains unexamined and what to do

The desk should acquire or inspect four items first:

1. **Marx, *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations*** — held: **no**. It carries the focal article’s positive definition of pre-capitalist property and reproduction, not merely background evidence.
2. **Ronald Meek, *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage*** — held: **no**; in_referee: **no**. It supplies the article’s intellectual-historical bridge between Smith, four-stages theory, and Marx.
3. **Marx and Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*** — held: **no**. It is the focal text’s synthetic statement of the early model, though less heavily used than *The German Ideology*.
4. **Marx, *Misère de la philosophie*** — held: **no**. Check whether the held English *Poverty of Philosophy* fully resolves this duplicate-title gap.

Cohen’s book needs no acquisition because held is **yes**, but Cohen is **not in the Referee**; it warrants a separate thinker review if the aim is to evaluate the focal article’s treatment of functional explanation. Meek likewise warrants both acquisition and thinker review.

The focal text’s most consequential unexamined matter, however, is not only bibliographic. It announces itself as “Part I of a longer work” and says Marx’s second model “was never fully worked out by Marx” [F21]. The supplied text therefore gives a detailed criticism of the first model but only a programmatic sketch of the second. The collection shows later elaborations of that sketch, but it does not supply the announced continuation in which the textual case might have been made symmetrically.

## Judgment

Across the supplied collection, the focal text is a conceptual hinge rather than a substantive beginning. It brings in a new Marxological dossier—especially *The German Ideology*, Meek, Cohen, *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations*, and smaller early-Marx texts—while temporarily dropping the comparative agrarian historians and twentieth-century transition theorists central to the earlier debate. The carried citations change function: Marx and Smith cease to be simply authorities or opponents in a theory of development and become the two poles of a genealogy internal to Marxism.

For further reading, *The German Ideology* and *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations* are indispensable; Meek is the priority secondary source; Cohen is a useful but narrower foil. The empirical literature dropped here should not be treated as superseded, because later texts return to it when the argument moves back from conceptual genealogy to historical explanation.
