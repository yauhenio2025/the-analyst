# Reading

## Position map: the road to 1985

The supplied pre-1985 collection contains ten documents, all assessable: the focal text is available in full, while the nine earlier documents are represented by checked profiles and verified passages. *The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion, 1550–1650* (1972) argues that commercial opportunities acquire determinate effects only through socially and politically situated merchant groups; its distinctive role is to establish the earliest anti-automatic method, qualified by its continuing attribution of real causal importance to commerce [F1]. *Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe* (1976) places agrarian class structures and class power before demographic or commercial variables; it provides the comparative historical foundation, while qualifying structural constraint by making outcomes depend on conflict [F2]. *The Origins of Capitalist Development* (1977) converts that foundation into a general critique of Neo-Smithian Marxism: trade cannot create the class relations that make accumulation compulsory, although it can operate powerfully once those relations exist [F3]. *Reply to Sweezy* (1978) identifies the unresolved explanatory gap in trade-centred transition theory—the unspecified processes of class formation, conflict, and primitive accumulation—without itself supplying a complete general mechanism [F4]. *A New Social Democracy?* (1980) independently applies a relational method to working-class politics, arguing that socialist capacity arises through self-activity rather than parliamentary or bureaucratic substitution; its qualification is that it addresses political strategy, not transition historiography [F5]. The two 1981 texts cannot be ordered developmentally: *Reagan, the Right and the Working Class* explains rightward movement through material pressures and weakened collective organization, while refusing to reduce it to either ideology or economic crisis alone [F6]; *The Right Wing and the Working Class: A Response* sharpens that claim by insisting that worsening conditions do not automatically yield militancy, though its immediate object remains political consciousness [F7]. *Again on Reform or Revolution in Poland* (1983) locates the limits of reform in the bureaucracy’s material control of production and force, but concerns a bureaucratic regime rather than the origin of capitalism [F8]. *Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?* (1984) distinguishes electoral logic from the constitution of power through mass action, qualified by its strategic rather than historical-theoretical object [F9]. Finally, *Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism* (1985) culminates the transition route by explaining why the already-criticized commercial model recurs inside Marxism: early Marx’s causal sequence assumes market dependence before it can derive capitalist development, whereas later Marx’s property concept makes reproduction and class conflict analytically prior [F10–F12]. The five political texts therefore form a parallel continuity in relational method, not demonstrated sources of the focal argument.

Read as the end of this supplied road, the focal text is not the origin of the substantive position but its most reflexive formulation. The 1972–78 works had repeatedly shown that opportunity, demography, and trade do not determine their own effects; historically formed relations and organized actors do. The focal article turns that criticism back upon Marxian historical materialism itself. It reconstructs rather than merely presupposes the rejected chain, diagnoses its circularity—capitalist competitive mechanisms are invoked to explain capitalism—and derives from later Marx a reproduction-centred alternative. In that limited sense it is a culmination: it supplies the conceptual genealogy and internal critique that the earlier empirical and polemical arguments lacked. It is not a complete resolution, because it explicitly leaves unexplained how feudal actors, reproducing themselves through feudal relations, transform those relations; indeed, the article calls this the problem Marx “barely posed and never solved” [F17]. Nor does it culminate the separate political sequence of 1980–84, whose relation to the focal text is analogical rather than genealogically established. This judgment covers only the ten supplied documents—nine of sixty-one earlier items plus the focal text—not the whole oeuvre; no later text is used.

The inheritance is substantive but also revisionary. The focal text does not merely repeat that trade is insufficient. It reconstructs the precise causal chain it rejects—productive forces, division of labour, mental/manual division, class and property—and asks under what conditions that sequence would actually operate. Its answer is that it operates systematically only where producers already depend upon competitive exchange [F15]. Thus the 1977 proposition is reargued at a more abstract level rather than silently presupposed. Likewise, primitive accumulation is no longer only the historical process missing from Sweezy’s account: it becomes the theoretical test separating two historical materialisms [F16].

The political texts of 1980–84 contribute a recognizable parallel proposition: conditions do not produce political outcomes automatically; organization, power, strategy, and collective activity mediate them. The 1981 response says that consciousness is shaped by actual conditions [F7], but the companion article makes collective defeat and sectional organization decisive [F6]. The Poland essay similarly derives reform’s limit from the bureaucracy’s material position [F8], while the Democratic Party essay distinguishes electoral aggregation from the construction of organized power [F9]. Yet the focal article neither cites these texts nor takes working-class strategy as its object. Shared relational logic is therefore evidence of methodological continuity across independent argument routes, not secure inheritance.

## What it resolves—and what it leaves open

| Earlier tension | Answer in the focal text | Left open | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| The 1972 account showed that opportunity is socially constituted but did not offer a general theory of why market opportunity transforms some systems and not others. | Property relations specify actors’ access to reproduction and therefore whether exchange is optional or compulsory. | The article does not reduce merchant organization to a direct application of the later Marxian model. | [F1], [F14] |
| The 1976 comparative argument established that similar trade and demographic pressures yield divergent outcomes, but its general theoretical warrant remained dispersed across cases. | The focal text explains that pre-capitalist possession permits reproduction outside compulsory competition, so trade cannot by itself generalize innovation or dissolve property forms. | The historical origins of particular property settlements still require concrete explanation. | [F2], [F21] |
| The 1977 critique established that trade cannot create capitalist class relations, but not why trade-centred reasoning repeatedly appears Marxian. | Early Marx’s transition model is reconstructed as Smithian: it attributes capitalist motivation and mechanisms to pre-capitalist producers once exchange appears. | The article’s retrospective procedure depends explicitly on concepts from later Marx. | [F3], [F10], [F22] |
| The 1978 reply demanded an account of the conditions under which primitive accumulation succeeds in some places and fails in others. | The later property-relations model identifies what must be transformed: direct producers must lose non-market access to their means of reproduction and become compelled to exchange. | It does not explain how feudal lords and peasants actually bring about that transformation; this becomes the newly sharpened problem. | [F4], [F16], [F17] |
| Earlier accounts placed class conflict at the centre but risked leaving its relation to structural reproduction under-theorized. | Feudalism is redescribed as the conflicted reproduction of possessing peasants and surplus-extracting lords, so conflict is internal to the mode rather than an external political accelerator. | No general solution is given to the collective-action or “prisoners dilemmas” generated by actors’ attempts to preserve existing relations. | [F20], [F23] |

The strongest resolution is therefore negative and conceptual. The focal text explains why exchange cannot bear the causal burden assigned to it: its developmental effects depend upon prior separation from the means of reproduction. This closes the logical gap in the first model by showing it to be circular rather than merely empirically inadequate. It also resolves the status of property relations. In the first model they are downstream expressions of productive forces; in the alternative they are antecedent relations that define how classes reproduce themselves [F14].

The positive resolution is deliberately incomplete. Once trade is denied the capacity automatically to dissolve pre-capitalist property, transition becomes harder rather than easier to explain. The article identifies the explanandum—transformation of lord-peasant property relations through the activity of feudal actors—but does not supply a general mechanism. Its achievement is to pose the problem sharply, not to solve its historical content [F17], [F23].

## Interlocutors and changed argumentative field

The principal inherited interlocutors are Marx and Smith. Marx had earlier supplied concepts of free wage labour, primitive accumulation, and class relations; here the text disputes the first model while building on the later property-relations model [F18]. Smith, previously the comparator for Neo-Smithian transition theory, now becomes the genealogical source of early Marx’s error [F19]. Sweezy and Wallerstein are inherited but displaced interlocutors: they appear near the conclusion as Marxists who adopted Smithian strategies, not as the focal article’s principal objects [F24]. North and Thomas are also inherited and qualified into a contemporary analogy: the early model can be read as approaching the property-rights school’s account of adaptive contractual forms [F25]. G. A. Cohen is a new interlocutor within the supplied pre-focal corpus, mentioned as offering a functionalist route to productive-force primacy [F26]. Postan, Hilton, Dobb, and the earlier agrarian archive are absent; because the article changes object from comparative outcomes to textual reconstruction, their absence does not establish rejection.

## Verdict: **culmination**

The focal text is a **culmination of the supplied transition agenda**, not of every earlier route [F27]. It culminates the sequence from socially conditioned opportunity (1972), through the priority of agrarian class structure (1976), the critique of Neo-Smithian development (1977), and the demand for a specified account of primitive accumulation (1978). Its distinctive accomplishment is to show that these earlier objections disclose two incompatible historical materialisms within Marx: one in which productive forces and exchange generate appropriate property relations, and another in which property relations organize reproduction and must themselves be historically transformed. But it is a critical and programmatic culmination rather than a completed theory. The article announces itself as Part I, says Marx’s second model was never fully worked out, and leaves the actual transformation of feudal property relations unresolved [F11], [F17]. The political writings of 1980–84 remain a parallel relational agenda, not something brought to completion here.

### Counter-evidence

- The focal article is not a completed synthesis. It states that the second model “was never fully worked out by Marx” and identifies itself as “Part I of a longer work to be published separately.” Its positive theory is consequently less developed than its criticism of the first model.
- Calling it a culmination must be restricted to the transition route. Nothing in the focal text resolves the strategic controversies over social democracy, working-class consciousness, Poland, or the Democratic Party.
- The 1972 relation is methodological rather than directly textual: the focal article does not cite the merchant-history essay. Its treatment as inheritance is therefore medium-confidence and should not be mistaken for demonstrated self-borrowing.
- The distinction between early and later Marx is explicitly constructed through “hindsight.” The focal text preserves an early-Marx passage recognizing that only generalized world-market competition assures productive-force permanence, which complicates a fully homogeneous first model.
- The focal article resolves the logical insufficiency of trade-centred explanation but not the comparative historical question posed in 1976 and 1978: why transformation succeeds in some places and fails in others.

### Open questions

1. Does the announced longer work survive under another title, and would it provide a symmetrical reconstruction of the second model?
2. How far can the 1972 account of socially constituted commercial opportunity be integrated with the later social-property framework without erasing the specificity of merchant organization?
3. Does the focal text’s sharp early/late division survive a fuller reading of the *Grundrisse*, *Capital*, and the early works in context?
4. Is Cohen’s functionalist account fairly characterized as pursuing “the same end,” or does the brief note suppress relevant distinctions?
5. Exactly how should class formation and class struggle solve the “prisoners dilemmas” created when feudal actors depend upon preserving the relations whose transformation must be explained?
6. The supplied sample omits fifty-one of sixty-one pre-focal documents. Would those missing texts establish direct links between the political and transition routes, or confirm that they remained independent applications of a relational method?
