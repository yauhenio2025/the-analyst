## The paper read backwards: what it culminates

Read backwards, em:CBT7B8CL/1985 culminates the 1972–78 formation of a class-relational account rather than founding it. The 1976 article had already argued that property relations impose “specific long-term patterns” on development; the focal text recasts that causal ontology as a contrast between two Marxisms, defining the later one through social-property relations that structure reproduction. Its object is therefore not newly discovered class causation. Its distinctive act is **theoretical self-clarification**: it turns the earlier rejection of demographic and commercial automatism into an internal reconstruction of Marx.

The continuity is particularly strong on transition. The 1977 article had already denied that trade could transform class relations, while the 1978 reply asked what conditions made primitive accumulation succeed unevenly. The focal text sharpens this into the question of how feudal actors, reproducing themselves feudally, transform feudal property relations. Thus its retrospective status is **culmination**: it codifies and discriminates between the premises already operating in the earlier historical argument.

The qualification is methodological. Earlier texts mainly compare historical outcomes or criticize trade-centred explanations; the focal paper instead reads Marx’s early and later works against one another. Its claim that there are “two fundamentally different historical materialisms” makes this a genuine reorientation in argumentative route, though not a rupture in causal ontology.

## The paper read forwards: what it seeds

Read forwards, the paper is a **way station**, not the unique origin of the mature framework. Its most direct sequel is em:LJFTS6KY/1989, *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism*, which resumes the two-model distinction and applies it to the English Revolution: the textual reconstruction becomes a historical reinterpretation of agrarian capitalism and the “landlord shell”.

Its most important prospective achievement is to pose, rather than solve, the mechanism of endogenous transition. em:ATQRKQMC/2001 answers in general form that capitalist social-property relations emerged from feudal actors carrying out feudal rules for reproduction; em:PWVE5QPN/2006 and em:C3E3GHEL/2007 repeat and systematize that answer [F5–F6]. These later texts do not merely repeat the focal paper’s vocabulary: they change its inferential role. What is an announced historical task in 1985 becomes a positive causal proposition and then a formal chain from property relations through reproductive rules to aggregate development and crisis.

The same relational grammar travels beyond transition history. The Soviet writings explain economic dynamics through class-property relations; the mature crisis writings explain how individually compulsory cost-cutting reproduces aggregate overcapacity and low profitability [F7–F8]. This is best treated as a **transfer or reargument**, not proof of direct inheritance: the objects differ, and most later crisis texts do not reproduce the focal article’s genealogy of early Marx.

The focal route also becomes less central than its positive framework. Later transition syntheses retain social-property relations, market dependence, rules for reproduction, and unintended consequences, but the extended discussion of individuation, communal property, and craft–manufacture–machinofacture largely recedes. Accordingly, the focal text seeds a durable problem and conceptual grammar, but its distinctive Marxological apparatus has selective uptake.

## The paper read whole: the joined verdict

Read whole, em:CBT7B8CL/1985 is a **middle-period reorientation and deepening**: retrospectively a culmination of the 1972–78 class-relational transition argument; prospectively a way station toward explicit rules-for-reproduction and unintended-transition accounts; and, as a rupture verdict, a reorientation in method rather than a break in substantive theory.

What **persists** is the claim that neither trade, technology, demography, institutions, nor opportunities determine development independently of historically formed social-property relations and the strategies those relations make rational. What **breaks** at the focal text is the immediate evidentiary route: comparative history and contemporary controversy give way to internal reconstruction of two Marxian historical materialisms [F1–F3].

What changes later is not attributable to 1985 alone. Crisis theory makes inter-capitalist competition the immediate mechanism of recession and recovery, creating tension with the focal paper’s call for a deepened appreciation of class formation and class struggle. Still later, political capitalism gives state-backed rents and asset appreciation greater conceptual autonomy, while explicitly placing the new circuit alongside rather than in place of productive capitalism. Those are stronger later revisions than anything occurring at the focal boundary.

The collection’s overall position is therefore: **formation before 1985; codification, historical specification, and transfer after it; late reorientation toward political capitalism**. The focal paper is the hinge at which an existing historical programme becomes self-conscious about its Marxological alternatives and states its hardest unresolved question.
