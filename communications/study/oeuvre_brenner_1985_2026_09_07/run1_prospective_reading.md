# Reading from 1985 onward

Read without any earlier work behind it, *Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism* begins with a double move. It rejects an early-Marxian sequence in which productive forces and exchange generate appropriate class and property relations, because that sequence works regularly only when capitalist market dependence already exists; and it retrieves from later Marx an alternative in which social-property relations organize reproduction and must themselves be transformed. The article’s decisive question is therefore not why commerce expands but how actors trying to reproduce feudal relations unintentionally dissolve them. Later writings make this a durable agenda. They develop its social-property framework into comparative histories of Europe, the Low Countries, England and the Yangzi delta; they answer its unresolved transition problem with the mechanism of unintended consequences generated by feudal reproduction and class conflict; and they extend its distinction between market-dependent and non-market reproduction to Soviet bureaucratic collectivism and modern capitalist competition. A separate political-economic route applies the broader principle that individually rational responses can reproduce aggregate crisis. Yet no later supplied text completes the focal article’s announced detailed reconstruction of Marx’s two models. The focal text is consequently an **origin** of a later, multi-field relational agenda in this supplied post-1985 corpus—but an origin of its conceptual grammar, not of every later empirical thesis.

Most later documents do **not** take up the focal article as a whole. The Soviet, long-downturn, imperialism and political-strategy writings instead borrow or independently reargue parts of its causal architecture. Calling those texts sequels would confuse transfer of a framework with continuation of the focal textual argument.

## What falls away

- The extended reconstruction of progressive **individuation** from tribal communal property to private property does not return as a major explanatory object in the supplied later texts. The nearest possible sequel, *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism* (1989), returns to the two models but concentrates on feudalism, agrarian capitalism and revolution rather than rebuilding this genealogy [F17].
- The focal text’s derivation of early Marx’s sequence through three stages—**craft, manufacture and machinofacture**—is not developed later in the supplied sample. Later writings retain the critique of productive-force determinism but abandon this detailed stage reconstruction [F18].
- The suggestion that the **family is the ultimate social atom** of early Marx’s mechanical individualism disappears. Later analyses use households and families as actors governed by property-specific rules, not as the foundation of a universal historical sequence [F19].
- The announced project of fully clarifying both Marxian models is not completed as such. Later works elaborate the second model and repeatedly reuse the criticism of the first, but no supplied document offers the symmetrical “Part II” promised by the focal article [F20].

## Verdict: **origin**

The focal text is an **origin** of the later social-property/reproduction agenda represented in this sample. It originates neither the later empirical histories nor every branch of Brenner’s politics and crisis theory. Its originating role is narrower and stronger: it formulates a portable causal grammar—property relations establish rules of reproduction; those rules structure actors’ strategies; aggregate outcomes can differ from intentions; and transformation cannot be inferred from opportunities, productive forces or markets alone. Later texts turn that grammar into comparative transition history, Soviet class analysis, capitalist crisis theory and analyses of state dependence. At the same time, they largely abandon the focal article’s detailed Marxological scaffolding. The position across the collection is therefore one of **conceptual expansion with textual narrowing**: the positive framework becomes increasingly general and historically operational, while the reconstruction that introduced it becomes a specialized point of departure.

### Counter-evidence

- *The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism* shares much of the mature framework in 1985, but same-year dating prevents establishing whether it follows the focal article.
- The focal article itself derives its positive framework from later Marx; “origin” therefore means origin within the supplied post-1985 trajectory, not first invention of the concepts.
- Only *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism* clearly takes up the focal article’s two-model argument as a whole. Most later continuities concern its positive framework rather than its complete textual thesis.
- The long-downturn theory gives competition greater immediate explanatory weight than the focal transition theory gives class struggle. This is a development of market dependence and unintended aggregation, not simple repetition.
- The political writings develop an anti-automatic relational method through organization and collective action, but their direct descent from the focal article is not textually demonstrated.
- “Abandonment” is limited to the supplied forty-eight later profiles. Absence from this sample cannot prove disappearance from the entire oeuvre.

4. How should the later priority given to inter-capitalist competition be reconciled with the focal demand for a deepened account of class formation and struggle?
