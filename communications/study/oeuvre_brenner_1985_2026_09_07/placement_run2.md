North, Douglass C. belongs with **New Institutional and Contract Theories of the Firm**, not with Brenner’s own transition tradition. In 1976’s *Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe*, North and Thomas represent the contractual account of serfdom rejected by the class-power argument. The 1985 focal text, *Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism*, explicitly compares early Marx’s individualist account with the “property rights school.” The same opposition is historically developed in 2001’s *The Low Countries in the Transition to Capitalism*, where market-responsive institutional evolution is rejected in favor of unintended transformations of social-property relations. The supplied 1991 *The Regulation Approach: Theory and History* does not contain an assessable citation of North despite the packet’s `cited_in` entry; its distinctive argument instead criticizes Regulation theory for subordinating capitalist social-property relations to institutions. North is therefore a strong addition as a recurrent opponent rather than an ally.

Thomas, Robert Paul belongs beside North in **New Institutional and Contract Theories of the Firm**. The 1976 essay treats their joint framework as a contractual reinterpretation of coercive agrarian relations; the 1985 focal text again invokes their joint work as the representative example of property-rights reasoning; and the 2001 Low Countries study opposes their price-, transaction-cost-, and enforcement-centered institutionalism to an explanation based on class-specific rules of reproduction. Thomas is cited less independently than North, but the recurrence across three substantive transition texts makes him a candidate in his own right.

Meek, Ronald does not warrant addition as a thinker around whom the Referee should organize a school. In 1985’s *Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism*, his *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage* supplies historical scholarship on Smith’s four-stages theory and commerce’s ambiguous status. In 1989’s *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism*, he appears only as a supporting reference while the essay restates the contrast between early Smithian Marx and the later social-property model. He is thus a specialist source used in passing, not an independently engaged position.

Cohen, G. A. fits **Marxology and the Critical Reading of Marx** as a sustained interpretive opponent. The 1985 focal text identifies his work as an alternative functionalist strategy for explaining why property relations adjust to productive forces. The 1986 *The Social Basis of Economic Development* makes that disagreement explicit: capitalist property relations generate the incentives and capacities for technical development, rather than arising because they functionally advance the productive forces. Cohen should therefore be added as a significant rival reconstruction of Marx, with the qualification that Brenner cites him as a foil rather than as an ally.
