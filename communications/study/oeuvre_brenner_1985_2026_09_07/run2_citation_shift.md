# Reading

## Position map and scope

The supplied collection comprises 58 dated documents from 1972 to 2025. It is substantial but selective. It omits, among other writings named in the supplied profiles, **“Dobb on the Transition from Feudalism to Capitalism” (1978), “The Agrarian Roots of European Feudalism” (1982), “The Rises and Declines of Serfdom” (1996),** and **“Property Relations and the Growth of Agricultural Productivity” (1997)**. Only the focal article is supplied in full; the other 57 documents are assessable through checked profiles and verified passages. Documents sharing a year cannot be placed in developmental order.

### Transition, development, and pre-capitalist history

- **The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion, 1550–1650** (1972) explains divergent commercial projects through merchant groups’ social composition, resources, and political access; its distinctive role is to reject automatic market causation without denying commercial opportunity.
- **Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe** (1976) makes agrarian class power condition the effects of population and trade; it establishes the comparative property-relations thesis while retaining demographic and commercial forces as conditional causes.
- **The Origins of Capitalist Development** (1977) argues that trade cannot create the free-wage-labour relations that make capitalist accumulation compulsory; it generalizes the transition thesis against neo-Smithian Marxism while allowing trade to provide demand.
- **Reply to Sweezy** (1978) demands specification of the relations and tendencies of “pre-capitalist commodity production”; it sharpens the earlier critique but does not itself provide the complete positive transition model.
- **Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism** (1985) reconstructs two incompatible historical materialisms within Marx and argues that the first presupposes capitalist mechanisms in order to explain capitalism; it is explicitly Part I and says the second model was never fully worked out.
- **The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism** (1985) extends the property-relations explanation through comparative European history; because it shares the focal text’s year, it cannot be placed developmentally before or after it.
- **The Social Basis of Economic Development** (1986) formalizes property relations as rules for reproduction and capitalism as compulsory market dependence; it stresses that transition is ordinarily an unintended consequence.
- **Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism** (1989) applies the focal distinction to the English Civil War and agrarian capitalism; it rejects the traditional bourgeois-revolution thesis without making political transformation irrelevant.
- **Merchants and Revolution** (1993) integrates commerce, colonial production, religion, merchant alignment, and London politics; it rejects both a unitary bourgeois-versus-feudal conflict and reduction to contingent factions.
- **The Low Countries in the Transition to Capitalism** (2001) tests the property-relations model beyond England; Dutch development remains qualified by dependence on predominantly pre-capitalist European markets.
- **England’s Divergence from China’s Yangzi Delta** (2002) places divergence before 1800 in contrasting social-property relations; coal and colonial resources reinforce rather than initiate England’s trajectory.
- **The Origins of Capitalism** (2006) restates transition as the unintended consequence of feudal actors pursuing feudal reproduction; commercialization is indispensable but insufficient.
- **Property and Progress** (2007) supplies the mature causal chain from social-property relations to reproduction strategies, development, and crisis; it credits Smith with identifying real capitalist mechanisms while rejecting their universalization.
- **Feudalism** (2011) integrates legal, political, and socioeconomic definitions of feudalism; feudal crisis is systemic rather than merely demographic.
- **The Pre-history of Core–Periphery** (2011) argues that commercialization and non-development were normally compatible under pre-capitalist relations; external connections matter but cannot substitute for transformed property relations.
- **From Theory to History** (2021) rejects Mann’s millennium-long European Dynamic and argues that class and state were merged under feudalism; European divergence is therefore contingent rather than an uninterrupted ascent.

### Working-class politics and reformism

- **A New Social Democracy?** (1980) locates reformism’s limits in union officialdom and parliamentarism; social democracy nevertheless depends on the struggles it subsequently contains.
- **Reagan, the Right and the Working Class** (1981) explains workers’ rightward drift through crisis, weakened organization, and sectional material interests; it does not treat Reagan’s victory as a consolidated fascist breakthrough.
- **The Right Wing and the Working Class: A Response** (1981) defends that material account of political consciousness; it limits the claim to significant sections and warns that surveys cannot disclose consciousness by themselves.
- **Again on Reform or Revolution in Poland** (1983) argues that workers’ democracy required overthrowing bureaucratic rule; revolutionary strategy is defined as guidance for day-to-day politics, not merely insurrection.
- **Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?** (1984) distinguishes the logic of electoral victory from the construction of mass power; its rejection concerns Democratic electoralism as strategy rather than every electoral tactic.
- **The Paradox of Social Democracy** (1985) systematizes the contradiction between reformist organizations and the movements sustaining them; reformism is defined socially and strategically rather than solely by programme.
- **The Elections and the Left** (1986) allows electoral work when subordinated to independent mass organization; this expands tactical scope without abandoning political independence.
- **The Problem of Reformism** (1993) defines reformism through theory, method, and social base; possible rank-and-file conservatism does not erase officials’ distinct structural interests.
- **A View of the Teamster Tragedy** (1998) argues that progressive officials still require autonomous rank-and-file pressure; it rejects both personal-following politics and a conspiracy explanation of Carey’s fall.
- **Elections & the Democrats** (2004) permits critical lesser-evil support for a Democrat while preserving organizational independence; it revises electoral tactics, not the strategic judgment on the party.
- **Structure vs Conjuncture** (2007) interprets the 2006 Democratic victory as an anti-Bush conjuncture within a durable rightward shift; the Southern Republican project gives that shift a specifically American form.
- **The Political Economy of the Rank-and-File Rebellion** (2010) treats rebellion as a response to employer assault and bureaucratic accommodation; it does not make labour militancy the cause of the secular profitability crisis.
- **Introducing Catalyst** (2017) joins neoliberal legitimacy crisis to the need for durable socialist organization; movement autonomy becomes debilitating when it turns into isolation.
- **Seven Theses on American Politics** (2022) describes parties as fiscal coalitions of divided working-class fractions under political capitalism; class structure underdetermines class politics.

### Capitalist crisis and the long downturn

- **Austerity & Interventionism** (1986) links military Keynesianism and intervention to industrial decline; temporary demand support does not repair productive weakness.
- **The Deep Roots of U.S. Economic Decline** (1986) explains relative American decline through weak investment, fixed capital, latecomer advantages, hegemony, and finance; it is not yet the full system-wide downturn theory.
- **Notes on the Crash and Crisis** (1988) broadens the object to a world profitability crisis; debt postpones depression but does not restore profitability.
- **The Regulation Approach** (1991) subordinates institutional regimes to capitalist social-property relations and competition; it disputes institutional primacy rather than institutional effects.
- **Failing to Bring the State Back In** (1993) shows finance defeating Clinton’s minimal industrial reconstruction; the proposed intervention was itself modest and market-oriented.
- **Clinton’s Failure & the Politics of U.S. Decline** (1995) embeds Clinton’s defeat in low profitability and transformed class forces; finance’s victory also reflects the absence of organized opposition.
- **The Economics of Global Turbulence** special issue (1998) explains the long downturn through competition, uneven development, manufacturing overcapacity, and falling profitability; direct class struggle affects distribution but is not assigned the deepest rhythm of crisis.
- **The Looming Crisis of World Capitalism** (1998) applies that theory to the international crisis; Keynesianism is credited with making the downturn milder while prolonging it.
- **Competition and Class** (1999) defends competition as the immediate accumulation and crisis mechanism; exploitation remains the source of surplus value.
- **Reply to Critics** (1999) clarifies fixed capital, uneven development, and failed adjustment; worker power can cause cyclical squeezes without explaining decades of low profitability.
- **The Boom and the Bubble** (2000) distinguishes a real manufacturing recovery from a later equity bubble; the boom was neither wholly fictitious nor a new secular economy.
- **The World Economy at the Turn of the Millennium** (2001) treats the American revival as real but not demonstrably durable; unresolved overcapacity supplies the negative presumption.
- **An Economy of Two, Three Many Enrons** (2002) interprets corporate fraud as a systemic effect of bubble growth and deteriorating returns; individual corruption remains real but insufficient.
- **Competition and Profitability** (2002) defends the competition mechanism against Zacharias; it is a narrow theoretical and empirical reply rather than a new synthesis.
- **The Boom, the Bubble, and the Future** (2002) traces the downturn to profitability, overcapacity, and the bubble’s reversal; its outlook remains conditional.
- **The Economy After the Boom** (2002) explains recession through weak profitability, collapsing investment, and reversal of the wealth effect; borrowing can still support temporary recovery.
- **The Trajectory of the Manufacturing Profit Rate** (2002) defends the sectoral profit-rate comparison against Duménil and Lévy; its scope is measurement rather than the entire crisis theory.
- **New Boom or New Bubble?** (2004) describes a debt- and housing-driven two-track economy; it does not claim uniform contraction.
- **The Capitalist Economy, 1945–2000** (2005) defends profitability and structural constraints against agency-centred objections; constraint is not mechanical determination.
- **The Economics of Global Turbulence** book (2006) consolidates the long-boom/long-downturn account; institutions, states, and policies mediate rather than cancel profitability dynamics.
- **What Is Good for Goldman Sachs Is Good for America** (2009) joins productive weakness to asset-price Keynesianism and shadow banking; finance amplifies and substitutes for accumulation rather than supplying an autonomous first cause.
- **Escalating Plunder** (2020) interprets pandemic rescue as state-supported predation; predation increasingly conditions production but does not wholly replace it.
- **The Long Downturn and Its Political Results** (2025) derives secular stagnation from capitalism’s uncoordinated competitive dynamism and identifies a state-backed asset circuit; that circuit emerges alongside, not instead of, productive capitalism.

### Bureaucratic societies and imperial power

- **Untitled contribution on the class nature of the Soviet Union** (1989) characterizes bureaucratic collectivism as politically organized surplus extraction; it can generate extensive growth while failing at intensive transformation.
- **Soviet Union–Eastern Europe, Part II** (1991) distinguishes restoration of private property from successful capitalist development; the Soviet outcome remains open and politically blocked.
- **The Soviet Union & Eastern Europe, Part I** (1991) explains planning failure through bureaucratic social relations rather than planning as such; democratically planned socialism remains a possible alternative.
- **Why Is the United States at War with Iraq?** (1991) explains the Gulf War as hegemonic projection amid relative economic decline; imperial action may be counterproductive even for capitalist interests.
- **What Is, and What Is Not, Imperialism?** (2006) explains imperialism through capital-dependent states in a multiple-state system; interstate reactions can produce outcomes contrary to every national capital’s interests.

Across these routes, the focal text occupies the middle of the transition agenda. The 1976–78 writings had already rejected automatic commercialization. The focal article changes the argumentative object: rather than primarily confronting agrarian historians or neo-Smithian Marxists, it locates the conflict within Marx’s own writings. The subsequent transition texts formalize and apply that reconstruction.

The pattern is a **bibliographic refocusing**, not a substantive abandonment. In the earlier transition argument, Marx supplied the distinction between capitalist free-wage-labour relations and pre-capitalist class systems. In the focal article, Marx becomes an internally divided object: “There are two Marxian models of the transition from feudalism to capitalism” [F6]. Smith changes in parallel. The earlier argument rejects trade as incapable of transforming class relations; the focal article says the first Marxian model “mirrors rather closely Adam Smith’s classic account” [F7]. The dispute is therefore reargued at a new level: an external criticism of neo-Smithian transition theory becomes an internal reconstruction of Marx’s two historical materialisms.

No consequential drop can be stated with the required textual anchoring. The citation records show that Postan, Hilton, Dobb, Wallerstein, and several works used in earlier transition writings are absent from the focal text. But the available earlier-profile anchors do not reproduce source sentences carrying both their identities and their argumentative roles. Their absence is therefore a bibliographic fact left open here, not a supported interpretation of what the silence accomplishes.

## What enters and how to read it

The first-citation pattern is narrower than the focal text’s conspicuous concentration on early Marx might suggest. *The German Ideology* and *The Communist Manifesto* occur in earlier profiles, so their prominence here is a changed use rather than a first citation. The securely new primary citation is *The Poverty of Philosophy*, used to delimit the early model through 1847 [F1]. The article also first cites *Moralizing Criticism and Critical Morality* as evidence for the greater political emphasis of Marx’s later-1840s writings [F5].

These two texts should be read together. *The Poverty of Philosophy* tests whether the productive-forces/division-of-labour sequence persists beyond *The German Ideology*. *Moralizing Criticism and Critical Morality* tests whether increased attention to political conflict alters that sequence or merely adds political forms to a still mechanically determined development. The focal text’s claim is not simply that early Marx neglected politics; it is that politics and class struggle lack independent causal efficacy when productive forces already determine the succession of property forms.

Cohen is the most consequential new secondary interlocutor. The note identifies *Karl Marx’s Theory of History: A Defence* as “an attempt to provide an alternative strategy to the same end through the use of functionalist explanation” [F2]. Cohen therefore matters not as evidence about Marx’s wording but as a possible answer to the focal article’s objection. If productive forces do not directly generate appropriate property relations, can functional explanation account for why relations facilitating productive development are selected? The 1986 **Social Basis of Economic Development** returns to Cohen, so this first citation begins a continuing theoretical engagement rather than an isolated aside [F2].

Meek opens a different reading route. *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage* is first cited to document the four-stages framework and the ambiguity of Smith’s commercial stage [F3]. Meek himself is first cited as the guide to that Enlightenment genealogy [F4]. This is a provenance claim distinct from the focal article’s immanent causal criticism. A reader could accept the criticism—that the first model presupposes compulsory market dependence—while remaining unconvinced that its genealogy is adequately described as Smithian or four-stages. Meek should therefore be read to test intellectual descent, not as the principal evidence for the article’s logical objection.

## What is carried and transformed

Marx is carried, but the focal article does not merely repeat the earlier appeal to him. In 1977, Marx supplies the distinction between capitalism as “the class system of production based on free wage labour” and pre-capitalist class systems. In 1985, the object becomes Marx’s own division into two models [F6]. The earlier use takes Marxian class categories as the basis for criticizing neo-Smithian Marxists; the focal use asks which Marxian historical materialism can sustain those categories.

Smith’s role changes just as sharply. Previously, the rejection of trade as the cause of transformed class relations made Smith the external representative of an inadequate market-centred mechanism. In the focal article, Smith becomes the diagnostic key for interpreting early Marx: the first model is said to mirror Smith’s account [F7]. The focal text thus does not simply oppose Marx to Smith. It uses Smith to oppose one Marx to another.

This change is inferential, not merely verbal. The first model begins from productive forces, exchange, and the division of labour and treats class and property as their effects. The second begins from property relations that structure how producers and exploiting classes reproduce themselves. Its historical problem is consequently harder: if pre-capitalist agents seek to preserve their established forms of reproduction, the text must explain how their actions nevertheless dissolve those relations. Later writings formalize this as rules for reproduction and unintended consequences, but the focal article itself acknowledges that the second model “was never fully worked out by Marx.”

## What remains unexamined

The highest-priority acquisition is *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations*. The focal text uses the material published under that title for its positive later-Marxian account: property relations connect direct producers to their means of production and to one another in ways that permit continuing reproduction [F10]. Without reading those passages directly, one can test the criticism of the first model but not adequately evaluate the positive textual basis attributed to later Marx.

The next priority is *The Communist Manifesto*. It is not a first citation in the supplied collection, but it is unheld and is assigned the important role of “magistrally” sketching the first transition model [F8]. It should be compared with the held *German Ideology* and *The Poverty of Philosophy*, not treated as an interchangeable witness.

Meek’s *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage* should follow the primary Marx reading. It is unheld and supports both the four-stages framework and the interpretation of Smith’s commercial stage [F9]. Meek is also outside the Referee, so the dossier has not independently assessed the thinker whose work carries this genealogical burden [F11].

Cohen’s person-level record is likewise outside the Referee [F12], although the cited book itself is held [F2]. Reading the book is necessary to determine whether the focal article has undermined productive-forces primacy generally or only the direct and Smithian mechanisms by which productive forces are made to generate fitting property relations.

The resulting reading order is:

1. ***Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations*** — to test the positive later-Marxian model [F10].
2. ***The Poverty of Philosophy*** and ***Moralizing Criticism and Critical Morality*** — to test the extension and political qualification of the early model [F1] [F5].
3. ***The Communist Manifesto*** alongside the held *German Ideology* — to test the focal article’s compact reconstruction of bourgeois development [F8].
4. **Cohen, *Karl Marx’s Theory of History*** — to assess the functionalist alternative [F2] [F12].
5. **Meek, *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage*** — to assess the Smithian and four-stages genealogy [F3] [F9] [F11].

Across the supplied collection, the focal article is best judged a **theoretical hinge**. It neither originates the anti-commercialization thesis nor supplies its final formulation. Its distinctive bibliographic and argumentative achievement is to convert an earlier dispute with trade-centred Marxists into an argument about Marx’s own divided historical materialism. The later transition writings retain that reconstruction while returning to comparative history. Across the broader collection, the same methodological position is repeatedly extended to new objects: opportunities, techniques, institutions, policies, and ideologies do not explain their own realization; their effects depend on the social relations through which actors reproduce themselves.

### Counter-evidence

- The focal concentration on early Marx is not wholly new. *The German Ideology* and *The Communist Manifesto* occur in earlier profiles, so their prominence here cannot establish first citation.
- The article limits the positive side of its reconstruction: "The second of these models was presented in the Grundrisse (1857-8), Capital (1867-1883), and other later works, but was never fully worked out by Marx." — doc: focal:em:CBT7B8CL
- Its projected comparison is incomplete: "This article is Part I of a longer work to be published separately." — doc: focal:em:CBT7B8CL
- The article acknowledges its retrospective method: "This section is, frankly, based on “hindsight”." — doc: focal:em:CBT7B8CL

### Open questions

- The available anchored evidence cannot support interpretive rows for consequential drops, although the citation records establish that several earlier interlocutors are absent.
- The packet cannot establish a developmental sequence between the focal article and *The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism*, both dated 1985.
- Cataloguing splits between translated and original-language titles complicate work-identity claims, especially for *The Poverty of Philosophy* and *Misère de la philosophie*.
- The omitted 1978, 1982, 1996, and 1997 writings may alter the chronology of the movement from anti-commercialization critique to the explicit “rules for reproduction” formulation.
- The focal article cannot by itself settle whether its Smithian genealogy of early Marx is historically correct; that requires direct reading of Meek and the relevant Enlightenment literature.
