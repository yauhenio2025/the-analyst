# Reading

## Verdict and halves

*Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism* (1985) does **not** mark an epistemic rupture. It is a **deepening and temporary exegetical reorientation** within an established social-property problematic. Before 1985, the historical works had already made class/property relations and class conflict—not trade, demography, or technical advance in themselves—the determinants of development and transition; the political writings likewise explained reformism, consciousness, and strategy through social position and collective action. The focal article changes the immediate object and method by reconstructing two incompatible Marxisms, foregrounding early Marx’s Smithian model, and posing more sharply how feudal society transforms its own constitutive relations. After 1985, comparative history returns, while the positive argument is formalized through rules of reproduction, unintended consequences, and political accumulation. The two useful halves are therefore **1972–1984: formation and application of class-relational explanations in commercial, agrarian, transition, and political analysis** and **1985–2025: explicit codification, comparative enlargement, and extension across feudalism, capitalist crisis, imperialism, and political capitalism**. The focal text is the hinge of codification, not the framework’s origin: it says that Marx “enables us to pose the problem of transition in an unprecedentedly sharp manner,” language appropriate to problem-sharpening rather than theoretical replacement [F1].

## Position map of the collection

All fifty-eight supplied documents can be assessed: the focal article is supplied whole; the other fifty-seven are represented by checked profiles containing verified passages. Except where a passage is quoted, the formulations below report what those profiles record rather than reproducing the texts’ wording. Same-year works are not assigned an internal sequence.

### Formation before 1985

*The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion, 1550–1650* (1972) argues that merchant groups’ social and political character shaped their responses to commercial opportunities. Its distinctive role is to begin with historically specific actors rather than self-acting market incentives; opportunity and profitability still matter, but only through the groups capable of exploiting them.

*Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe* (1976) makes agrarian class structure determine the effects of demographic and commercial change. It establishes the comparative agrarian programme; capitalist development requires a specific outcome of class conflict, not merely peasant freedom or growing trade [F2].

*The Origins of Capitalist Development* (1977) generalizes the argument: trade and production for profit do not create capitalism without transformed class relations, market dependence, and free wage labour. Its distinctive role is the direct critique of neo-Smithian Marxism; world-market demand remains consequential once mediated by property relations [F3] [F5].

*Reply to Sweezy* (1978) presses trade-centred transition theory to specify the relations and conflicts of “pre-capitalist commodity production.” It is a polemical clarification rather than a new programme; distinguishing feudal decline from capitalist rise is declared explanatorily insufficient [F9].

*A New Social Democracy?* (1980) explains reformist restraint through the social position of trade-union officials and parliamentary actors. It opens the political-strategic route; social democracy can arise from mass struggle even though its officials subsequently contain that struggle [F6].

*Reagan, the Right and the Working Class* (1981) explains workers’ rightward movement through crisis, sectional interests, and weakened collective organization. Its role is to connect material conditions to consciousness; it does not treat the Reagan vote as uniform ideological conversion.

*The Right Wing and the Working Class: A Response* (1981) defends this materialist account against explanations centred on manipulated or simply false consciousness. Its methodological qualification is explicit: voting and survey evidence cannot alone establish workers’ consciousness.

*Again on Reform or Revolution in Poland* (1983) treats bureaucratic control of production, surplus, and state force as incompatible with workers’ democracy. It extends class-relational analysis beyond capitalist society; revolutionary strategy is presented as a framework for daily policy, not merely a plan for insurrection.

*Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?* (1984) distinguishes electoral logic from the logic of mass direct action and rejects strategic reliance on the Democratic Party. It sharpens political independence; its claim concerns strategic use of the party, not political abstention.

### The focal reorientation and immediate codification

*Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism* (1985) reconstructs an early Smithian Marx, in whom productive forces and exchange determine property, against a later Marx for whom social-property relations structure reproduction. Its distinctive contribution is genealogical and diagnostic: it gives an explicit Marxian lineage to an argument already present in the 1976–78 works. Its central qualification is incompleteness. The article is “Part I,” and its conclusion poses rather than solves the transformation of feudal relations [F1] [F11].

*The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism* (1985) returns to comparative European history and explains divergent feudal crises through social-property relations, class organization, and political accumulation. It consolidates the empirical programme; demographic and commercial pressures retain mediated effects. Because it shares the focal year, its relationship to the focal article cannot be ordered chronologically [F12].

*The Paradox of Social Democracy* (1985) systematizes the contradiction between reformism’s dependence on mass movements and its officials’ tendency to contain them. It develops the political route independently of the focal Marx exegesis; limited reforms and concessions remain possible.

*Austerity & Interventionism* (1986) connects military Keynesianism and intervention abroad to domestic industrial decline. Its role is to join contemporary political economy to strategic analysis; deficit stimulus can delay contraction while worsening productive weakness.

*The Deep Roots of U.S. Economic Decline* (1986) explains relative US manufacturing decline through investment, fixed capital, late development, and the costs of hegemony. It begins the later long-downturn route; relative US decline is distinguished from world-economic crisis.

*The Elections and the Left* (1986) allows electoral work where it is subordinated to independent movement-building. It qualifies, rather than abandons, the pre-focal criticism of electoralism; independent candidacies may have educational value.

*The Social Basis of Economic Development* (1986) provides the most immediate theoretical codification by linking property relations to rules of reproduction and long-term development or non-development [F7]. It moves beyond the focal article’s exegesis but retains its central problem: capitalist transition must be an unintended rather than designed outcome [F8].

*Notes on the Crash and Crisis* (1988) embeds the 1987 crash in a longer profitability crisis and explains why debt postpones rather than resolves depression. It begins the mature crisis analysis; finance is neither autonomous nor causally irrelevant.

*Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism* (1989) most directly takes up the focal two-model reconstruction. It applies the Smithian/social-property contrast to the English Civil War and argues that a mechanically determined transition renders bourgeois revolution theoretically unnecessary [F3]. Rejecting the traditional thesis does not mean political conflict lacked consequences.

The untitled 1989 contribution on the Soviet Union treats bureaucratic collectivism as a property system based on political exclusion and extra-economic extraction. Its role is to generalize laws-of-motion analysis across modes of production; analogy with pre-capitalism does not identify the Soviet Union as feudal.

### Extension across Soviet transition, capitalist institutions, and politics

*Soviet Union–Eastern Europe, Part II* (1991) distinguishes restoration of capitalist property from successful capitalist development. Marketization and privatization may generate dislocation and underdevelopment rather than convergence.

*The Regulation Approach* (1991) gives capitalist social-property relations and competition explanatory priority over historically variable institutional regimes. Institutions still matter, but within structural constraints.

*The Soviet Union & Eastern Europe, Part I* (1991) derives bureaucratic crisis from the social relations governing information, labour, and surplus rather than from planning as such. Democratic planning remains conceptually possible.

*Why Is the United States at War with Iraq?* (1991) explains the Gulf War through hegemonic projection amid relative economic decline. Oil remains a background interest but not a sufficient explanation.

*Failing to Bring the State Back In* (1993) explains the defeat of Clinton’s minimal interventionism through finance and free-market political power. Its distinctive role is to analyse the political limits on state-led renewal; the proposed programme was itself modest.

*Merchants and Revolution* (1993) returns to the 1972 object and reconstructs commercial and political transformation through differentiated merchant groups, privileges, and alliances. It expands the earlier history while rejecting both an undifferentiated bourgeois-revolution model and a purely contingent political account.

*The Problem of Reformism* (1993) defines reformism through its theory, political method, and organizational social basis rather than through programme. It systematizes the route opened in 1980; progressive officials may be tactical allies but cannot ground strategy [F6].

*Clinton’s Failure & the Politics of U.S. Decline* (1995) relates austerity and party politics to low profitability, industrial decline, debt, and the altered composition of US capital. Finance is decisive within a broader structural transformation.

*A View of the Teamster Tragedy* (1998) gives organizational specificity to workers’ self-activity by making autonomous rank-and-file organization indispensable. Progressive officials remain conditional allies subject to independent pressure [F6].

### The long-downturn programme

*The Economics of Global Turbulence* (1998) explains the long downturn through uneven development, competition, fixed capital, overcapacity, overproduction, and falling profitability. It establishes a major later agenda distinct from the focal history of transition. Class struggle affects distribution and the labour process but does not by itself rule the deepest cycle.

*The Looming Crisis of World Capitalism* (1998) applies that account to the international crisis. It treats neoliberalism and finance principally as consequences and amplifiers of productive stagnation; Keynesianism both mitigates and prolongs the downturn.

*Competition and Class* (1999) makes competition the immediate mechanism compelling cost reduction and labour-process transformation [F5]. It extends the market-dependence argument into mature capitalist dynamics; exploitation remains the source of surplus value.

*Reply to Critics* (1999) defends uneven development and explains persistent low profitability through fixed capital, insufficient exit, and continued entry. It leaves open a new upturn after sufficient destruction or reallocation of capacity.

*The Boom and the Bubble* (2000) distinguishes a genuine recovery of manufacturing profitability from later speculative expansion. The 1990s recovery is not dismissed as wholly fictitious.

### Mature comparative transition theory and crisis analysis

*The Low Countries in the Transition to Capitalism* (2001) contrasts peasant possession with market-dependent farming and explains capitalist relations as emerging from feudal actors’ attempts to follow feudal rules of reproduction [F4]. Dutch capitalism remained limited by its predominantly pre-capitalist environment.

*The World Economy at the Turn of the Millennium* (2001) asks whether the long downturn has ended and finds a real but fragile US boom. Crisis is a substantial risk, not an inevitability.

*An Economy of Two, Three Many Enrons* (2002) treats corporate fraud as a systemic result of bubble-driven accumulation, weak profitability, and deregulation. Individual corruption is acknowledged but does not carry the explanation.

*Competition and Profitability* (2002) technically defends international competition as the source of the 1965–73 profitability decline. Its scope is narrower than the whole long-downturn thesis; short-run wage effects remain possible.

*England’s Divergence from China’s Yangzi Delta* (2002) globalizes the social-property comparison by contrasting English market dependence with Yangzi peasant possession. It places divergence before 1800; coal and colonial resources may reinforce but do not initiate it.

*The Boom, the Bubble, and the Future* (2002) synthesizes the profitability-centred account and presents slow growth or stagnation as the likely medium-term outcome. The prediction remains probabilistic.

*The Economy After the Boom* (2002) explains recession through overcapacity, weak profits, and collapsed investment and names “stock-market Keynesianism” as a debt-supported substitute for productive revival. Consumption may postpone contraction.

*The Trajectory of the Manufacturing Profit Rate* (2002) defends the sectoral evidence for international competitive pressure. It is a methodological intervention about measurement and aggregation rather than a restatement of the entire theory.

*Elections & the Democrats* (2004) combines political independence with tactical Democratic voting against the greater evil. This revises electoral application, not the priority of independent mass movements.

*New Boom or New Bubble?* (2004) interprets recovery as increasingly driven by housing, household debt, and asset prices. It extends the bubble analysis while acknowledging some profitability improvement.

*The Capitalist Economy, 1945–2000* (2005) defends profitability and structural constraint against explanations centred on unconstrained agency or state power. Structure conditions rather than mechanically determines action.

The 2006 book version of *The Economics of Global Turbulence* consolidates the long-boom, long-downturn, and failed-adjustment account. Both Keynesianism and monetarism have contradictory effects.

*The Origins of Capitalism* (2006) states the positive transition thesis in its clearest form: capitalist relations arise as the unintended consequence of feudal actors pursuing feudal ends [F8]. Commercialization is indispensable but insufficient.

*What Is, and What Is Not, Imperialism?* (2006) explains imperialism through capitalist state dependence and multiple-state interaction. It restricts “accumulation by dispossession” to the political creation of capitalist conditions; uncontrolled interstate reactions may make state action diverge from aggregate capitalist interests.

*Property and Progress* (2007) codifies the mature causal chain from social-property relations through reproduction rules to developmental patterns and crises [F2]. It retains Smith’s mechanisms where capitalist relations make them compulsory. It also systematizes political accumulation as the feudal analogue of capital accumulation [F10, unverified].

*Structure vs Conjuncture* (2007) places the 2006 election within long-term changes in accumulation, labour power, and party organization. It integrates political and economic routes while retaining the significance of conjunctural repudiation.

### Finance, feudalism, and political capitalism

*What Is Good for Goldman Sachs Is Good for America* (2009) explains the financial crisis through long-term productive stagnation, asset-price Keynesianism, and financial fragility. Finance amplifies and transmits crisis rather than solely originating it.

*The Political Economy of the Rank-and-File Rebellion* (2010) explains labour rebellion through profitability crisis, employer attack, and bureaucratic failure. It joins the pre-focal self-activity thesis to the long-downturn account; militancy did not initiate the profitability decline.

*Feudalism* (2011) integrates legal, political, and economic definitions and develops the lord–peasant relation into a theory of state-building, political accumulation, and systemic crisis. Demographic adjustment remains subordinate rather than irrelevant [F10, unverified].

*The Pre-history of Core–Periphery* (2011) argues that commercialization and non-development were compatible and mutually reinforcing under pre-capitalist property relations. England and the northern Netherlands remain exceptional capitalist cases.

*Introducing Catalyst* (2017) defines neoliberalism as austerity combined with politically driven upward redistribution. It synthesizes later political and economic conclusions; neither autonomous protest nor electoralism alone is adequate.

*Escalating Plunder* (2020) interprets pandemic bailouts as state-enabled corporate predation and upward redistribution. It supplies empirical material for political capitalism; the bailout is not treated as productive restructuring.

*From Theory to History* (2021) criticizes Michael Mann’s separation of political and economic power by making feudal extraction and state-building a merged process. It extends political accumulation while rejecting both autonomous-state and unilinear-European explanations [F10, unverified].

*Seven Theses on American Politics* (2022) names “political capitalism” as a low-growth configuration in which political power increasingly determines returns and parties mobilize materially divided working-class fractions. Class structure underdetermines political alignment.

*The Long Downturn and Its Political Results* (2025) joins persistent low profitability to political capitalism, rent-oriented circuits, and working-class fragmentation. Political capitalism remains an emerging configuration alongside productive capitalism, not a replacement mode of production.

## The line of development

The decisive evidence against rupture lies before the focal text. In 1976, the object is already the historically formed class structure that regulates the consequences of demography and commerce: “It is the structure of class relations, of class power” that determines their long-term effects. In 2007, the same explanatory commitment appears in more formal language: “The causal chain runs from historically specific, politically reproduced social property relations” through reproduction rules to aggregate patterns and crises [F2]. The later text does not merely repeat a vocabulary; it converts an earlier comparative proposition into an explicit multi-level method. What persists is the direction of explanation—from property and class relations to actor strategies and aggregate development—while what changes is the degree of formalization.

The focal anti-Smithian argument is likewise anticipated. The 1977 critique states that trade cannot determine transformed relations of production; the 1989 chapter states that commerce alone cannot promote or explain transition between modes [F3]. The former passage’s object is trade’s causal power in capitalist origins; the latter’s is transition between modes of production. Both exclude exchange as an autonomous solvent of pre-capitalist relations. The focal text therefore does not introduce the anti-commercialization thesis. It reconstructs an internal Marxian genealogy for it, showing why an early division-of-labour model presupposes capitalist mechanisms in order to explain capitalism.

There is nevertheless genuine development. The 1977 argument identifies divergent outcomes of class conflict as the heart of transition. The 2001 text specifies that capitalist relations must result from feudal actors attempting to follow feudal rules of reproduction [F4]. The focal text stands between these formulations: it defines the task as explaining how lords and peasants, while reproducing themselves feudally, transform the relations sustaining them. Yet it acknowledges that the problem remained unsolved. The later unintended-consequence thesis is therefore neither a rupture nor a claim fully present before 1985. It is a solution to a pre-existing question whose form the focal article sharpens [F8].

The same pattern holds for market dependence. The 1977 claim concerns the social condition under which producers must meet socially necessary labour time; the 1999 claim concerns competition’s systematic compulsion to intensify work and control the labour process [F5]. The later argument presupposes capitalist relations and asks how their dynamics operate. It extends transition theory into accumulation and crisis theory rather than replacing one problematic with another.

The political writings provide an independent control case. The 1980 text makes movement independent of social-democratic officialdom necessary; the 1998 Teamsters analysis makes autonomous rank-and-file organization indispensable even under a relatively progressive official [F6]. The later work supplies organizational specificity, but the strategic method persists. Because this route crosses 1985 without taking Marxian exegesis as its object, it confirms that the focal text did not reorganize the entire oeuvre.

What changes at the focal point is chiefly presentation and interlocution. The 1976 work argues through comparative European outcomes; the 1986 text gives an abstract property-to-rules-to-development sequence [F7]. Between them, the focal article turns to Marx’s texts to distinguish two historical materialisms. This is a real reorientation, but a temporary one: comparative history continues in the same-year *Agrarian Roots*, while the 1986 chapter formalizes the same causal problem. Since the two 1985 works are unordered, no direction of influence can be inferred from publication year or packet order [F12].

The interlocutor shift is similarly limited. Sweezy’s trade-centred account had already been connected to Smith before 1985; later work again criticizes Smith for universalizing mechanisms that operate only under particular social-property relations [F9]. Early Marx is a newly foregrounded textual object, but Smithianism, commercialization, and the conditions of capitalist development remain the controversy. The focal article changes the route through the argument rather than its destination.

“Political accumulation” is a more substantive enlargement, but it does not occur at the focal text. The 1976 account already explains feudal outcomes through power and force. Later theory explicitly names state-building as the feudal analogue of capitalist accumulation [F10, unverified]. This gives the feudal ruling class a positive rule of reproduction and links lordly extraction to warfare and state formation. Because the focal article does not itself systematize that concept, the innovation cannot warrant periodizing the oeuvre at the focal article as a rupture.

## Judgment across the collection

Across the supplied collection, the position is continuous but progressively differentiated. The earliest historical works reject automatic responses to economic opportunities and locate causal power in historically formed social groups, property relations, and class conflicts. The focal article provides the clearest Marxian genealogy for that position and formulates the outstanding transition problem at a higher level of precision. Subsequent works divide into several routes: comparative transition history, capitalist competition and crisis, bureaucratic collectivism, political strategy and reformism, imperialism, and political capitalism. These are not all direct inheritances of the focal argument. The long-downturn corpus presupposes capitalist market dependence but develops an independent explanation through profitability, fixed capital, uneven development, and overcapacity. The political corpus retains a pre-focal commitment to self-activity and organizational independence. Their coexistence limits any claim that 1985 inaugurated an entirely new oeuvre.

For periodization, 1985 is nevertheless useful as a **hinge of explicit self-codification**. Before it, the programme is already operating in comparative history and political analysis. At it, the programme receives an explicit distinction between Smithian and social-property historical materialisms. After it, that distinction is formalized, comparatively enlarged, and applied across modes of production and contemporary capitalist dynamics. The final 2025 position still makes capitalism’s competitive property relations both the source of technological dynamism and, through uncoordinated investment, the source of stagnation. That is an enlargement of the earlier framework, not its displacement.

The collection is extensive but not complete. It covers fifty-eight texts from 1972 to 2025 but excludes twelve of the sixty-one selected documents. It also lacks the focal article’s announced continuation and referenced intermediate studies on serfdom and agricultural productivity. The verdict therefore applies to the supplied collection; it cannot prove that no omitted text contains an earlier or intermediate formulation.

### Counter-evidence

- The focal article makes an unusually explicit conceptual distinction: "There are two Marxian models of the transition from feudalism to capitalism." — doc: focal:em:CBT7B8CL. This supports treating 1985 as a codifying hinge, although pre-focal texts already contain the substantive alternative.
- The focal text calls the models “entirely incompatible,” which might suggest rupture at the level of Marx interpretation. The oeuvre-level evidence nevertheless shows that Brenner’s preferred side of that incompatibility was operating before 1985.
