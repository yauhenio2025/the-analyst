# Reading

The focal text does **not** mark an epistemic rupture. It is best periodised as a **deepening**—with a temporary Marxological reorientation—inside a class-relational problematic already explicit by 1972 and fully articulated by 1976–78. Before 1985, the historical writings already reject automatic responses to trade, make class/property relations and class conflict decisive, distinguish market exchange from capitalist compulsion, and define transition as the transformation of social relations [E1.F1–E1.F4]. The focal text does something important but narrower: it reconstructs these commitments as a choice between two incompatible Marxian historical materialisms and sharpens the unsolved question of how feudal reproduction unintentionally transforms itself [E2.F1–E2.F2]. After 1985, that reconstruction is occasionally resumed, especially in 1989, but the oeuvre chiefly returns to historical explanation and generalizes the pre-existing framework across comparative development, Soviet society, capitalist crisis, state action, and working-class politics [E1.F5–E1.F7]. The defensible halves are therefore **1972–84: formation of class-relational history and independent class politics** versus **1985–2025: theoretical codification, comparative extension, and transfer to capitalist crisis and political capitalism**. This is a useful periodisation, but not a rupture at the focal text: the dividing line marks expansion and systematization rather than replacement.

## Why this is deepening rather than rupture

The strongest evidence against rupture is chronological. The focal article’s positive commitments are already asserted in 1976–78: class relations impose developmental limits; trade cannot transform those relations; capitalist compulsion depends on specific social relations; and transition requires an account of class conflict and primitive accumulation. The focal text itself acknowledges the continuity by citing the 1977 critique, but changes the evidentiary route. It reconstructs Marx’s texts to explain why one model is circular and why another can pose the transition problem. That is an innovation in **method and interlocutors**, not in the central object or causal ontology.

Nor does the post-1985 corpus divide cleanly from the pre-1985 corpus. *The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism* continues the comparative historical route in the focal year; *The Paradox of Social Democracy* continues the political route; and *The Social Basis of Economic Development* formalizes the property/reproduction chain. Later texts repeatedly return to pre-focal objects: *Merchants and Revolution* expands the 1972 merchant study, the mature transition writings revisit the 1976–78 controversy, and the political writings continue arguments formed in 1980–84. The focal Marxological apparatus itself has much narrower uptake: *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism* clearly reargues it, whereas most later texts presuppose or independently restate the positive social-property framework.

The post-focal corpus nevertheless forms a recognizable second half. Its distinctive agenda is **generalization**. The transition mechanism is specified as the unintended consequence of property-specific reproduction; the same relational grammar is extended to bureaucratic collectivism; market dependence becomes the basis of capitalist competitive compulsion; and individually rational reproductive strategies explain aggregate overcapacity and stagnation. Finally, political-capitalism texts add state-supported rent and asset circuits. These are genuine revisions and transfers, but they accumulate over decades rather than occurring at the focal text.

The best period judgment is therefore: **1985 is a hinge of theoretical self-clarification, not a rupture**. Periodisation at this point is useful if its halves are called “formation” and “codification/extension,” but misleading if it implies that the social-property problematic began there.

### Counter-evidence

- The focal text does introduce a genuinely new **argumentative route** in the supplied corpus: sustained internal comparison of early and later Marx. If periodisation privileges interlocutors and textual method over causal ontology, “reorientation” would be defensible.
- *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism* takes up the focal two-model argument directly, so the focal text is not an outlier.
- The packet dates *The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism* to the same year as the focal article. Its mature social-property analysis prevents any claim that the focal text demonstrably caused the post-1985 formulation.
- The 1977 text’s emphasis on free wage labour is narrower than later accounts of market-dependent capitalist farmers. This is a conceptual revision, but the supplied evidence does not locate it uniquely at the focal text.
- The crisis theory’s claim that competition rather than class struggle governs the deeper rhythms of recession creates real tension with the focal call for a “deepened appreciation” of class formation and struggle. That tension emerges in the 1990s, not at the 1985 boundary.
- Political capitalism may prove a more substantial late reorientation than the focal article, especially if state-backed rents acquire causal autonomy. The 2025 text nevertheless explicitly presents the new circuit as emerging “alongside” productive capitalism.
