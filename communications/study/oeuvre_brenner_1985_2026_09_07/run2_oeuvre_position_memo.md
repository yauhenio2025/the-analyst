# Reading

## Compact position map

The supplied collection contains fifty-eight assessable documents spanning 1972–2025. The focal article is held whole; the other fifty-seven are represented by checked profiles with verified passages. The descriptions below report what those profiles record, except where quotation marks identify verified wording. Same-year works are not placed in compositional order. The collection is substantial but not complete: most importantly, it does not contain the continuation announced by the focal article [F11].

### Formation before 1985

- **em:VAYFENVQ/1972, *The Social Basis of English Commercial Expansion, 1550–1650***: commercial opportunities acquire effects through the social and political character of merchant groups. Its distinctive role is actor-centred commercial history; opportunity remains causally relevant, but conditionally.
- **em:27CQX9UR/1976, *Agrarian Class Structure and Economic Development in Pre-Industrial Europe***: class relations determine how demographic and commercial forces affect development. It establishes the comparative agrarian programme; capitalist development requires a particular settlement of class conflict, not peasant freedom or trade alone [F8].
- **em:HZHLWZ2R/1977, *The Origins of Capitalist Development: A Critique of Neo-Smithian Marxism***: trade cannot create capitalist development without transformed class relations and market dependence. It generalizes the anti-commercialization thesis; world markets still matter through the relations they encounter [F2].
- **em:9VI9FSBK/1978, *Reply to Sweezy***: transition theory must specify social relations, class formation, and conflict. Its role is polemical clarification; it does not deny that feudal decline and capitalist rise can be distinguished, but denies that the distinction explains either process.
- **em:BHRWPT7F/1980, *A New Social Democracy?***: reformist restraint follows from officialdom’s social position and parliamentary method. It opens the political-strategic route; social democracy may arise from mass struggle even while its officials later contain that struggle.
- **em:TGWH6ZPU/1981, *Reagan, the Right and the Working Class***: crisis, weakened organization, and sectional interests explain the working-class drift rightward. It joins material conditions to consciousness; the Reagan vote is not treated as uniform ideological conversion.
- **em:6U35NCDN/1981, *The Right Wing and the Working Class: A Response***: consciousness responds to actual conditions and perceived practical alternatives. It defends the preceding materialist interpretation; voting and survey evidence cannot establish consciousness by themselves.
- **em:Q6WXW5PM/1983, *Again on Reform or Revolution in Poland***: bureaucratic control of production, surplus, and force is incompatible with workers’ democracy. It extends class-relational analysis beyond capitalism; revolutionary strategy is defined as a guide to daily policy, not merely insurrection.
- **em:SS9844LV/1984, *Can the Left Use the Democratic Party?***: electoral logic obstructs mass direct action. It sharpens political independence; opposition to using the Democratic Party strategically is not political abstention.

### The focal hinge and immediate codification

- **em:CBT7B8CL/1985, *Marx’s First Model of the Transition to Capitalism***: early Marx’s Smithian division-of-labour model and later Marx’s social-property model are incompatible. Its distinctive role is exegetical codification: it relocates an established anti-Smithian argument inside Marx’s oeuvre. Its decisive qualification is incompleteness—it is Part I and sharpens rather than solves the positive problem of transition [F1] [F10].
- **em:9RJCZ6KQ/1985, *The Agrarian Roots of European Capitalism***: divergent agrarian class structures explain divergent European responses to feudal crisis. It returns to comparative history; demographic and commercial pressures retain mediated effects. Because it shares the focal year, publication dates do not establish a compositional order between the two works.
- **em:HYA4MA32/1985, *The Paradox of Social Democracy: The American Case***: reformism depends upon yet contains the mass movements that sustain it. It systematizes the political route; collective bargaining and reform can win short-term concessions while weakening independent organization.
- **em:3JSL5XP4/1986, *Austerity & Interventionism***: military Keynesianism and intervention temporarily sustain demand while deepening industrial decline. It connects economic policy to socialist strategy; stimulus is effective in the short run but self-undermining.
- **em:S52G8SSP/1986, *The Deep Roots of U.S. Economic Decline***: relative US manufacturing decline follows from weak investment, inherited fixed capital, late development elsewhere, and hegemonic costs. It opens the long-downturn route; relative US decline is distinguished from global crisis.
- **em:KUF7CP3S/1986, *The Elections and the Left***: electoral work is useful only when subordinate to independent movements. It qualifies the 1984 polemic; independent socialist candidacies can have educational value.
- **em:XTTG43Z5/1986, *The Social Basis of Economic Development***: property relations impose rules of reproduction whose aggregate operation produces development or non-development. It is the closest abstract development of the focal problem; capitalist transition remains unintended [F3].
- **em:3PBW7WA9/1988, *Notes on the Crash and Crisis***: the stock-market crash belongs to a longer profitability crisis. It introduces debt-mediated postponement; finance is neither autonomous nor inconsequential.
- **em:LJFTS6KY/1989, *Bourgeois Revolution and Transition to Capitalism***: the focal distinction undermines the traditional bourgeois-revolution interpretation of the English Civil War. It is the clearest direct historical uptake; rejecting that interpretation does not erase political consequences.
- **em:2VPKTHSN/1989, untitled contribution on the Soviet Union**: bureaucratic collectivism is a property system based on political exclusion and extra-economic extraction. It generalizes laws-of-motion analysis; resemblance to pre-capitalism does not make the USSR feudal.

### Soviet transition, institutions, and contemporary politics

- **em:IMRP3JQ7/1991, *Soviet Union–Eastern Europe, Part II***: capitalist restoration and successful capitalist development are distinct. It applies transition analysis to contemporary regimes; privatization may yield dislocation rather than growth.
- **em:D28G2RWK/1991, *The Regulation Approach***: capitalist social-property relations and competition constrain institutional regimes. It extends the framework to mature capitalism; institutions retain historical efficacy within those constraints.
- **em:GR426WC5/1991, *The Soviet Union & Eastern Europe, Part I***: bureaucratic planning fails because of its social relations, not because planning is intrinsically impossible. It supplies a non-capitalist crisis theory; democratic planning remains possible in principle.
- **em:K67KNHJC/1991, *Why Is the United States at War with Iraq?***: war serves hegemonic projection amid relative decline. It develops the imperial-political route; oil is a background interest rather than a sufficient cause.
- **em:8X5QYXZG/1993, *Failing to Bring the State Back In***: finance and free-market power defeat even minimal state intervention. Its role is structural political economy; Clinton’s programme was already modest.
- **em:ZH86SA7Z/1993, *Merchants and Revolution***: differentiated merchant groups and alliances connect commercial change to political transformation. It greatly expands the 1972 history; commerce matters without automatically dissolving inherited relations.
- **em:ZJHK954P/1993, *The Problem of Reformism***: reformism is defined by theory, method, and social basis rather than programme. It consolidates the pre-focal political route; progressive officials may be allied with but cannot ground strategy.
- **em:U42IYG2I/1995, *Clinton’s Failure & the Politics of U.S. Decline***: low profitability and industrial transformation condition finance’s political ascendancy. It develops the politics-of-decline thesis; finance is decisive within a broader structural change.
- **em:BD8RCVGB/1998, *A View of the Teamster Tragedy***: autonomous rank-and-file organization is indispensable even under progressive leadership. It gives organizational specificity to self-activity; progressive officials remain conditional allies.

### The long-downturn route

- **em:ZFPK9NTQ/1998, *The Economics of Global Turbulence***: competition, uneven development, fixed capital, and overcapacity produce persistent low profitability. It establishes a major independent programme; class struggle affects distribution but does not directly govern the deepest cycle.
- **em:L7CX4T2I/1998, *The Looming Crisis of World Capitalism***: finance and neoliberalism are principally consequences and amplifiers of productive stagnation. It applies the long-downturn argument to the 1998 crisis; Keynesianism both mitigates and prolongs stagnation.
- **em:4DYCDX3A/1999, *Competition and Class***: competition is the immediate compulsion behind accumulation and labour-process transformation. It extends the market-dependence argument; exploitation remains the source of surplus value.
- **em:A5KQX6MT/1999, *Reply to Critics***: sunk fixed capital and continued entry explain failed adjustment. It clarifies the persistence of stagnation; a new upturn remains possible after sufficient capacity destruction.
- **em:T33SBUEY/2000, *The Boom and the Bubble***: the 1990s combined genuine profitability recovery with speculative expansion. It formulates the boom/bubble synthesis; the recovery was not wholly fictitious.

### Mature comparative transition and crisis theory

- **em:ATQRKQMC/2001, *The Low Countries in the Transition to Capitalism***: capitalist relations emerge through feudal actors carrying out feudal reproduction strategies. It gives comparative historical form to the positive mechanism; Dutch capitalism remained constrained by a predominantly pre-capitalist environment [F4].
- **em:TAGBBUU9/2001, *The World Economy at the Turn of the Millennium***: the US boom was real but structurally fragile. It tests whether the downturn had ended; crisis remained a risk rather than a certainty.
- **em:F4ET2EQE/2002, *An Economy of Two, Three Many Enrons***: corporate fraud was systemic to bubble-driven accumulation. It specifies institutional manifestations; individual corruption is not denied.
- **em:KFEA7SRZ/2002, *Competition and Profitability***: international competition explains the 1965–73 manufacturing-profitability decline. It is a technical defence; short-run wage effects remain possible.
- **em:UYYQABRK/2002, *England’s Divergence from China’s Yangzi Delta***: divergent social-property relations produced divergent paths before 1800. It globalizes the comparative programme; coal and colonial resources reinforce rather than initiate divergence.
- **em:TJJZHZ6J/2002, *The Boom, the Bubble, and the Future***: overcapacity and falling profitability make stagnation the likely medium-term outcome. It synthesizes the crisis argument; the forecast is probabilistic.
- **em:TP2BBTZI/2002, *The Economy After the Boom***: excess capacity and weak profitability caused investment collapse. It identifies stock-market Keynesianism; consumption can postpone contraction.
- **em:CB8B4RIL/2002, *The Trajectory of the Manufacturing Profit Rate***: sectoral profit-rate evidence supports the competition account. It is a measurement intervention rather than a complete crisis theory.
- **em:FDEWCETE/2004, *Elections & the Democrats***: political independence can coexist with tactical Democratic voting. It revises electoral application; lesser-evil support is conditional rather than party allegiance.
- **em:DXBHHXIJ/2004, *New Boom or New Bubble?***: housing, debt, and asset appreciation drive the recovery. It extends the bubble analysis; some profitability recovery is acknowledged.
- **em:BHSWIJKN/2005, *The Capitalist Economy, 1945–2000***: profitability and structural constraints condition agency and state action. It answers agency-centred criticism; conditioning is not mechanical determination.
- **em:QP74AWIU/2006, *The Economics of Global Turbulence***: the long boom and long downturn follow the trajectory of profitability under competitive accumulation. It is the mature crisis statement; Keynesianism and monetarism have contradictory effects.
- **em:PWVE5QPN/2006, *The Origins of Capitalism***: transition is the unintended consequence of feudal agents pursuing feudal ends. It gives the focal question its clearest mature answer; commercialization is indispensable but insufficient [F5].
- **em:BJ4R4EPJ/2006, *What Is, and What Is Not, Imperialism?***: imperialism arises through capital-dependent states interacting in a multiple-state system. It restricts “accumulation by dispossession”; state policies may diverge from aggregate capitalist interests.
- **em:C3E3GHEL/2007, *Property and Progress***: social-property relations, reproduction rules, aggregate patterns, and crises form one causal chain. It codifies the mature programme; Smithian mechanisms are valid under capitalist conditions rather than universally [F6].
- **em:UFFBTFJW/2007, *Structure vs Conjuncture***: the 2006 election must be read against long-term changes in accumulation and class power. It joins political and economic routes; conjunctural repudiation still matters.

### Finance, feudalism, and political capitalism

- **em:32EZD9WF/2009, *What Is Good for Goldman Sachs Is Good for America***: the financial crisis joins productive stagnation to asset-price Keynesianism and financial fragility. It integrates finance into the long downturn; finance amplifies rather than solely originates crisis.
- **em:WNQLQHP7/2010, *The Political Economy of the Rank-and-File Rebellion***: profitability crisis, employer attack, and bureaucratic failure generate rebellion. It joins labour history to political economy; militancy did not initiate the profit decline.
- **em:S8AUYJ5G/2011, *Feudalism***: legal, political, and economic definitions of feudalism are integral. It develops the lord–peasant model into a theory of state-building and systemic crisis; demographic adjustment remains subordinate rather than irrelevant.
- **em:C8KU3XVS/2011, *The Pre-history of Core–Periphery***: commercialization and non-development were compatible under pre-capitalist relations. It broadens the comparative scope; England and the northern Netherlands remain exceptions.
- **em:7QSF5QUB/2017, *Introducing Catalyst***: neoliberalism combines austerity with politically driven upward redistribution. It draws strategic conclusions from the long downturn; neither isolated protest nor electoralism suffices.
- **em:NE7AJREG/2020, *Escalating Plunder***: pandemic intervention protects corporate predation and redistributes upward. It supplies material for political capitalism; bailout is not productive restructuring.
- **em:2887CIMF/2021, *From Theory to History***: feudal extraction and state-building form a merged political economy. It develops political accumulation against Michael Mann; both autonomous-state and unilinear-European explanations are rejected.
- **em:V68V5D49/2022, *Seven Theses on American Politics***: political capitalism makes state-mediated power increasingly important to returns and party formation. It reframes contemporary politics; class structure underdetermines political alignment.
- **em:VE9TU4JA/2025, *The Long Downturn and Its Political Results***: competitive investment produces both dynamism and secular stagnation, while political capitalism creates a state-backed rent circuit alongside productive accumulation. It is the collection’s latest synthesis; political capitalism is emerging rather than a replacement mode of production [F9].

## The paper read backwards: what it culminates

Read backwards, *Marx’s First Model* culminates an argument already established through historical controversy. The 1972 study had made reactions to commercial opportunity depend on merchant groups’ social and political character. The 1976 article made class power determine the effects of demographic and commercial change [F8]. Most decisively, the 1977 critique stated that trade cannot determine the transformation of class relations [F2]. The objects move from English merchants and comparative agrarian structures to Marx’s texts, but the inferential direction persists: opportunities, exchange, and productive forces cannot explain their own social conditions.

The focal paper’s distinctive contribution is therefore not the discovery of social-property explanation. It is an **exegetical codification** of that explanation. Its object is the coexistence of two incompatible transition models in Marx; its scope is the movement from feudalism to capitalism; and its diagnostic role is to show that the early model repeatedly assumes capitalist mechanisms—competitive production, market dependence, and self-transforming private property—in order to explain capitalism’s emergence. Its positive conclusion is narrower: later Marx “enables us to pose the problem of transition in an unprecedentedly sharp manner” [F1].

This backward reading also defines the paper’s limits. The political-strategic route—from *A New Social Democracy?* through the reformism, election, and rank-and-file writings—crosses 1985 without relying on Marx exegesis. Its continuing insistence on independent organization is a parallel application of class-relational explanation, not an inheritance demonstrably produced by this article. The focal text codifies one central historical-theoretical programme without reorganizing every branch of the oeuvre.

## The paper read forwards: what it seeds

Read forwards, the paper is a way station between an established negative argument and a later positive mechanism. Its outstanding question is precise: if commerce does not dissolve feudal relations automatically, how can lords and peasants transform those relations while attempting to reproduce themselves as feudal classes? Because the published text identifies itself only as Part I, it does not establish whether its own projected continuation supplied that answer [F10].

The immediate abstract development comes in em:XTTG43Z5/1986, *The Social Basis of Economic Development*. There the argument is formalized as a movement from property relations through actors’ rules for reproduction to long-term development or non-development [F3]. This is not merely repeated terminology. The focal article diagnoses why productive-force and exchange explanations fail; the 1986 chapter supplies the mediating level—socially rational reproduction strategies—through which property structures generate aggregate outcomes.

The historical mechanism then becomes explicit. em:ATQRKQMC/2001, *The Low Countries in the Transition to Capitalism*, says that capitalist social-property relations had to result from feudal actors carrying out feudal rules of reproduction [F4]. em:PWVE5QPN/2006, *The Origins of Capitalism*, states the result still more sharply as the unintended consequence of pursuing feudal goals in feudal ways [F5]. The 1977 formulation had located transition in divergent outcomes of class conflict; the later formulation explains how actions oriented toward reproducing an existing order can unintentionally undermine it. That is the principal conceptual deepening around which the focal paper stands.

The article also clarifies a contrast later used outside transition history: exchange is not the same thing as market dependence. Once capitalist social-property relations compel producers to exchange competitively, competition produces systematic innovation and labour-process transformation. The long-downturn writings presuppose this condition and ask about its contradictory aggregate effects—overinvestment, overcapacity, falling profitability, and failed adjustment. Their immediate object and evidential route differ from those of the focal article, so conceptual continuity does not establish direct descent [F7].

The same caution applies to political capitalism. The 2025 synthesis argues that capitalism’s productivity-enhancing investment dynamic can itself produce secular stagnation [F9]. That is compatible with the focal concern for unintended aggregate outcomes, but the supplied evidence does not show whether it was derived from the 1985 exegesis or independently developed within the broader social-property framework [F7].

## The paper read whole: joined verdict

Read whole, em:CBT7B8CL/1985 is best placed as a **middle-period exegetical reorientation, retrospective culmination, prospective way station, and theoretical deepening—not an epistemic rupture** [F1].

It is a **culmination** because the decisive anti-Smithian claim precedes it: trade cannot itself transform relations of production [F2]. It is a **reorientation** because it changes immediate method and object, moving from comparative causal history to sustained interpretation of early and later Marx. It is a **way station** because it sharply poses a problem that the held later texts formulate through rules of reproduction and unintended consequences [F3] [F4] [F5]. It is a **deepening** because the direction of explanation persists while the mediating mechanism becomes more explicit [F8].

What persists across the collection is not vocabulary alone. In the earlier historical work, class relations determine how demographic and commercial pressures affect development. In the mature codification, social-property relations impose rules of reproduction whose aggregate execution generates developmental patterns and crises [F8]. The object of inference remains the relationship between historically specific social structures, individually rational conduct, and collective outcomes.

What breaks is more limited. The focal text foregrounds Marx’s internal development and the incompatibility between two historical materialisms. Later works return to comparative history, extend comparison to the Low Countries and Yangzi delta, and develop distinct routes concerning bureaucratic collectivism, capitalist crisis, imperialism, political strategy, and political capitalism. Those routes do not all presuppose the focal paper. The political writings continue a pre-1985 argument about self-activity; the long-downturn works reargue capitalist competition through profitability and fixed capital; the political-capitalism writings extend analysis to state-mediated rents. Direct inheritance from the focal article remains unestablished [F7].

Across the supplied collection, the position is therefore continuous but increasingly differentiated. The earliest works reject self-acting opportunities and locate causal power in social groups, property relations, and class conflict. The focal article gives that position its clearest Marxian genealogy and its sharpest statement of the unresolved transition problem. Later works supply the reproduction-rule and unintended-consequence mechanism, expand the comparison, and carry related explanatory principles into other modes of production and contemporary capitalism [F1] [F8] [F9].

The most defensible periodization is consequently **1972–84: formation and application of class-relational explanation; 1985–2025: explicit codification, comparative enlargement, and extension** [F1]. This is a judgment about the supplied corpus. The unheld Part II could alter the focal paper’s prospective placement, and the collection cannot establish the chronology of formulations in omitted work [F10] [F11].

## Open question

The principal unresolved question is whether the announced Part II existed, circulated, or appeared under another title. If it contained the rules-of-reproduction and unintended-consequence mechanism, the focal project itself was closer to a mature culmination; if not, the published article remains chiefly a problem-sharpening way station [F10].

A second question concerns the later independent routes. The collection establishes compatibility between the focal concern with socially structured reproduction and the later account of capitalist investment producing stagnation, but it does not establish a compositional or citational line between them. Drafts, composition records, or explicit retrospective citations would decide whether the long-downturn and political-capitalism programmes inherited the focal argument or independently extended the broader social-property framework [F7].

### Counter-evidence

- The focal text makes a stronger and more explicit conceptual division than the preceding historical works: “There are two Marxian models of the transition from feudalism to capitalism.” — doc: focal:em:CBT7B8CL. This supports treating 1985 as a codifying hinge, though not as the framework’s origin.
- Its description of the models as “entirely incompatible” marks a rupture within its interpretation of Marx, but the supplied pre-1985 writings already occupy the social-property side of that division.
- The focal paper’s detailed reconstruction of early Marx’s progressive individuation largely disappears from the later sample. That disappearance concerns the criticized model, not abandonment of the article’s preferred problematic.
- The same-year *Agrarian Roots* already contains developed comparative history. Publication year and packet order cannot establish whether it applies the focal codification or was composed independently.
