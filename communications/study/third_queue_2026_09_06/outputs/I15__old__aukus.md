## Analysis

### Regulatory landscape

1. **REG1 — AUKUS trilateral defence and security framework**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** Australian, UK and US governments and defence agencies.  
   **Stated purpose:** Strengthen collective defence, deter China, improve supply-chain resilience and accelerate military innovation; the agreement aims to support Australia’s acquisition of “nuclear-powered submarines” under Pillar I and develop “quantum technologies, cybersecurity, hypersonic missiles, and artificial intelligence” under Pillar II.  
   **Scope:** Defence procurement, technology development, industrial collaboration and security coordination among the three AUKUS states.  
   **Key provisions:** Pillar I submarine acquisition; Pillar II advanced capabilities; coordinated defence-industrial policy; and integration of selected firms, investors and research institutions. The article’s broader interpretation is that AUKUS constitutes an “enmeshment of regulatory control, industrial and defence policy, and wider social relations”.

2. **REG2 — US export-control and AUKUS licensing reforms, including ITAR-related changes**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** US Congress, US executive agencies and the Department of Defense.  
   **Stated purpose:** Expedite defence trade and protect sensitive technologies while supporting trusted allies.  
   **Scope:** Controlled goods, military technology and sensitive dual-use technology moving among AUKUS countries.  
   **Key provisions:** An “exception from US export control licensing requirements” and reforms that “remove barriers to investment in sensitive technology”. The article reports that Australian firms such as The Whisky Project Group and Hypersonix Launch Systems benefited through US defence-innovation contracts.

3. **REG3 — Australia’s Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act 2024**  
   **Regulatory body:** Australian Government.  
   **Stated purpose:** Facilitate trusted defence trade and accelerate delivery of military capabilities.  
   **Scope:** Controlled goods and technology transferred among Australia, the UK and the US.  
   **Key provisions:** Transfers can occur “between AUKUS states without export licences”, thereby “expediting delivery of critical defence capabilities”.

4. **REG4 — US Defense Production Act Title III domestic-source treatment for Australia**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** US Congress and US executive agencies.  
   **Stated purpose:** Integrate secure critical-mineral supply chains supporting manufacturing, clean energy and defence.  
   **Scope:** Australian suppliers of critical minerals and associated technologies.  
   **Key provisions:** Australia was added as a “‘domestic source’ under Title III”, making Australian suppliers “eligible for US funds”.

5. **REG5 — Australian critical-minerals financing and industrial-support regime**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** Australian Government, Export Finance Australia, the Critical Minerals Facility and Northern Australia Infrastructure Facility.  
   **Stated purpose:** Support net zero, manufacturing, supply-chain resilience and defence capability.  
   **Scope:** Critical-mineral mining, processing, storage and related technologies.  
   **Key provisions:** AUD 2 billion expansion in financing, an AUD 6 billion Critical Minerals Facility, grants through the Critical Minerals Development Program, and project-specific public investment. The government’s financing was expressly linked to “supporting the Australian and United States’ manufacturing sectors”.

6. **REG6 — South Korean export-finance capital-limit regime and its 2023 amendment**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** South Korean National Assembly, government, Financial Services Commission and Eximbank.  
   **Stated purpose:** Enable financing of large defence-export packages and sustain “K-Defense”.  
   **Scope:** Eximbank financial support to foreign borrowing countries purchasing South Korean arms.  
   **Key provisions:** The prior law capped support to a borrowing country at “40 percent of its equity capital”; the government proposed an increase in the lender’s statutory capital limit, and the revised decree came into effect in December 2023.

7. **REG7 — Japan’s Three Principles on Arms Export and proposed revised guidelines**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** Japanese Government, Liberal Democratic Party and Komeito coalition.  
   **Stated purpose:** Historically constrain military exports; proposed reform seeks to expand defence-equipment transfers and industrial scale.  
   **Scope:** Japanese defence equipment and technology exports.  
   **Key provisions:** Transfers were limited to non-combat areas including “rescue, transportation, vigilance, surveillance, and naval minesweeping”, while jointly developed equipment could be exported only to partner countries. Proposed revisions would “allow export of defence equipment and technologies to other countries”.

8. **REG8 — US semiconductor export controls and CHIPS Act industrial policy**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** US Department of Commerce, CHIPS Program Office and other federal agencies.  
   **Stated purpose:** De-risk Chinese technological dependence, protect national security and rebuild domestic semiconductor capacity.  
   **Scope:** Chinese semiconductor firms, legacy chips, US semiconductor manufacturers and defence-linked production.  
   **Key provisions:** October 2022 controls targeted Chinese firms and “older technologies, including legacy chips”; CHIPS funding included USD 1.5 billion for GlobalFoundries and support for defence-related manufacturing. The policy joins national-security controls with subsidies intended to “strengthen domestic capacity”.

9. **REG9 — UK National Security Strategic Investment Fund regime**  
   **Regulatory bodies:** UK Government, British Technology Investments and British Business Bank.  
   **Stated purpose:** Invest in dual-use advanced technologies serving security and industrial objectives.  
   **Scope:** Technology firms, venture-capital funds and defence-related start-ups.  
   **Key provisions:** NSSIF operates as the UK Government’s “‘corporate venturing arm for dual-use advanced technologies’” and had committed GBP 220 million across 217 companies by July 2023.

### Regulatory evolution

1. **EV1 / REG1 — Creation, 2021.** AUKUS was announced on “15 September 2021” as a trilateral defence and security partnership. Its creation was driven by China-related security concerns, technological competition and bipartisan politics; Australian Labor backed it “within 24 hours”. The article argues that bipartisanship reduced scrutiny and provided an institutional base for industrial and regulatory change.

2. **EV2 / REG1 — Expansion from military pact to economic-security regime.** Pillar II broadened AUKUS from submarines into critical minerals, AI, quantum, cyber, hypersonics and supply-chain policy. The driving force was an order marked by “persistent disruption in global supply chains” and the “securitisation of technologies and ideas”. Defence, industry, export finance and research institutions consequently became more tightly integrated.

3. **EV3 / REG2–REG3 — Contraction of licensing barriers, 2023–24.** US exceptions and Australia’s Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act reduced export-licensing requirements within the AUKUS bloc. Governments presented this as “defence trade cooperation”, while firms and investors benefited from faster access to sensitive markets.

4. **EV4 / REG4–REG5 — Expansion of critical-minerals support, 2023–24.** Australia’s US domestic-source status and expanded public financing tied climate, industrial and defence goals together. The bilateral agenda prioritised “streamlining defence trade cooperation” and a “responsible and stable supply of critical mineral”.

5. **EV5 / REG6 — Expansion of South Korean export-finance capacity, 2023.** Industry concerns that major defence exports could fail for lack of financing prompted the statutory-capital proposal. Both governing and opposition lawmakers supported the amendment, demonstrating bipartisan accommodation of defence-export interests.

6. **EV6 / REG7 — Proposed relaxation of Japanese arms-export constraints, 2023.** The LDP–Komeito coalition sought revised guidelines after restrictive rules contributed to a defence sector of “small scale”, with “more than 100 companies having left the industry”. Defence-industrial scaling and participation in allied supply chains drove the change.

7. **EV7 / REG8 — Expansion of US techno-security controls and subsidies, 2022–24.** Export controls widened to include legacy chips, while CHIPS subsidies strengthened domestic firms. The shift reflects “the simultaneous securitisation of economic policy and economisation of strategic policy”.

8. **EV8 / REG9 — Expansion of government-backed defence venture capital, 2023–24.** UK and US security institutions increasingly incorporated venture capital and start-ups. The AUKUS Defense Investor Network brought together private VCs with “400 USD billion in funds”, reflecting a structural move from conventional procurement toward state-supported dual-use investment.

### Regulatory effects

1. **EF1 / REG1 — Intended, large:** AUKUS deepens military interoperability, defence collaboration and advanced-technology development. Affected parties are AUKUS governments, armed forces, defence firms and research institutions. Principal winners are trusted defence suppliers and allied security agencies; excluded jurisdictions and non-approved suppliers lose market access. The framework explicitly promotes “efficient defence and industrial collaboration”.

2. **EF2 / REG1 — Unintended, large:** Bipartisanship can suppress scrutiny and socialise long-term strategic and fiscal risks. Australia entered AUKUS with “little debate or scrutiny”, while the “higher cost and risks of nuclear-powered submarines were never previously justified”. Taxpayers and displaced spending priorities bear potential costs.

3. **EF3 / REG1 — Unintended, moderate:** Pillar I delays could leave Australia without an effective sovereign submarine capability and marginalise domestic shipbuilding firms. The text warns that delays could leave Australia with “no sovereign submarine capability”, particularly harming South Australia’s naval shipbuilding sector and ASC.

4. **EF4 / REG2–REG3 — Intended, moderate:** Licensing exemptions accelerate controlled technology transfers and defence delivery. Winners include approved AUKUS contractors and start-ups; regulators and firms still outside the trusted bloc lose relative access. The reform operates “without export licences”.

5. **EF5 / REG2–REG3 — Unintended, moderate:** Reduced controls can weaken oversight and privilege firms developing sensitive technology. The article says the reforms “put companies who develop advanced military technology at an advantage”. Public-security and proliferation risks are identified implicitly but not quantified.

6. **EF6 / REG4–REG5 — Intended, large:** Public financing expands allied critical-mineral extraction and processing. Winners include Lynas, Fortescue, Arafura, Meteoric and ASM; governments seek diversified supply chains. Examples include USD 258 million for a Lynas refinery and AUD 840 million for Arafura.

7. **EF7 / REG4–REG5 — Unintended, large:** Industrial and defence risks are shifted toward public balance sheets while benefits accrue selectively to firms. The article describes “huge capital injection from the state” and state agencies acting as “financier of last resort”. Taxpayers bear downside exposure, although the text does not measure defaults or returns.

8. **EF8 / REG6 — Intended, moderate:** Raising South Korean export-finance capacity supports large arms deals and reduces the possibility that exports will be annulled for lack of financing. Winners are Korean defence firms and heavy-industry conglomerates; public financial institutions assume greater concentration risk.

9. **EF9 / REG7 — Intended, moderate:** Relaxing Japanese export restrictions can expand production, exports and participation in AUKUS-linked projects. Winners include Mitsubishi and Kawasaki Heavy Industries; the reform may reverse contraction caused by over 100 firms leaving the industry.

10. **EF10 / REG8 — Intended, large:** US controls and subsidies seek to rebuild semiconductor manufacturing and protect defence supply. The US share of global chip production had fallen to “only 12%” from 37% in 1990, creating the political rationale for subsidies.

11. **EF11 / REG8 — Unintended, moderate:** Controls fragment supply chains and channel public funding toward selected incumbents. The wider order is described as “geoeconomic fragmentation”, and subsidies favour firms such as GlobalFoundries, BAE Systems and Microchip Technology.

12. **EF12 / REG9 — Unintended, large:** Security regulation and public venture funding enhance the structural power of venture capital. VC gains “power to define the structure, to choose the game, as well as set the rules”. Winners include defence-tech investors and scalable start-ups; losers may include non-defence innovators and public priorities crowded out by military spending.

### Compliance analysis

- **COMP1 / REG2–REG3:** Direct costs include classification, security, recordkeeping and determining whether goods qualify for the AUKUS exemption; indirect costs include approval delays where exemptions do not apply. Compliance rates and enforcement intensity are not reported. The principal gaming risk is reclassifying dual-use products or structuring transfers through approved AUKUS entities, inferred from reforms that “remove barriers to investment in sensitive technology”.  
  **Compliance rate:** Unknown. **Enforcement:** Unknown for exempt transfers; otherwise presumed active but not documented.

- **COMP2 / REG4–REG5:** Firms bear application, eligibility and project-reporting costs, while governments bear financing and credit risks. Firms may frame commercially useful projects as simultaneously serving clean energy and national security because funding covers technologies “critical for EVs, wind turbines, and defence capabilities”.  
  **Compliance rate:** Unknown. **Enforcement:** Moderate/unknown.

- **COMP3 / REG6:** Eximbank and exporters must comply with country-exposure and statutory-capital rules. The prior 40% cap constrained financing, motivating legal amendment rather than evasion.  
  **Compliance rate:** Apparently high, because the cap “hampered” support. **Enforcement:** Moderate to strict.

- **COMP4 / REG7:** Japanese firms faced high indirect costs from limited production scale and restricted export markets; more than 100 companies exited. Firms could use joint development with partner countries because the rules allowed export of “jointly developed defence equipment”.  
  **Compliance rate:** Apparently high. **Enforcement:** Strict historically.

- **COMP5 / REG8:** Semiconductor firms bear technology-classification, customer-screening and supply-chain restructuring costs. Potential gaming strategies include shifting production, ownership or product definitions outside controlled categories, but the article provides no direct evidence of their use.  
  **Compliance rate:** Unknown. **Enforcement:** Strict in stated intent.

- **COMP6 / REG9:** Start-ups bear investment-screening and security-eligibility requirements but gain state-backed capital and procurement access. Investors reduce market risk by aligning products with military concepts; VC firms “advocated to the Pentagon different warfare concepts that suit their products”.  
  **Compliance rate:** Unknown. **Enforcement:** Moderate/unknown.

### Regulatory arbitrage

1. **ARB1 — Jurisdictional arbitrage involving REG2 and REG3.** Firms can route qualifying controlled technology through the licence-free AUKUS zone. Users include AUKUS contractors and defence-tech start-ups. The gap is preferential treatment based on allied jurisdiction rather than the intrinsic sensitivity of technology. **Scale:** Likely common within eligible AUKUS trade, but the source does not quantify it.

2. **ARB2 — Definitional arbitrage involving REG1, REG4, REG5 and REG9.** Dual-use technologies can be characterised simultaneously as climate, civilian-industrial and defence assets to access multiple financing streams. The regulatory opening is visible where minerals are “critical for EVs, wind turbines, and defence capabilities”. Users include critical-mineral firms, start-ups and civilian conglomerates. **Scale:** Common.

3. **ARB3 — Structural arbitrage involving REG1 and REG9.** Venture capital uses state procurement, subsidies and security institutions to reduce technological and market risks while retaining private upside. Start-ups receive a larger share of a defence budget that reached “USD 886 billion for 2024”. **Scale:** Widespread in the emerging defence-tech ecosystem.

4. **ARB4 — Structural arbitrage involving REG6.** Increasing Eximbank’s statutory capital enables financing beyond the practical constraint imposed by the country-exposure cap without abolishing that cap directly. Users are major Korean defence exporters and foreign buyers. **Scale:** Common for large export packages.

5. **ARB5 — Definitional/jurisdictional arbitrage involving REG7.** Joint development with approved partners provides an export route under otherwise restrictive Japanese rules. The gap is the distinction between domestically developed and “jointly developed defence equipment”. **Scale:** Historically limited but strategically important.

6. **ARB6 — Timing arbitrage involving REG1.** Firms and governments commit capital and restructure industries ahead of uncertain submarine delivery. This can lock in beneficiaries before strategic performance is demonstrated; the article notes that there may be “few concrete results to show for years to come”. **Scale:** Common across long-horizon defence programs.

### Regulatory interactions

1. **INT1 — REG1, REG2 and REG3: Complementary.** AUKUS supplies the security framework, while US and Australian licensing reforms operationalise faster technology transfer. Together they expedite “delivery of critical defence capabilities”.

2. **INT2 — REG1, REG4 and REG5: Complementary and overlapping.** Defence, clean-energy and critical-minerals policies share funding agencies and beneficiaries. This expands supply-chain resilience but blurs whether subsidies principally serve climate, industrial or military objectives.

3. **INT3 — REG1, REG6 and REG7: Complementary.** Korean export-finance reform and prospective Japanese export liberalisation allow non-member partners to engage more deeply with Pillar II. Japan and Korea are described as “key defence partners under Pillar II”.

4. **INT4 — REG2/REG3 and conventional technology controls: Conflicting.** AUKUS exemptions seek speed and integration, whereas traditional export controls seek scrutiny and containment. The reforms reduce barriers precisely for “sensitive technology”, creating an oversight trade-off.

5. **INT5 — REG8 and REG1: Complementary.** US semiconductor restrictions and subsidies reinforce AUKUS technological sovereignty and secure defence supply chains. Both combine “high-end computing capabilities, clean tech, quantum and AI” as security imperatives.

6. **INT6 — REG9 and conventional procurement rules: Gap-creating.** Venture funds and start-up accelerators allow security agencies to support technologies before conventional procurement maturity. This encourages innovation but also permits investors to shape military demand around products that “suit their products”.

### Reform dynamics

**Reform proposals**

- **RP1 / REG7:** Relax Japan’s arms-export guidelines to permit wider transfers. **Proponents:** LDP, Komeito and defence manufacturers. **Opponents:** Not identified in the source. **Likelihood:** Possible; the coalition “pledged that the government would push through revised guidelines”.

- **RP2 / REG6:** Increase South Korea Eximbank’s statutory capital to support defence exports. **Proponents:** Government, ruling and opposition lawmakers, and defence industry. **Opponents:** Not identified. **Likelihood:** Likely/implemented in substantial part; the revised decree came into effect in December 2023.

- **RP3 / REG2–REG3:** Continue streamlining AUKUS defence-trade controls. **Proponents:** AUKUS governments, contractors and defence-tech investors. **Opponents:** Potential oversight and arms-control constituencies, although these are not named. **Likelihood:** Likely because exemptions have already been legislated.

- **RP4 / REG1:** Require clearer plans, funding and safeguards for submarine transfers. **Proponents:** US Senator Roger Wicker and other congressional sceptics. **Opponents:** Actors prioritising rapid implementation. **Likelihood:** Possible; Republican members demanded a “‘concrete plan’ for the sale of submarines”.

**Reform obstacles**

- Bipartisan convergence can weaken review because AUKUS initially received “little debate or scrutiny”.
- US submarine-production constraints and congressional resistance may delay transfers; lawmakers argued the US must have “enough submarines for our own security”.
- UK shipbuilding weakness creates implementation risk, described as the “devastating state of the UK submarine-building industry”.
- Established beneficiaries have incentives to preserve subsidies and exemptions because Pillar II “has become a business in its own right”.
- Security classification and the merging of economic and military objectives make accountability difficult; the system layers “security imperatives on economic institutions and vice versa”.
- Japanese historical restrictions and domestic coalition bargaining may slow export liberalisation.
- Public fiscal exposure may eventually provoke resistance, but the article does not document a developed taxpayer-led reform coalition.

### Meta

- **Regulations analyzed:** 9  
- **Unintended effects found:** 6 major categories  
- **Arbitrage opportunities identified:** 6  
- **Overall regulatory quality:** **Medium-low**. The arrangements appear effective at mobilising capital, accelerating allied trade and building selected industries, but transparency, distributional neutrality and risk allocation are weak. This assessment is anchored in the coexistence of transfers “without export licences”, substantial public capital injections, and the original absence of “debate or scrutiny”.

### Counter-evidence

- AUKUS’s official rationale is collective security and capability development, not private enrichment; Pillar II explicitly promotes “efficient defence and industrial collaboration”.
- Public funding may generate employment and domestic capability rather than merely rents; Albanese argued that resilience can “maximise Australian jobs”.
- Export-control exemptions are restricted to trusted AUKUS partners rather than amounting to wholesale deregulation.
- Japanese arms-export restrictions historically maintained strong limits, permitting transfers chiefly in “non-combat areas”.
- South Korean compliance with its financing cap appears to have driven formal legislative reform rather than evasion.
- The article acknowledges that AUKUS is not “completely geopolitically fetishist”; genuine security considerations remain relevant.
- Some US congressional resistance indicates that bipartisanship is not absolute, particularly where legislators seek “enough submarines for our own security”.
