# Reading

## Public resources underwrite firms and strategic capabilities

The most consequential documented distinction is between **public suppliers of finance and regulatory access** and **private or institutional recipients positioned to build mines, manufacturing, weapons, semiconductors, and dual-use technology**. The article reports grants, loans, contracts, investments, export-control exemptions, and research arrangements. These are not interchangeable: a programme total is not a recipient allocation, a pledge is not expenditure, and eligibility for funds is not receipt.

The inventory shows that governments do more than purchase finished defence equipment. They finance extraction and processing, subsidise manufacturing and research, award development contracts, and provide regulatory access. Private firms, start-ups, research institutions, and defence suppliers receive these resources at different stages of the value chain. The text does not provide a consolidated accounting of taxes paid, public equity acquired, repayment terms, procurement prices, or downstream public returns.

### Counter-evidence

- The article does not portray every private actor as advantaged. It reports that uncertainty marginalized South Australia’s naval shipbuilding sector and ASC, while describing their possible windfall profits as unrealized and conditional [F19].
- Eligibility and regulatory access do not demonstrate receipt: Australian suppliers became eligible for US funds, but the designation alone does not establish an award to each supplier [F15].
- Share-price appreciation is evidence of a market response, not proof of Hanwha’s realized revenue or profit from the prospective contract [F16].
- The submarine burden evidence remains contingent: the text reports unjustified costs and risks and the possibility of a capability gap, not a quantified or observed loss [F17] [F18].
