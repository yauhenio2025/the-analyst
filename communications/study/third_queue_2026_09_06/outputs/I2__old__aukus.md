## Resource-flow analysis

### Executive assessment

The article depicts AUKUS—especially Pillar II—as a publicly financed market-making system, not merely a military alliance. Governments and security agencies supply grants, loans, procurement contracts, export-control exemptions, R&D infrastructure and political legitimacy. Defence primes, critical-minerals firms, technology start-ups and venture-capital funds capture much of the resulting commercial value. The public sector and Australian taxpayers bear financing, delivery and strategic risks, while start-ups and investors benefit from state-backed demand and reduced regulatory barriers.

The central asymmetry is the conversion of public security expenditure and public risk into private accumulation. The authors describe security agencies as “capital providers” and venture capital as an “intermediar[y] between capital providers (the state) and start-ups.” This transfer is obscured by narratives of “technological sovereignty,” “supply-chain resilience,” jobs and national security.

All article references below refer to Wijaya and Hayes, **“AUKUS ‘behind the scenes’: through the lens of militarised neoliberalism” (2025)**, with the underlying source named where useful.

### Principal asymmetries

1. **Public capital, private capture — severe; obscured.**  
   Beneficiaries: A7–A18, A23. Cost bearers: A4–A6. Governments provide subsidies, procurement and risk reduction while firms retain contracts, intellectual property, equity upside and share-price gains. Mechanism: state grants, concessional loans, procurement and co-investment. Anchor: firms benefit from “huge state subsidies,” while VCs mediate between “capital providers (the state) and start-ups.” The offered justification is national security, jobs, innovation and technological sovereignty.

2. **State absorbs uncertainty while venture capital gains scalable opportunities — severe; partly hidden.**  
   Beneficiaries: A15–A17, A23. Cost bearers: A2–A6. The state supplies guaranteed demand and relaxes controls because “start-ups require access to large markets to be easily scalable and minimise technological risks.” The article says the state “guarantees minimum conditions for capital accumulation.”

3. **Security framing converts sectoral interests into national interests — severe; obscured.**  
   Beneficiaries: defence, minerals, semiconductor and technology firms. Cost bearers: taxpayers and excluded sectors. The authors argue that regulatory controls “rearticulate the interests of particular business groups…as being of general and national interest.” Justifications include “de-risking China,” “supply-chain resilience” and “sovereign industrial capacity.”

4. **Benefits are immediate; strategic capability is delayed and uncertain — severe; overt but politically managed.**  
   Beneficiaries: current contractors and subsidy recipients. Cost bearers: A21 and potentially A22. Commercial flows begin now, but submarine delays could leave Australia with “no sovereign submarine capability.”

5. **Prime and financial actors have stronger capture capacity than local firms — moderate; obscured.**  
   Beneficiaries: A11–A17. Cost bearers: smaller Australian firms that may not win scalable projects. The article warns AUKUS “could become a gold mine for US venture capitalists,” while asking whether Australian firms receive comparable value.

6. **Universities contribute publicly supported knowledge but commercial/security actors direct its use — moderate; obscured.**  
   Beneficiaries: governments and defence contractors. Cost bearers: A20 and the broader public research mission. Mechanism: the “close triangular relationship between the academia/R&D landscape, defence, and the state.”

7. **Regulatory liberalisation is selective — moderate; overt rules, obscured distribution.**  
   Beneficiaries: firms inside the AUKUS trusted network. Cost bearers: firms and states outside it. Export controls are relaxed within AUKUS while being tightened against China; licence exemptions “put companies who develop advanced military technology at an advantage.”

8. **Potential regional employment is traded against concentration of fiscal and strategic risk — moderate; overt.**  
   Beneficiaries include local employees and SMEs, with Lockheed’s centre “expected to generate over 1400 local jobs.” Cost bearers remain taxpayers if jobs, spillovers or capabilities fail to materialise.

### Value chains

#### VC-backed defence-technology chain

1. **Public appropriations — A2/A3:** adds patient, large-scale capital and guaranteed demand; captures strategic capability, but bears downside risk.  
2. **Public investment vehicles — Pentagon units/NSSIF:** screen and channel funds; capture institutional control and policy influence.  
3. **Venture capital — A15:** selects firms and structures ownership; captures equity appreciation and fees.  
4. **Start-ups — A16/A17/A23:** produce prototypes, software and weapons; capture contracts, valuations and intellectual property.  
5. **Military customers — A1–A3:** deploy systems; capture operational capability.  

**Extraction points:** VC ownership and fees; private retention of publicly de-risked IP; procurement margins; regulatory advantages. Anchor: VC operates as an intermediary between “capital providers (the state) and start-ups.”

#### Critical-minerals chain

1. **Public financing and regulation — A1/A2:** grants loans and “domestic source” eligibility.  
2. **Mining firms — A7/A8:** extract rare earths; capture project revenues and asset appreciation.  
3. **Processors — A9 and related firms:** refine minerals; capture processing margins.  
4. **Defence/clean-tech manufacturers — A11/A12 and others:** incorporate inputs into weapons, EVs and energy technology.  
5. **Governments/militaries:** procure final capabilities.

**Extraction points:** subsidised project finance, publicly supported processing facilities and defence procurement. The same minerals are justified as critical to “EVs, wind turbines, and defence capabilities,” broadening the political coalition supporting subsidies.

#### Guided-weapons chain

1. **Australian taxpayers — A4:** provide fiscal resources.  
2. **Australian Government/GWEO — A1:** allocates AUD 4.1 billion plus AUD 2.5 billion.  
3. **Defence primes — A11/A12:** supply technology and production systems.  
4. **Australian SMEs/consultants — A14:** contribute engineering, components and labour.  
5. **Australian and allied militaries — A21:** consume weapons and capability.

**Extraction points:** prime-contractor margins, technology dependence and public underwriting of facilities. Potential public value consists of jobs, interoperability and “self-reliance,” but the article does not quantify the public return.

### Meta

- **Total identified flows:** 32.
- **Total value tracked:** Not reliably aggregable because the text mixes AUD, USD and GBP, includes overlapping programme envelopes, and cites the entire USD 886 billion US defence budget rather than an AUKUS-only allocation.
- **Visible quantified envelopes:** at least **AUD 10.216 billion**, **USD 4.846 billion** excluding the overall US defence budget and the USD 400 billion of VC assets, and **GBP 938 million** in UK public/private commitments. These are directional lower-bound sums, not net transfers or strictly AUKUS-only totals.
- **Most significant asymmetry:** Public institutions finance and de-risk industrial and technological development while private defence firms, mineral companies, start-ups and venture funds retain much of the commercial upside.
- **Primary beneficiaries:** A7–A18 and A23, particularly defence primes, critical-minerals companies, semiconductor producers, VC funds and defence-tech start-ups.
- **Primary cost bearers:** A4–A6 and A21; additionally A20 and A22 bear institutional or sector-specific risks.
- **Core extraction mechanism:** Security narratives legitimate subsidies, procurement, public venture investment and selective deregulation, converting national-security budgets into protected private markets.
- **Visibility:** Individual grants and contracts are generally overt; aggregate incidence, opportunity costs, risk transfer and ownership of resulting value are obscured.

### Counter-evidence

- AUKUS-related spending may create public and regional benefits rather than solely private extraction. Lockheed’s Australian centre is “expected to generate over 1400 local jobs,” and the government frames AUKUS as an opportunity to “maximise Australian jobs.”
- Public investment may address genuine supply-chain and capability failures. The stated goals include “secure diversified supply chains” and improving “self-reliance.”
- Defence spending can generate shared R&D, interoperability and manufacturing capacity. The Japan–Australia project aims at “enhancing strategic capabilities in robotic and autonomous systems.”
- Private capital also bears some investment risk: UK programmes reportedly attracted “GBP 718 million of investments from private investors,” and Sequoia supplied private seed capital to Mach Industries.
- Not every potential industry beneficiary wins. Delays have “further marginalised” South Australia’s shipbuilding sector and ASC, showing that benefits are uneven even within the defence coalition.
- Political support is not unanimous. “Fifty of 800 grassroots Labor branches and left-aligned unions passed motions opposing AUKUS.”
- US congressional resistance creates constraints on private and allied expectations: Republican senators moved to “block legislation enabling the sale of Virginia-class submarines.”
- The article provides a critical political-economy interpretation, but does not calculate net welfare, firm profits, subsidy repayment rates or the counterfactual cost of inaction.

### Open questions

1. What proportion of each announced envelope has actually been disbursed rather than merely pledged?
2. How much of the USD 886 billion US defence budget is attributable to AUKUS or Pillar II?
3. Which grants are loans, equity investments, guarantees, tax concessions or non-repayable subsidies?
4. What repayment terms, interest rates and security attach to the AUD 840 million Arafura package and US rare-earth loans?
5. Who owns intellectual property created through Defence Innovation Hub, NSSIF and university-funded projects?
6. What profits, equity gains and management fees accrue to VC funds from publicly de-risked defence start-ups?
7. What share of procurement value remains in Australia versus flowing to US, UK, Japanese or South Korean firms?
8. Are promised employment figures net job additions, and what is the public subsidy per sustained job?
9. What are the opportunity costs for health, education, civilian R&D, climate adaptation or non-military industry?
10. Who bears cost overruns or cancellation losses if submarine and Pillar II projects fail?
11. Do export-control exemptions weaken accountability, safety review or parliamentary oversight?
12. How are environmental and Indigenous-land risks from expanded critical-minerals extraction allocated?
13. Will Australian SMEs gain durable production capacity or remain subordinate suppliers to foreign primes?
14. What mechanisms ensure that publicly financed technology produces public returns rather than exclusively private intellectual property?
15. How much strategic risk does Australia assume through deeper integration into possible US–China conflict?
