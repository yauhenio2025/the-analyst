# Reading

The text’s governing mapping is **CAPITALISM AS RELIGION**, but its thesis depends on a more specific chain: **money embodies a this-worldly promise of absolute wealth; capital seeks to redeem that promise through growth; entrepreneurs and experts interpret it through economic myths**. The religious frame therefore does more than decorate capitalism with cultic vocabulary. It connects money’s paradoxical claim to unlimited possibility with capital’s growth imperative and with the institutional cycles through which innovations gain and lose credibility [F1] [F2] [F3] [F4].

A second organizing mapping presents **money as generalized power or a key**. Money opens access not merely to commodities but to material, social, temporal, and spatial possibilities [F5] [F6]. This mapping supplies the premise for the religious one: because money promises “the option to have options,” it can occupy the functional place assigned to a transcendent absolute [F7] [F8]. By contrast, the text’s images of money flowing back and capitalist machinery remaining in motion explain capital’s processual dynamics; they are important elaborations, but they do not independently carry the thesis [F9] [F10].

### Counter-evidence

The text repeatedly marks limits to a literal or classificatory identification of capitalism with religion. It denies that the comparison can begin from a general scientific definition of religion [F11], distinguishes the historical cycles of capitalist myths from claims to timeless religious validity [F12], and presents the entrepreneur as “comparable with” prophets and priests rather than as literally holding a religious office [F16]. It also qualifies the emancipatory imagery of money: the same medium functions as freedom for the rich and discipline for the poor [F13], while higher-order optionality presupposes wealth beyond subsistence [F14].

### Open questions

- The article supplies conceptual parallels but no audience evidence showing that readers actually experience capitalism as religion.
- Claims of inheritance from Christianity, Protestantism, or nineteenth-century criticism would require the cited historical sources and contextual comparison; this article alone establishes only its own account of those relationships.
- The text proposes, rather than empirically demonstrates here, that money can fill the place of the “holy cosmos” [F8].
- Further evidence would be needed to test whether innovation cycles consistently operate through the stated myth sequence across industries and periods.
- The closing addiction/disillusionment frame is conditional and prospective, not evidence that capitalism has already undergone such a withdrawal [F19].
