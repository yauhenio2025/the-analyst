# Reading

The article’s most consequential use of examples is to distinguish **solving problems within a shared framework** from **changing the framework that determines what counts as a problem**. Scientific paradigm change provides the controlling comparison. Scientists divided by paradigms may nevertheless share a general commitment to solving scientific puzzles, whereas rival social groups may disagree about whether the social order itself is the problem [F6] [F7]. Chen uses slavery, marriage, childhood, Nazism, and right-wing populism primarily as conceptual tests of Jaeggi’s cumulative, problem-solving account—not as a representative history or empirical survey of progress.

## What the examples establish

Taken together, the cases challenge the idea that a formal vocabulary of crisis, problem solving, and learning supplies a universally cumulative or neutral criterion of progress. Abolition may negate inherited know-how [F3] [F4]; marriage and childhood combine acknowledged progress with new problems or diminished capacities [F9] [F12] [F13]; and Nazism and right-wing populism show that exclusionary movements can also represent themselves as solving crises [F10] [F11]. The scientific-social comparison explains why this difficulty is sharper in social life: scientists may retain a common puzzle-solving goal, while rival social actors can disagree about the identity of the problem itself [F7].

These examples establish conceptual possibilities and expose insufficiencies in the theories under discussion. They do not determine how frequently progressive transitions produce losses, calculate the net gains and losses in any particular history, or provide a comparative empirical study of scientific and social change. Additional historical and population-level evidence would be needed for those conclusions. The article’s psychologist alternative also leaves open whether collective recognition can justify a judgment of progress rather than merely help constitute it.

For **examples**, the relevant sections were assessed as completely covered under the criterion of separately inventorying historical cases, counterexamples, and illustrative models while preserving ownership and argumentative context. The outcome is findings present and supported within the stated scope. Scientific paradigm shifts, childhood, and civil society are clearly used as examples [F6] [F12] [F14]. The inventory prioritizes examples doing substantial argumentative work; it does not convert every passing political reference or citation into a separate case.

For **attributes**, the assessment likewise found complete, supported coverage in the sections on moralism, historicism, and incommensurability. The claim-relevant attributes include abolition’s transformation of the conception of humanity [F1] and the methodological, evidential, and world-view differences between paradigms [F5]. A limitation is that some empirical attributes—especially the demographic consequences attributed to modern marriage—are asserted without accompanying population evidence.

For **inferential functions**, the assessment found supported examples in the criticism and alternative sections. Abolition, marriage, and Nazism/right-wing populism operate as counterexamples to cumulative or merely formal problem-solving accounts [F3] [F8] [F10]. Their function is to test conceptual sufficiency, not to estimate prevalence or recurrence.

For **comparisons**, coverage was assessed as complete and supported. The article preserves both sides of the contrast between slaveholders and abolitionists [F2] and between scientific and social revolution [F7]. The scientific-social comparison remains an analogy rather than a comparative empirical study.

For **limits and qualifications**, coverage was also assessed as complete and supported. The abolition conclusion remains modal [F4]; marriage remains progress despite its asserted new problems [F9]; the Nazi and populist examples pose an interpretive question rather than establish a comprehensive classification [F11]; childhood retains both gains and losses [F13]; and interacting practices do not entail a societal purpose [F15]. The article provides no population-level basis for treating these examples as representative or recurrent.

### Counter-evidence

- None of the retained examples is directly contradicted by another passage in the article.
- The marriage and childhood examples supply internal qualifications: new problems or diminished experiences do not lead Chen to deny that the transitions count as progress [F9] [F12].

### Open questions

- What historical or population-level evidence supports the demographic claims made in the marriage example?
- How should gains and losses of practical know-how be compared across incommensurable forms of life?
- How common are transitions in which acknowledged progress diminishes some capacities or experiences?
- Can collective recognition justify progress rather than merely constitute a social judgment that progress has occurred?
- How does the psychologist alternative answer objections from relativism, quietism, or the possibility of collective endorsement of fascist movements?
