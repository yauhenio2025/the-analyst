# Incentive-structure analysis

## Executive diagnosis

The subsea-cable system is a **hybrid market-security regime**. Private firms generally decide what gets built according to expected demand and return, while states increasingly try to redirect those decisions toward security, sovereignty, and industrial-policy objectives. The central misalignment is explicit: infrastructure expansion remains “**largely driven by the commercial interests of tech and telecommunication firms**,” while governments want networks aligned with national-security priorities.

The system therefore rewards four behaviors:

1. **Corporations build commercially valuable capacity**, especially where hyperscalers provide demand and financing—“**These firms need vast bandwidth and reliable interconnections, which they pursue by financing their own cables**.”
2. **The United States exploits control over market access, finance, and sanctions**, making firms avoid Chinese suppliers even where those suppliers bid less—“**factoring in potential sanctions, HMN Tech became an increasingly costly option**.”
3. **China subsidizes national champions and duplicates routes after exclusion**, rather than abandoning network expansion—“**China is intensifying its efforts to build infrastructure in regions where it enjoys more influence**.”
4. **Connected middle powers protect domestic industrial capacity and hedge between blocs**, exemplified by France’s selective cooperation and nationalization of ASN—“**France is not strictly aligned… but selectively engages with both United States and Chinese projects**.”

The emergent equilibrium is not clean decoupling. It is **partial exclusion plus parallel, overlapping expansion**: “**we observe network duplication, with both the United States and China building parallel systems with overlapping geographies**.”

## 1. Actors

### ACT1 — United States government
- **Interests:** Exclude Chinese suppliers from critical infrastructure, protect surveillance/security advantages, support SubCom, and preserve US firms’ centrality. Anchor: “**block Chinese firms from entering critical infrastructure markets and prevent cable landings in Chinese territory**.”
- **Resources:** Sanctions, Entity List restrictions, diplomatic pressure, landing reviews, access to US hyperscalers, export finance, and grants. Anchor: “**Employing incentives and, to a larger degree, threats, the United States aimed to tilt the decision in favor of SubCom**.”
- **Constraints:** Existing infrastructure inertia, private ownership, foreign landing jurisdictions, and alternative Chinese-backed routes. Anchor: “**their influence remains spotty and uneven**.”
- **Information:** Strong regulatory and intelligence access, but incomplete visibility into private consortium bargaining and global cable operation.
- **Horizon/risk:** Long-term strategic horizon; comparatively willing to impose short-term disruption.

### ACT2 — Chinese government
- **Interests:** Build Chinese-controlled connectivity, grow HMN Tech and state telecom firms, and position China at the center of data flows. Anchor: “**China aims to capture 60% of the global market**.”
- **Resources:** Subsidies, state-owned firms, policy coordination, domestic demand, and financing. Anchor: “**the Chinese government provides extensive financial support**.”
- **Constraints:** US sanctions, reliance on foreign consortium partners, Western security concerns, and restricted access to US customers.
- **Information:** Close access to state-owned participants and HMN Tech; less control over multinational consortia.
- **Horizon/risk:** Long-term and strategically risk-tolerant.

### ACT3 — France/French state
- **Interests:** Strategic autonomy, resilience, domestic employment, access to supply capacity, and influence over network development. Anchor: France sought to “**maintain influence over network development and guard against foreign interference**.”
- **Resources:** Central landing geography, regulatory jurisdiction, ownership stakes in Orange, public investment institutions, and ASN ownership.
- **Constraints:** Fiscal pressure, limited tech-sector scale, reliance on US firms, and exposure to US sanctions. Anchor: “**fears of sanctions and a deep reliance on US-American tech companies limit France’s capacity for selective engagement**.”
- **Horizon/risk:** Long-term strategic horizon, tempered by budget constraints.

### ACT4 — Multinational cable consortia
- **Interests:** Complete projects, minimize cost, retain access to customers and finance, and avoid sanctions.
- **Resources:** Supplier selection, pooled capital, route and landing choices.
- **Constraints:** Coordination among heterogeneous members and exposure to multiple jurisdictions.
- **Information:** Better knowledge than governments about commercial costs and project feasibility, but uncertainty about future sanctions.
- **Horizon/risk:** Medium-to-long term and strongly risk-averse because projects are capital-intensive and difficult to reverse.

### ACT5 — HMN Tech
- **Interests:** Win globally significant contracts, increase market share, and overcome incumbent suppliers.
- **Resources:** Chinese subsidies, manufacturing and laying capacity, ties to Hengtong.
- **Constraints:** Entity List restrictions and sanctions fears among customers.
- **Strategic position:** Challenger. Anchor: China used support “**to help HMN Tech close the gap with industry leaders like SubCom and ASN**.”

### ACT6 — SubCom
- **Interests:** Win cable-supply contracts and preserve incumbent market position.
- **Resources:** US political support, Export–Import Bank financing, and established capabilities.
- **Constraint:** Its SeaMeWe-6 bid was initially more expensive than HMN Tech’s. Anchor: HMN bid was “**substantially lower than the asking price of competitor SubCom**.”

### ACT7 — Alcatel Submarine Networks (ASN)
- **Interests:** Commercial growth, stable ownership, and continued production.
- **Resources:** Calais plant, seven cable ships, intellectual property, and global leadership.
- **Strategic importance:** “**leads the global market with more than 800,000 km of optical fiber deployed**.”

### ACT8 — Orange
- **Interests:** Profitable connectivity, diversified partnerships, resilience, and compatibility with French policy.
- **Resources:** Consortium stakes, cable ownership, Orange Marine, and state-linked ownership.
- **Behavioral profile:** Hedging rather than bloc alignment. Anchor: Orange “**has not ruled out cooperation with Chinese firms or participation in projects landing in China**.”

### ACT9 — US hyperscalers and global technology firms
- **Interests:** Cheap, reliable, high-capacity connectivity and low regulatory risk.
- **Resources:** Large demand, direct cable finance, and decisive purchasing power.
- **Systemic leverage:** Loss of access to these customers can change supplier economics. Anchor: US tech firms “**were expected to be among the most important customers**.”

### ACT10 — Smaller and third-party landing states
- **Interests:** Connectivity, investment, resilience, and autonomy from either great power.
- **Resources:** Geographic landing rights and, in hub states, control over indispensable routes.
- **Constraints:** Security vulnerability and limited coercive capacity. Anchor: interventions create “**new risks and uncertainties for third-party states**.”

## 2. Incentive structures

### INC1 — Commercial return on capacity
- **Actor:** ACT4, ACT8, ACT9 and private investors.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Building routes with expected bandwidth demand and reliable customers.
- **Mechanism:** Capacity sales, service revenues, and control over connectivity.
- **Measurement:** Forecast demand and project return—a proxy vulnerable to speculative overbuilding.
- **Gaming vulnerability:** 0.25.
- **Alignment:** Strong for capacity growth; weak for redundancy and security.
- **Anchor:** Infrastructure historically developed through “**private investors backing projects based on the promise of future returns**.”

### INC2 — US market-access conditionality
- **Actor:** ACT4 and suppliers.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Choosing suppliers acceptable to US regulators and customers.
- **Punished behavior:** Working with sanctioned Chinese firms.
- **Mechanism:** Entity List restrictions and threatened loss of US hyperscaler business.
- **Timing:** Contingent but credible.
- **Gaming vulnerability:** 0.30; ownership restructuring can attempt to obscure affiliation.
- **Anchor:** Stakeholders were warned they “**would be unable to sell capacity to US tech firms**.”

### INC3 — Chinese subsidized bidding
- **Actor:** ACT5 and Chinese telecom firms.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Aggressive bids that increase global market share.
- **Mechanism:** State subsidies reduce the private cost of bidding below commercial rivals.
- **Measurement:** Contracts and market share, both imperfect proxies for secure and economically sustainable connectivity.
- **Gaming vulnerability:** 0.65 because subsidies can be disguised and bids can understate full economic cost.
- **Anchor:** HMN Tech bid approximately $500 million “**backed by state subsidies**.”

### INC4 — US industrial support for SubCom
- **Actor:** ACT6.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Competing against subsidized Chinese suppliers in strategic projects.
- **Mechanism:** Export–Import Bank financing and “training grants.”
- **Anchor:** Financing allowed SubCom “**to reduce its price to $600 million**.”

### INC5 — Consortium risk minimization
- **Actor:** ACT4.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Avoiding suppliers likely to generate sanctions, financing loss, or customer exclusion.
- **Measurement:** Project bankability and regulatory acceptance rather than lowest engineering cost.
- **Gaming vulnerability:** 0.45 because actors can lobby to label rivals as security risks.
- **Anchor:** “**factoring in potential sanctions, HMN Tech became an increasingly costly option**.”

### INC6 — Strategic ownership and sovereignty
- **Actor:** ACT3.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Retaining domestic supply, maintenance, and repair capacity even at an immediate fiscal cost.
- **Mechanism:** State acquisition and public ownership.
- **Anchor:** France agreed to acquire “**80% of ASN for €350 million**.”

### INC7 — Hub-state leverage
- **Actor:** ACT3 and ACT10.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Using landing geography to secure ownership, domestic firms, and political influence.
- **Anchor:** Egypt “**successfully leveraged this position, ensuring that Telecom Egypt holds stakes in many of these projects**.”

### INC8 — Underprovision of resilience by private actors
- **Actor:** Private owners and investors.
- **Rewarded behavior:** Capacity that can be monetized; unrewarded behavior includes spare redundancy and security capabilities whose benefits diffuse across users.
- **Misalignment:** Severe public-good gap.
- **Anchor:** Public investment may be required “**where private investments are insufficient to address systemic risks**.”

## 3. Predicted behaviors

### BEH1 — Consortia avoid sanctioned or sanction-prone suppliers
This is rational because a nominally cheaper supplier can jeopardize the project’s largest customers. It differs from pure cost minimization by prioritizing geopolitical acceptability. **Likelihood: very likely.** Evidence: “**consortia have become reluctant to work with the Chinese firm, fearing sanctions and a loss of business with US hyperscalers**.”

### BEH2 — China duplicates routes after exclusion
Exclusion raises the value of Chinese-controlled alternatives and makes sunk industrial capacity politically important. **Likelihood: very likely.** Evidence: EMA “**was launched in direct reaction to US interference in SeaMeWe-6**.”

### BEH3 — The United States combines subsidies with coercion
Washington will use positive inducements where useful but rely more heavily on threats because control over US market access creates stronger leverage. **Likelihood: very likely.** Evidence: “**Employing incentives and, to a larger degree, threats**.”

### BEH4 — Private firms hedge across geopolitical blocs
Firms with global customers will avoid complete alignment unless sanctions make hedging too costly. **Likelihood: likely.** Evidence: Orange “**selectively engages with both United States and Chinese projects**.”

### BEH5 — Hub states pursue strategic stakes or nationalization
States with geographic or industrial centrality will convert that position into ownership and domestic capacity. **Likelihood: likely for capable hubs.** Evidence: France’s case shows room for “**autonomous realignment strategies**.”

### BEH6 — Security justifications also support domestic champions
States will bundle national-security claims with industrial policy because the same intervention can exclude a rival and support a home firm. **Likelihood: very likely.** Evidence: interventions “**often aim to support domestic champions and limit the opponent’s influence**.”

### BEH7 — Network capacity continues expanding despite decoupling rhetoric
Demand from AI, cloud services, and duplicated strategic routes keeps investment high. **Likelihood: very likely.** Evidence: the network sees “**continued expansion… as a consequence of increasing public and private investments in parallel and overlapping cable systems**.”

## 4. Principal–agent problems

### PA1 — States as principals, private infrastructure firms as agents
- **Principal:** ACT1, ACT2, or ACT3.
- **Agent:** ACT4, ACT8, ACT9.
- **Principal wants:** Security-compatible routes, suppliers, redundancy, and national control.
- **Agent wants:** Return, customer access, and flexibility.
- **Asymmetry:** Both hidden information and hidden action. Firms know project economics and negotiations better; states cannot fully observe commercial decision logic or future rerouting.
- **Moral hazard:** High where firms can quietly preserve ties that governments consider risky.
- **Alignment tools:** Sanctions, landing licenses, subsidies, ownership, and procurement restrictions.
- **Residual loss:** Firms retain circumvention incentives because “**private corporations that control these systems often have incentives to circumnavigate political interference**.”

### PA2 — Consortium members versus consortium management/collective decision
- **Principal:** Participating firms collectively.
- **Agent:** Consortium decision process and project managers.
- **Problem:** Individual members differ in exposure to US sanctions, Chinese finance, and route benefits.
- **Observable result:** Initial support for HMN Tech shifted when sanctions became concrete.
- **Residual loss:** Chinese withdrawals removed around 20% of financing. Anchor: they withdrew, “**taking with them approximately 20% of the total investment volume**.”

### PA3 — French public versus state owner of ASN
- **Principal:** French citizens/state.
- **Agent:** APE and ASN management.
- **Public objective:** Sovereignty, resilience, employment, and economic return.
- **Agent risks:** Overinvestment, politicized contracting, protected inefficiency, or optimistic revenue forecasts.
- **Adverse-selection risk:** Medium because the acquisition occurred after Nokia faced pressure and sought divestment.
- **Alignment:** Commercial reporting, staged acquisition, and profitability requirements.
- **Residual uncertainty:** The “**doubling or tripling of ASN’s revenues**” is a forecast, not an observed result.

### PA4 — Security regulators versus firms invoking security claims
- **Principal:** Governments and publics seeking genuine risk reduction.
- **Agents:** Domestic suppliers, telecom firms, and bureaucracies.
- **Risk:** Commercial interests may be presented as national-security imperatives.
- **Anchor:** Interventions cite security while they “**often aim to support domestic champions**.”
- **Moral-hazard level:** Medium-high because security evidence may be classified or difficult for outsiders to verify.

## 5. Collective-action problems

### CA1 — Global network security as a public good
- **Actors:** States, cable owners, users, and technology firms.
- **Individual incentive:** Let others pay for redundant routes, repair vessels, monitoring, and protection.
- **Collective outcome:** Insufficient resilience and maintenance capacity.
- **Free riding:** Yes; users benefiting from global connectivity need not directly fund protection.
- **Why suboptimal:** A disruption imposes system-wide costs not captured by a single investor.
- **Anchor:** “**private actors lack incentives to establish redundancies and ensure cable security**.”

### CA2 — Global regulatory vacuum
- **Type:** Coordination/public-goods problem.
- **Individual incentive:** Preserve national discretion over landings and security.
- **Collective outcome:** Fragmented safeguards for transnational infrastructure.
- **Coordination failure:** Bad equilibrium; cables cross jurisdictions while enforcement remains national.
- **Anchor:** Protection requires governance frameworks that “**fill the global regulatory vacuum and ensure the integrity of the network**.”

### CA3 — Consortium financing after geopolitical exit
- **Type:** Assurance and coordination problem.
- **Individual incentive:** Reduce exposure or withdraw if others will cover the financing gap.
- **Outcome:** Project delay or funding shortfall.
- **Observed solution:** New Malaysian and Indonesian firms entered and incumbents increased contributions.
- **Anchor:** “**To offset the financing gap, two telecommunication firms… joined the consortium**.”

### CA4 — Bloc security versus global interoperability
- **Type:** Negative-sum coordination problem.
- **Individual incentive:** Each major power builds controlled routes to reduce dependence.
- **Collective outcome:** Higher costs and fragmented supplier access, although additional physical capacity may improve redundancy.
- **Anchor:** “**both the United States and China building parallel systems with overlapping geographies**.”

## 6. Game structures

### GAME1 — US–China supplier-exclusion game
- **Type:** Repeated negative-sum game with elements of prisoner’s dilemma.
- **Players:** ACT1 and ACT2.
- **US strategies:** Permit Chinese participation; subsidize US rivals; threaten sanctions; impose restrictions.
- **Chinese strategies:** Accept exclusion; subsidize bids; restructure ownership; build alternative routes.
- **Payoffs:** Each gains strategic control by excluding the other, but mutual duplication increases cost and political risk.
- **Nash equilibrium:** Reciprocal exclusion and parallel construction, given distrust and the strategic value of network control.
- **Pareto optimal:** No; interoperable expansion could cost less, but neither side trusts the other.
- **Anchor:** US policy excludes Chinese firms while China pursues “**Chinese-controlled connections**.”

### GAME2 — SeaMeWe-6 supplier-selection game
- **Type:** Coordination game under coercive external pressure.
- **Players:** Consortium members, HMN Tech, SubCom, and the US government.
- **Payoff structure:** HMN offers lower direct price; SubCom offers lower sanctions-adjusted risk.
- **Equilibrium:** Majority selection of SubCom.
- **Anchor:** “**a majority opting to award the contract to SubCom**.”

### GAME3 — France’s hedging game
- **Type:** Repeated coordination/hedging game.
- **Players:** France, United States, China, Orange, and other partners.
- **Strategies:** Align with one bloc, selectively participate in both, or build autonomous capacity.
- **Equilibrium:** Selective engagement plus domestic capacity, constrained by US pressure.
- **Cooperation conditions:** Projects must remain commercially useful without triggering prohibitive sanctions.
- **Anchor:** France’s balanced stance is “**increasingly undermined by growing US pressure**.”

### GAME4 — Oligopolistic cable-supply competition
- **Type:** Repeated strategic competition among four suppliers.
- **Players:** SubCom, ASN, NEC, HMN Tech.
- **Payoff:** Large contracts, learning effects, ship utilization, and market share.
- **Equilibrium:** State-backed competition around a small supplier base.
- **Anchor:** The segment is “**highly oligopolistic, dominated by just four globally relevant suppliers**.”

## 7. Perverse incentives and gaming opportunities

### PERV1 — Security intervention produces network duplication
- **Intended outcome:** Reduce exposure to a rival’s infrastructure.
- **Actual outcome:** Rival-controlled alternative cables along similar routes.
- **Mechanism:** Exclusion makes duplication strategically valuable.
- **Severity:** Significant but mixed, because duplication also adds capacity and redundancy.
- **Anchor:** PEACE “**closely mirrors the route of SeaMeWe-6**.”

### PERV2 — Sanctions reduce influence over, but not use of, Chinese cables
- **Intended outcome:** Separate US firms and data from sanctioned systems.
- **Actual outcome:** US firms may not buy capacity, yet data can still transit those systems.
- **Mechanism:** Internet routing and third-party use are not fully controlled by contracting restrictions.
- **Severity:** Moderate.
- **Anchor:** “**some of their data will inevitably pass through these cables**.”

### PERV3 — Resilience policy can reinforce inefficient national favoritism
- **Intended outcome:** Secure supply and maintenance.
- **Potential outcome:** Protected domestic contractors face weaker competitive discipline.
- **Mechanism:** Strategic designation shields firms from market exit.
- **Confidence:** Inferential.
- **Anchor:** Member states “**continue to prioritize the interests of domestic industries**.”

### GAM1 — Corporate ownership restructuring to reduce political stigma
- **Strategy:** Transfer ownership while preserving close political and industrial ties.
- **Metric exploited:** Formal ownership rather than effective affiliation.
- **Example:** Huawei sold a majority stake in HMN Tech to Hengtong.
- **Letter versus spirit:** Formal distancing from Huawei without eliminating Western concern over Chinese political ties.
- **Detectability:** Moderate.
- **Anchor:** “**This move was intended to distance HMN Tech from Western security concerns surrounding Huawei**.”

### GAM2 — Security framing as industrial protection
- **Strategy:** Present supplier exclusion as risk mitigation while also steering contracts toward domestic firms.
- **Metric exploited:** “Trusted supplier” status, which is difficult to separate from nationality.
- **Detectability:** Difficult where intelligence is secret.
- **Anchor:** Interventions cite security but “**often aim to support domestic champions**.”

### GAM3 — Subsidized below-market bidding
- **Strategy:** Use state backing to submit bids whose price does not reflect full economic cost.
- **Metric exploited:** Tender price.
- **Letter versus spirit:** Competes through the formal bid process while potentially undermining market-based comparison.
- **Prevalence:** Common in strategic Chinese projects according to the article.
- **Anchor:** China uses subsidies “**to distort competition in favor of domestic firms**.”

### GAM4 — Training grants as indirect bid support
- **Strategy:** Improve a favored supplier’s package through grants to consortium partners.
- **Metric exploited:** Total proposal value rather than supplier price alone.
- **Detectability:** Easy because grants are official.
- **Anchor:** Grants were used “**to make the SubCom proposal more attractive**.”

## 8. Rent-seeking assessment

### RENT1 — Supplier protection through geopolitical restrictions
- **Rent seekers:** Domestic suppliers benefiting from rival exclusion, especially ACT6 and potentially ACT7.
- **Type:** Regulatory/strategic-market rent.
- **Mechanism:** Security restrictions narrow the eligible supplier pool.
- **Value extracted:** Not quantified; likely substantial given project values around $500–600 million.
- **Value created:** Potential security assurance and domestic industrial capacity.
- **Deadweight loss:** Higher procurement costs and reduced competition.
- **Who pays:** Consortium members and ultimately capacity customers.
- **Anchor:** HMN’s bid was “**substantially lower**” before US-backed adjustments and sanctions pressure.

### RENT2 — Hub-state ownership rents
- **Rent seeker:** State-controlled firms in geographically indispensable jurisdictions.
- **Type:** Infrastructure-access rent.
- **Mechanism:** Landing geography is exchanged for ownership stakes or influence.
- **Value created:** Connectivity, maintenance responsibility, and local investment may accompany the rent.
- **Anchor:** Egypt used its position to ensure Telecom Egypt “**holds stakes in many of these projects**.”

The evidence does **not** establish that either mechanism is pure rent extraction. Security and resilience may create genuine value, so the rent-seeking classification remains qualified.

## 9. Outcomes and goal alignment

### GOAL1 — Reliable global connectivity
- **Stated goal:** Robust capacity for internet traffic and economic activity.
- **Alignment:** INC1 and some route duplication support it; bloc exclusion can undermine interoperability.
- **Outcome:** Continued network expansion.
- **Anchor:** Cables carry “**over 97% of all internet traffic**.”

### GOAL2 — National security
- **Stated goal:** Limit surveillance, sabotage, and dependency risks.
- **Alignment:** Sanctions, screening, domestic capacity, and redundancy partly support it.
- **Outcome:** Lower exposure to selected suppliers but greater geopolitical contestation.
- **Anchor:** States view cables as “**strategic assets and potential security risks**.”

### GOAL3 — Network resilience
- **Stated goal:** Redundancy, repair capacity, and protection.
- **Alignment:** Public investment and duplicated routes help; private return incentives underprovide it.
- **Anchor:** Governments should prioritize “**creating cable redundancies and investing in deployment and maintenance capacities**.”

### GOAL4 — Industrial sovereignty
- **Stated goal:** Preserve domestic manufacturing, ships, technology, and employment.
- **Alignment:** Chinese subsidies and French nationalization directly support it.
- **Anchor:** Losing ASN risked “**layoffs and a loss of industrial capacity**.”

### Key outcomes
- **OUT1 — SeaMeWe-6 awarded to SubCom:** Benefits SubCom and US strategy; harms HMN Tech and withdrawing Chinese investors. Commercial efficiency is ambiguous because sanctions changed the relevant cost.
- **OUT2 — Chinese route duplication:** Benefits Chinese suppliers and adds capacity; imposes duplication costs and deepens bloc rivalry.
- **OUT3 — ASN nationalization:** Preserves French capability and access; transfers commercial risk to taxpayers.
- **OUT4 — Continued globalization through overlapping systems:** Contrary to simple fragmentation narratives, expansion continues.
- **OUT5 — Persistent systemic vulnerability:** Cables remain hard to monitor and protect. Anchor: “**Most subsea cables are located at depths that make them difficult to monitor and protect**.”

**Overall alignment score: 0.54.** The system performs well at producing commercially demanded capacity, moderately at preserving national strategic capabilities, and poorly at supplying global security as a public good.

## 10. Incentive cascades

### CASC1 — US sanctions cascade
1. US authorities signal sanctions against HMN Tech.
2. Consortium members recalculate total risk, not merely bid price.
3. SubCom becomes the lower-risk option.
4. The consortium selects SubCom.
5. Chinese investors withdraw around 20% of financing.
6. Replacement investors and higher incumbent contributions fill the gap.
7. China develops alternative controlled routes.
8. The final outcome is supplier exclusion plus network duplication.

**Total effect:** Mixed—US exclusion succeeds locally, but Chinese-controlled alternatives proliferate.  
**Anchor:** “**Once the threat of sanctions became more concrete, other consortium members were getting second thoughts**.”

### CASC2 — French supply-security cascade
1. Nokia considers selling ASN under financial pressure.
2. France fears foreign acquisition, plant closure, layoffs, and supply bottlenecks.
3. APE acquires 80% for €350 million.
4. France secures manufacturing and laying capacity.
5. Taxpayers assume commercial exposure, while France gains strategic autonomy.

**Total effect:** Mixed-positive, conditional on ASN’s commercial performance.  
**Anchor:** “**losing access to ASN could create bottlenecks for critical French projects**.”

## 11. Redesign recommendations

### REC1 — Pay explicitly for resilience
- **Problem:** Private investors cannot capture the full benefit of spare routes, repair capacity, and monitoring.
- **Change:** Competitive public contracts for redundancy, rapid repair readiness, and geographic diversification.
- **Expected improvement:** Converts a diffuse public benefit into a bankable revenue stream.
- **Potential gaming:** Firms may relabel commercially planned capacity as “redundancy” or overstate readiness.
- **Safeguard:** Pay for audited service availability and repair times rather than capital expenditure alone.
- **Feasibility:** 0.75.
- **Anchor:** Public investment may be needed where private investment is “**insufficient to address systemic risks**.”

### REC2 — Replace nationality-only restrictions with auditable risk standards
- **Problem:** “Trusted supplier” rules can become disguised industrial protection.
- **Change:** Apply transparent technical, ownership, governance, and cybersecurity tests to all suppliers.
- **Expected improvement:** Reduces rent-seeking while preserving legitimate security screening.
- **Potential gaming:** Complex ownership chains and nominal corporate separation.
- **Safeguard:** Beneficial-ownership disclosure and continuing compliance audits.
- **Feasibility:** 0.55.
- **Anchor:** HMN’s ownership change was intended “**to distance HMN Tech from Western security concerns**.”

### REC3 — Coordinate sanctions with continuity planning
- **Problem:** Exclusion can create funding gaps and induce rival duplication.
- **Change:** Before sanctions, assess replacement financing, supplier capacity, customer effects, and likely retaliation.
- **Expected improvement:** Limits project disruption and unintended route fragmentation.
- **Potential gaming:** Firms may exaggerate replacement costs to avoid restrictions.
- **Feasibility:** 0.65.
- **Anchor:** Chinese withdrawal removed “**approximately 20% of the total investment volume**.”

### REC4 — Build regional incident-response and repair pools
- **Problem:** Protection is transnational, but governance is nationally fragmented.
- **Change:** Pool cable ships, threat intelligence, repair stock, and emergency landing permissions regionally.
- **Expected improvement:** Faster repairs and less free riding.
- **Potential gaming:** Members may undercontribute while relying on pooled assets.
- **Safeguard:** Contributions based on landing count, traffic exposure, and ability to pay.
- **Feasibility:** 0.70.
- **Anchor:** Governance frameworks should “**ensure the integrity of the network**.”

### REC5 — Impose commercial governance on strategic state ownership
- **Problem:** Nationalization can preserve capacity but invite politicized investment and protected inefficiency.
- **Change:** Give ASN an independent board, published strategic mandate, return targets, and periodic review.
- **Expected improvement:** Separates legitimate resilience obligations from commercial performance.
- **Potential gaming:** Costs may be shifted into vaguely defined sovereignty functions.
- **Feasibility:** 0.80.
- **Anchor:** France characterized the acquisition as both “**strategic**” and “**profitable**.”

### REC6 — Preserve multi-supplier capability
- **Problem:** A four-firm oligopoly creates bottlenecks and strategic dependence.
- **Change:** Support entry, workforce training, interoperable standards, and ship capacity without guaranteeing individual firms’ market shares.
- **Expected improvement:** More competition and resilience.
- **Potential gaming:** Subsidy recipients may remain permanently dependent on public support.
- **Feasibility:** 0.50.
- **Anchor:** Supply is “**dominated by just four globally relevant suppliers**.”

## 12. Cross-document patterns

Only one source document was provided, so true cross-document comparison is unavailable. Within the article, three internally recurring patterns appear:

1. **Market-security misalignment:** Commercial return drives construction, while states seek security and control.
2. **Security-industrial bundling:** Measures framed as security policy also advance domestic suppliers.
3. **Exclusion-induced adaptation:** Blocking one route or supplier changes rather than eliminates investment, often producing parallel infrastructure.

The main contested diagnosis inside the article is whether current rivalry means deglobalization. The article rejects simple fragmentation: “**the network is currently getting upgraded and expanded**,” while recognizing simultaneous attempts at “**decoupling**.”

### Counter-evidence

- The article does not show that all state intervention is protectionist: cables are genuinely vulnerable because they are “**difficult to monitor and protect**.”
- Chinese subsidies may distort price competition, so HMN Tech’s lower bid does not necessarily establish superior economic efficiency: China uses subsidies “**to distort competition in favor of domestic firms**.”
- Duplication is not purely wasteful because rapidly growing demand may absorb additional capacity; demand currently “**surges with the expansion of AI and cloud-based services**.”
- France’s nationalization need not become an inefficient bailout: the state expects the acquisition to be “**profitable**” and forecasts substantial revenue growth.
- Private incentives do sometimes produce reliability because hyperscalers seek “**vast bandwidth and reliable interconnections**”; the underprovision problem is strongest for system-wide redundancy and security, not ordinary service quality.
- US intervention did not completely remove Chinese participation from SeaMeWe-6 because “**China Unicom remained**.”
