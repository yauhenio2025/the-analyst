# Reading

The article’s most consequential distinction is between classifying AUKUS solely as a geopolitical security arrangement and classifying it through **militarised neoliberalism (MN)**. These are not mutually exclusive memberships: the authors explicitly describe AUKUS as both a security partnership and a mutation of neoliberalism [F1]. Their objection is therefore not that geopolitical or alliance classifications are false, but that state-centric versions omit the economic institutions, social relations, and fractions of capital through which AUKUS operates [F2] [F15].

The taxonomy is thus organised around a core question: **Is AUKUS classified only by its interstate security form, or also by the political-economic relations it reorganises?** The article answers “both,” then places the second classification hierarchically beneath the concept of militarised neoliberalism: bipartisanship and regulatory control provide its political foundation; combined capitalist development supplies its industrial pattern; and venture capital and start-ups constitute its emerging business-class component [F5] [F8] [F9]. Japan and South Korea cross-cut the member/non-member boundary because they participate functionally in Pillar II without becoming parties to the trilateral pact [F10].

What is established is a nonexclusive classification of AUKUS, explicit definitions of MN and the older Washington Consensus, named institutional and business instances, and several context-dependent membership rules. What remains unestablished are exhaustive category boundaries, numerical thresholds for inclusion, and a general test determining how much security-economic linkage is sufficient to classify arrangements other than AUKUS as militarised neoliberalism.

### Counter-evidence

- The article does not deny AUKUS’s security or geopolitical character; it explicitly preserves that classification [F1] [F15].
- Formal opposition complicates the depiction of seamless bipartisanship: some Australian Labor branches and unions opposed AUKUS, and US Republican senators threatened to block submarine-enabling legislation. The article nevertheless treats these as internal opposition within broader bipartisan support.
- Pillar I delays and possible non-delivery weaken claims that AUKUS necessarily produces Australian sovereign submarine capability or benefits all anticipated domestic defence firms [F16].
- Named beneficiaries establish membership only for those instances; they do not demonstrate that the article’s business categories are exhaustive or that every firm in the same sector benefits [F13].

### Open questions

- What minimum degree of linkage between market coordination and security institutions is sufficient for an arrangement to qualify as militarised neoliberalism?
- Are MN, the New Washington Consensus, and state developmentalism nested levels of one taxonomy, or partially competing descriptions? The article relates them but supplies no exhaustive hierarchy.
- Which Pillar II activities confer “partner” status, and can participation be ranked below formal AUKUS membership?
- How should firms be classified when they cross ownership, sector, financing, and procurement categories—for example, civilian conglomerates producing military technology with state finance?
- How would Japan’s proposed export revisions alter the exact set of admissible equipment, destinations, and participating firms?
- Evidence of implementation, contract performance, ownership, and funding flows would be needed to determine whether announced beneficiaries actually realise the anticipated gains.
