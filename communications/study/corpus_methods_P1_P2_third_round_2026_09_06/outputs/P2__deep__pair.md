# Corpus reading

## Shared inquiry and position map

The collection asks how contemporary security strategy reorganises markets, firms, infrastructure, and state institutions without simply replacing neoliberal or privately organised economic structures.

- **[aukus] Wijaya and Hayes, “AUKUS ‘behind the scenes’”** examines contemporary AUKUS, principally its political economy and Pillar II, while extending attention to Japan and South Korea. Its central answer is that AUKUS is simultaneously a security partnership and “a mutation of neoliberalism” arising through geoeconomic conflict and bipartisan politics [F1]. Its distinctive role is to theorise **militarised neoliberalism**: security imperatives are layered onto economic institutions, regulatory controls create selected markets, and business, political, and military networks are reconnected. The article expressly does **not** determine whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific toward conflict [F2], and its claim of strong bipartisanship is qualified by implementation delays and uncertainty [F3].

- **[subsea] Abels, “Private Network Realignment”** examines the privately owned global subsea-cable network through the SeaMeWe-6 conflict and France’s nationalisation of Alcatel Submarine Networks. Its central answer is that states pursue **private network realignment**: retrospective political intervention intended to redirect infrastructure and associated markets by influencing corporations [F4]. Its distinctive role is to place corporate network power and state strategy in the same explanatory frame rather than treating firms as state instruments. Its major qualification is that realignment encounters infrastructure inertia and is consequently uneven and incomplete [F5]. France demonstrates room for autonomous industrial strategy, but only for a well-positioned network hub with relevant industrial capacity [F6].

The publication years—2025 for [aukus] and 2026 for [subsea]—do not by themselves establish an intellectual sequence. The defensible relation is conceptual continuity and qualification: both describe security-driven state intervention within market-organised systems, but they analyse different objects and use different mechanisms and warrants.

## Strongest agreement and strongest qualification

The strongest substantive agreement concerns the coexistence of state activism and market organisation. [aukus] says AUKUS “reconnects, rather than deconstructs, the global economy” in favour of selected interests [F9]. [subsea], from a different empirical route, argues that cable networks have “been shaped by market forces more than by state planning” [F10]. Together, these answers license neither a return-of-the-state narrative nor a claim that corporations operate independently of strategic policy. They instead describe political attempts to redirect markets, capital, firms, and infrastructure whose prior organisation continues to matter [F11].

The sources nevertheless weight the result differently. [aukus] foregrounds effective market creation: “regulatory controls generate market opportunities” for lead firms in selected sectors [F7]. [subsea] foregrounds incomplete control: realignment strategies “encounter resistance” from infrastructure systems [F5]. This is not a contradiction after alignment. The first proposition concerns the distributive effect of policy within AUKUS-linked sectors; the second concerns the capacity to redirect an inherited, transnational physical network. Market opportunities can be generated even when network restructuring remains partial.

The most important unresolved difference is therefore one of **effectiveness and generalisability**. [aukus] offers a broad causal reconstruction in which bipartisanship, regulatory controls, industrial policy, and security institutions produce new coalitions and accumulation routes. [subsea] warns that outcomes depend on network and corporate position [F13] and that even major powers cannot fully engineer infrastructure development. The collection does not test the AUKUS argument against the same positional and infrastructural criteria used in the cable cases. Any integrated claim that militarised neoliberalism operates through private network realignment should therefore be labelled an **analyst inference**, not textual inheritance.

## Corpus judgment

The collection jointly licenses the conclusion that contemporary security strategy often operates **through**, rather than simply against, market institutions and private economic power. State agencies create opportunities, redirect capital, influence firms, and attempt to reshape infrastructure; their effects are selective and conditioned by inherited institutional, corporate, and network positions.

It does not license a general claim that states have regained comprehensive control, that all strategic intervention is equally effective, or that AUKUS-linked engagement constitutes autonomous positioning. Nor does it determine whether AUKUS increases the probability of Indo-Pacific conflict.

The principal question requiring further evidence is whether the broad AUKUS pattern—bipartisan policy fusion, selected market creation, and incorporation of business actors—faces the same forms of corporate resistance and infrastructural inertia demonstrated in the cable sector. Deciding that question would require passages or comparative evidence tracing failed, redirected, or circumvented AUKUS initiatives and showing how firm and network positions affected implementation. Resolving the conflict question would instead require evidence directly linking AUKUS’s political-economic mechanisms to specified conflict risks or deterrent effects over a defined period.

### Counter-evidence

- [aukus] prevents a categorical account of settled implementation: “Yet, as widely reported elsewhere, severe delays emerging out of the devastating state of the UK submarine-building industry” [F3].
- [subsea] prevents a categorical account of state control: “Strategies of network realignment encounter resistance” from other states and infrastructure inertia [F5].
- France’s example does not establish unrestricted middle-power autonomy because its companies lack resources comparable to US and Chinese competitors [F15].

### Open questions

- Whether AUKUS will tip the Indo-Pacific toward conflict remains explicitly unanswered [F2].
- Whether AUKUS initiatives face corporate resistance and infrastructure inertia comparable to subsea networks is not tested.
- The retained [aukus] evidence does not establish whether Japan or South Korea pursues autonomous positioning rather than alliance-linked engagement.
- The two cable cases do not demonstrate the long-term generalisability of private network realignment across other infrastructure sectors.
