# Comparative reading

## Comparison question and position map

**Comparison question:** How do states reconnect market-organised strategic networks to security objectives, through which regulatory, financial, industrial, or coercive instruments, and with what demonstrated limits or results?

This question is justified because both documents reject a purely state-centric account while examining renewed state intervention in networks shaped by firms and markets. The comparison is nevertheless bounded: *aukus* studies a security partnership and its associated political-economic networks, whereas *subsea* studies a physical communications network, a procurement contest, and ownership of a strategic supplier. Their instruments can be compared functionally, but their outputs cannot be measured on a common scale.

- **[aukus] Wijaya and Hayes, “AUKUS ‘behind the scenes’”** treats AUKUS—announced by Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States in 2021—as both a security partnership and a “mutation of neoliberalism” arising under bipartisanship [F1]. Its distinctive role is to theorise **militarised neoliberalism**: market coordination becomes linked to security institutions and alliances, with Japan and South Korea included as associated Pillar II cases rather than AUKUS members [F1]. Its evidence concerns regulations, public financing, industrial policy, and venture capital, usually as reported policies or mechanisms rather than independently verified economic effects. It expressly does not determine whether AUKUS will move the Indo-Pacific toward conflict [F18].

- **[subsea] Abels, “Private Network Realignment”** examines the United States, China, and France through two principal cases: the US–China contest over SeaMeWe-6 and France’s 2024 nationalisation of Alcatel Submarine Networks (ASN) [F2]. Its distinctive contribution is the concept of **private network realignment**—state attempts to reshape largely privately owned infrastructure by influencing corporations. It contrasts Chinese industrial support, US regulatory-coercive pressure, and French industrial ownership [F2]. Its strategy categories are ideal types rather than mutually exclusive empirical boxes [F20], and France’s supplier-nationalisation route cannot automatically be extended to differently positioned network hubs or other infrastructure sectors [F21].

Publication order does not establish argumentative development between the texts. The 2025 AUKUS article and 2026 subsea article offer independent but compatible routes to a common claim about the coexistence of market organisation and state intervention; the supplied evidence does not establish direct inheritance.

## Main comparison: common pattern, divergence, and exception

### A common state–market pattern

The strongest supported continuity is not “deglobalisation” or uniform state control. It is the effort to **redirect networks that remain organised through markets and corporations**. In *aukus*, militarised neoliberalism is described as market coordination “linked to security institutions and specific alliances.” In *subsea*, private network realignment consists of political interventions that reshape predominantly privately owned networks. Together, these support a broad state–market intervention pattern while preserving the difference between a security-industrial partnership and physical cable infrastructure [F3].

Regulation is common to both routes. Australia’s Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act facilitates controlled technology transfers among AUKUS states without export licences [F5]. In SeaMeWe-6, the United States instead uses regulation and threatened sanctions to make a Chinese supplier commercially hazardous to a multinational consortium [F12]. Both affect corporate choice, but in opposite transactional directions: the Australian rule removes barriers within a favoured group, whereas the US cable strategy raises the prospective cost of using a disfavoured supplier.

Public finance is likewise recurrent but not equivalent. Australia’s AUD 2 billion critical-minerals expansion is presented as support for Australian and US manufacturing [F6]. China’s subsidies make HMN Tech’s SeaMeWe-6 bid more competitive [F11]. France goes further into ownership, committing €350 million for 80% of ASN [F14]. These are three forms of market shaping—financing an industrial field, subsidising a strategic bid, and acquiring a supplier—but their success cannot be ranked without a reader-selected standard such as contract capture, retained domestic capacity, delivery speed, or long-term resilience.

### Different mechanisms and different evidential outcomes

The SeaMeWe-6 case provides the clearest reported decision outcome. China’s industrial strategy supported HMN Tech; the United States combined financing with coercive-regulatory leverage; the consortium ultimately selected SubCom [F11] [F12]. On the article’s restricted criterion of the cable-laying sector, the United States is judged “arguably” more successful thus far [F13]. That finding cannot be transferred to the whole global cable network or to AUKUS.

The AUKUS evidence is broader institutionally but weaker as an effectiveness test. It reports appropriations, controls, export-finance changes, and venture mechanisms [F5] [F6] [F7] [F9] [F10]. These show institutional reorganisation, not a common final outcome. South Korea’s amendment removes a financing constraint [F7]; Japan’s rules are associated with industrial contraction [F8]; the UK’s NSSIF connects government and venture investment [F9]. Their units, periods, and dependent variables differ, so they should not be aggregated into a single measure of “industrial success.”

France supplies the main exception to a simple US–China bifurcation. Its state acquires a strategic supplier while the country selectively engages with projects linked to both sides [F14] [F15]. This qualifies any proposition that strategic networks divide neatly into two exclusive spheres. Yet France is not a universal middle-power template: its strategy depends on an existing supplier, landing-hub position, fiscal capacity, and industrial base [F21].

### Testing the network-duplication pattern: PEACE versus EMA

The parallel Chinese cables must be kept separate. PEACE became operational in 2022, was built by HMN Tech, and follows a route similar to SeaMeWe-6 [F16]. EMA is described as a project launched **in direct reaction** to US interference in SeaMeWe-6 [F17]. Shared geography supports a duplication comparison, but it does not transfer EMA’s reactive chronology to PEACE. Thus, the corpus supports a pattern of overlapping infrastructure, with only EMA classified as reactive.

## Supported judgment and missing evidence

Across the collection, the most defensible judgment is that renewed security competition does not simply replace markets with state command. Instead, states use licensing, sanctions, subsidies, public finance, procurement influence, venture mechanisms, and ownership to redirect networks still substantially organised through firms. AUKUS illustrates broad institutional fusion across defence, economic agencies, finance, and technology; SeaMeWe-6 shows direct regulatory-coercive influence over a corporate procurement decision; France demonstrates an autonomous industrial route based on ownership [F3].

No unconditional ranking follows. If the reader’s criterion is **immediate influence over a cable-laying decision**, the supplied evidence conditionally favours the United States in SeaMeWe-6 [F13]. If the criterion is **retention of national supplier capacity**, France’s ASN acquisition is the clearest completed ownership intervention, but its long-term benefits remain unproven [F14]. If the criterion is **breadth of institutional integration**, the AUKUS account documents the widest range of agencies and financial actors, but it does not provide a common measure of realised impact [F4].

The most useful missing evidence would therefore be:

1. comparable before-and-after indicators for production capacity, delivery, employment, prices, and resilience;
2. operational and financing evidence for EMA;
3. a common time series for Chinese, US, and French shares of cable supply, ownership, and laying;
4. independently verified effects of AUKUS funding and regulatory reforms;
5. equivalent evidence for all three AUKUS members and associated Japan and South Korea;
6. corporate decision evidence distinguishing state pressure from commercial incentives;
7. tests of whether duplicated cable routes increase resilience or merely reproduce geopolitical dependence.

The findings cannot be generalised beyond the supplied AUKUS and subsea-cable coverage. No established cultural or translation limitation appears in the texts, but institutional labels such as partnership, partner, hub, nationalisation, and bipartisanship retain case-specific meanings.

### Scope and record qualifications

For **aukus**, the recorded case-identity, case-answer, and comparison-limit assessments are all **inconclusive and unchecked**, despite reports of complete inspection of the abstract, principal sections, conclusion, and notes. The identity record covers AUKUS, its members, Pillars I and II, and the militarised-neoliberalism framework, but gives no closing date and treats Japan and South Korea only as extensions. The answer record identifies numerous policies and mechanisms but warns that these are often commitments rather than independently verified outcomes and lack common units, dates, and denominators. The limits record identifies opposition, delays, and different Japanese and South Korean constraints; it found no established translation issue and no systematic identical-criterion comparison of all participants. These statuses reflect evidence-record limitations, not demonstrated absence from the source.

For **subsea**, the recorded identity, answer, and comparison-limit assessments are likewise **inconclusive and unchecked**, notwithstanding reported complete inspection of the abstract and relevant analytical and case sections. The identity record covers the two principal cases and three state powers but notes unequal objects and time boundaries. The answer record covers China, the United States, France, PEACE, and EMA, while noting that EMA’s operational status is unresolved and causal relationships are reported rather than independently demonstrated. The limits record preserves ideal-type, alignment, network-hub, and sectoral qualifications; it found no established translation issue and notes that other hubs receive only brief treatment.

The corpus-level matrix assessment is **inconclusive, unchecked, and only partially covered**. It supports the cautious common criterion of state intervention in market-coordinated strategic networks, including a shared regulatory element, but not a common effectiveness measure, causal ranking, or universal proposition.

### Counter-evidence

- France’s selective engagement with both US and Chinese projects counters a strict two-bloc alignment model [F15].
- Australian internal opposition and submarine-delivery constraints qualify a uniform bipartisanship-and-implementation pattern [F19].
- PEACE’s parallel route does not carry EMA’s explicitly attributed reactive status [F16] [F17].
- The strategy categories are ideal types, so apparent cases of instrument mixing do not falsify the typology but prevent exclusive classification [F20].

### Open questions

- What are the realised production, employment, resilience, and security effects of AUKUS-linked funding?
- Did South Korea’s capital-limit amendment produce additional defence exports?
- What is EMA’s financing completion, construction, and operational status?
- How durable was the US SeaMeWe-6 advantage beyond the supplier decision?
- Will France’s ASN acquisition retain capacity profitably and improve network resilience?
- How do corporate commercial preferences compare with state pressure in determining cable routes and suppliers?
- No supplied evidence settles a common causal or quantitative effectiveness ranking.
