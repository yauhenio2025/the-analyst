# Comparative reading

## Comparison question and position map

**Comparison question:** How do states reconnect market-organised strategic networks to security objectives, through which regulatory, financial, industrial, or coercive instruments, and with what reported results and limits?

The criteria—strategic object, state instrument, corporate or market mechanism, reported result, and comparability limit—fit both documents’ shared inquiry into state intervention in networks substantially organised through firms. They do not create a common outcome scale: AUKUS is a security-industrial partnership, SeaMeWe-6 is a cable procurement contest, and ASN is a strategic supplier.

- **[aukus] Wijaya and Hayes, “AUKUS ‘behind the scenes’”** examines the Australia–United Kingdom–United States partnership announced in 2021 and extends its analysis to Japan and South Korea [F1]. Its central claim is that AUKUS is not merely a security partnership but a mutation of neoliberalism in which market coordination is linked to security institutions and alliances [F2]. Its distinctive role is therefore conceptual and institutionally broad: it traces regulation, public finance, industrial policy, and venture capital. Most cited effects are policy commitments or reported mechanisms rather than independently verified economic or security outcomes. It expressly brackets whether AUKUS will move the Indo-Pacific toward conflict [F18].

- **[subsea] Abels, “Private Network Realignment”** focuses on the United States, China, and France through the US–China SeaMeWe-6 conflict and France’s 2024 nationalisation of Alcatel Submarine Networks [F3]. Its distinctive claim is that governments attempt to realign predominantly private networks by influencing corporate decisions. In SeaMeWe-6, it contrasts Chinese industrial support with US regulatory-coercive intervention; France supplies an industrial-ownership route [F3]. These strategy categories are ideal types, not exclusive empirical classifications [F20]. France’s route also depends on a network and industrial position that other hubs or sectors may not share [F21].

The documents provide independent, compatible arguments about the coexistence of market organisation and renewed state intervention. Publication dates do not establish inheritance or argumentative progression.

## Main comparison

### State intervention without simple replacement of markets

The strongest cross-document pattern is that security competition redirects networks still organised substantially through firms and markets. The AUKUS article describes market coordination linked to security institutions and alliances; the subsea article places corporations at the centre of many infrastructure networks [F4]. This is a functional continuity, not equivalence between a defence partnership and a physical communications system.

Regulatory intervention is common to both, but its transactional direction differs. Australia’s Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act removes export-licensing barriers among AUKUS states [F5]. The United States’ subsea strategy instead raises the expected cost of working with Chinese suppliers through regulatory and sanctioning pressure [F12]. One facilitates exchange inside a preferred group; the other discourages a disfavoured corporate relationship. Both influence market choice without eliminating private firms from implementation [F19].

Finance and ownership reveal further variation. Australia directs AUD 2 billion toward critical-minerals financing that is presented as supporting Australian and US manufacturing [F6]. China uses subsidies to strengthen HMN Tech’s position in a specific cable bid [F11]. France acquires a strategic supplier through public ownership [F14]. These are respectively field-level financing, bid support, and supplier acquisition. They cannot be ranked without a stated reader criterion such as contract capture, retained capacity, delivery, or resilience.

### Different evidential outcomes

SeaMeWe-6 offers the clearest immediate decision outcome. Chinese subsidies supported HMN Tech, while the United States used regulatory and sanctioning leverage; the consortium voted in late 2021 to award the contract to SubCom [F11] [F12]. On the narrow criterion of cable laying, the article says the United States was “arguably” more successful “thus far” [F13]. This qualification prevents transfer of that advantage to cable ownership, financing, the entire communications network, or AUKUS.

The AUKUS evidence is institutionally broader but less suitable for an effectiveness judgment. It documents financing, transfer rules, export constraints, public venture institutions, and semiconductor policy [F5] [F6] [F7] [F8] [F9] [F10]. These demonstrate different forms of institutional reorganisation. They do not supply common dependent variables: South Korea removes a financing constraint, Japan’s rules are associated with industrial contraction, and the UK establishes a venture-finance route.

France is the principal exception to a strict US–China division. It pursues national ownership while remaining not strictly aligned in communications infrastructure [F14] [F15]. Yet this is not a universal middle-power model. Other hubs occupy different positions, and infrastructure sectors in which geographical nodes matter less provide fewer equivalent opportunities [F21].

### Testing duplication: PEACE and EMA

PEACE and EMA must remain separate cases. PEACE became operational in 2022, was built by HMN Tech, and is owned by a Hengtong subsidiary [F16]. EMA, by contrast, is explicitly attributed as a direct reaction to US interference in SeaMeWe-6 [F17]. Similar routes support a finding of network duplication, but they do not permit EMA’s reactive chronology to be transferred to PEACE. Nor does the supplied evidence establish EMA’s operational completion.

## Supported judgment and missing evidence

Across the collection, renewed security competition does not simply replace markets with state command. States employ licensing, sanctions, subsidies, public finance, venture institutions, and ownership to redirect networks that continue to depend on corporate investment and implementation [F4] [F19]. AUKUS supplies the broadest account of institutional fusion; SeaMeWe-6 shows immediate influence over a procurement decision; France demonstrates an autonomous ownership route.

Only conditional evaluations follow:

- If the criterion is **immediate influence over the SeaMeWe-6 supplier decision**, the evidence favours the United States [F12] [F13].
- If the criterion is **completed intervention to retain a national supplier**, France’s ASN transaction is clearest, although its long-term effects remain unproven [F14].
- If the criterion is **breadth of institutional integration**, the AUKUS account covers the widest range of economic, security, and financial institutions, but does not provide a common measure of realised impact [F2].

The most useful missing evidence is comparable before-and-after data on production capacity, employment, delivery, prices, resilience, and security outcomes; operational and financing evidence for EMA; common time series for national shares of cable ownership, supply, and laying; independently verified effects of AUKUS measures; and corporate evidence separating state pressure from commercial incentives. The collection also cannot determine whether route duplication improves resilience or merely reproduces geopolitical dependence.

No conclusion should be generalised beyond the covered AUKUS institutions, SeaMeWe-6 procurement, PEACE and EMA projects, and French cable-sector intervention. No established translation limitation appears, but terms such as *partner*, *hub*, *nationalisation*, and *bipartisanship* retain case-specific institutional meanings.

### Scope and record qualifications

For **aukus**, the recorded case-identity, case-answer, and comparison-limit outcomes remain **inconclusive and unchecked**, despite reported complete inspection of the abstract, main sections, conclusion, and notes. The identity assessment covers AUKUS, its members, Pillars I and II, and militarised neoliberalism, but identifies no closing date and treats Japan and South Korea as extensions rather than members. The answer assessment identifies numerous instruments and mechanisms but cautions that they are often commitments rather than independently verified outcomes and lack common units, dates, and denominators. The limits assessment records opposition, delays, and distinct Japanese and South Korean constraints; it found no established translation issue and no systematic identical-criterion comparison across all participants. These statuses concern the retained evidence record and do not establish that the source itself lacks answers.

For **subsea**, the identity, answer, and comparison-limit outcomes are also **inconclusive and unchecked**, notwithstanding reported complete inspection of the relevant analytical and case sections. The identity assessment covers the two principal cases and three focal state powers while noting unequal objects and time boundaries. The answer assessment covers China, the United States, France, PEACE, and EMA, but EMA’s operational status remains unresolved and causal relationships are reported rather than independently demonstrated. The limits assessment preserves the ideal-type, alignment, network-position, and sectoral qualifications; no translation issue is established, and other network hubs receive only limited treatment.

The corpus-level matrix assessment remains **inconclusive, unchecked, and partially covered**. It supports a cautious common criterion of state intervention in market-coordinated strategic networks, including regulatory intervention, but not a common effectiveness measure, universal causal proposition, or unconditional ranking.

### Counter-evidence

- France’s non-strict alignment counters a simple two-bloc model [F15].
- Australian opposition and delivery constraints qualify a uniform bipartisanship-and-implementation pattern [F22].
- PEACE’s parallel route does not carry EMA’s explicitly attributed reactive status [F16] [F17].
- Instrument mixing prevents exclusive classification of states under the subsea article’s ideal types [F20].

### Open questions

- What are the realised production, employment, resilience, and security effects of AUKUS-linked measures?
- Did South Korea’s amendment generate additional defence exports?
- What is EMA’s financing, construction, and operational status?
- How durable was the US SeaMeWe-6 advantage beyond the supplier decision?
- Will ASN nationalisation improve French capacity and network resilience?
- How much of each corporate decision resulted from state pressure rather than commercial incentives?
- Do overlapping cable routes increase resilience or reproduce geopolitical dependence?
