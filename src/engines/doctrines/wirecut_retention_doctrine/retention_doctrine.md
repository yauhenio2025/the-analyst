# Retention Doctrine — the merged laws (coherence Phase 6, memo §2.2-C7)

These are the laws of keeping a viewer who did not ask to watch. They are
doctrine, not code: no mechanism downstream measures compliance — YOU apply
them in judgment, and the operator overrules you in review. Every law bends to
grounding: retention craft shapes the TELLING; it never invents facts.

1. **The first three seconds ARE the video.** A viewer decides to stay before
   the fourth second. Those seconds must show the story — its most arresting
   grounded image, motion, or number — never a logo, a card, or throat-
   clearing context. Whatever the opening strategy, the first thing on screen
   earns the next thing.

2. **Open loops, honestly.** Pose a question early and keep it fairly open:
   the viewer stays to see it closed. "Fairly" is binding — the question must
   be real in the source, the withholding must not distort it, and the video
   must actually close it. Curiosity gaps that the ending never pays are
   clickbait, not journalism.

3. **Every beat changes state.** After each clip the viewer must know, feel,
   or expect something they didn't before. A beat that restates, decorates, or
   merely continues is a beat where thumbs leave. If two adjacent beats leave
   the viewer in the same state, one of them is not earning its seconds.

4. **Holds are earned.** Lingering is a spend: a slow beat buys emotional
   weight only when the preceding beats built the credit for it. Plan WHERE
   the film may breathe and say what earns it; an unearned hold is just a
   slow patch.

5. **Modality resets.** Attention habituates to a channel. When the same
   channel has led for several beats — the narrator's voice, the same shot
   scale, text doing the work — hand the lead to another: let a picture answer
   a spoken question, a figure land as designed text, silence follow speech.
   The reset re-opens the viewer's attention without new content.

6. **Pattern interrupts, placed not sprinkled.** A deliberate break in the
   established rhythm — a hard cut after dissolves, a wide after closes, a
   sudden quiet — resets the habituation clock. Plan a few, at the story's own
   turns; an interrupt at a random moment is noise, and interrupts everywhere
   are a new (exhausting) pattern.

7. **Signal progress.** A viewer stays longer when they can feel where they
   are in the story's shape — a question narrowing, a count advancing, a
   journey visibly nearer its end. Let structure show through: the film should
   feel like it is going somewhere specific, because it is.

8. **The motif pays off.** One concrete image, planted early, returning
   transformed, closing the film — that is the cheapest structure a video can
   own. A motif that appears once is set dressing; plant it, thread it, pay it
   off.

9. **End before the summary.** The film ends on its strongest closing beat —
   the consequence, the unresolved question, the motif's final turn. It never
   ends on a recap. The moment the video starts summarizing itself, it has
   already ended; cut there — and strike the flag: the last spoken line must
   not tell the viewer what the film meant. One sentence after the strongest
   line spoils the ending.

The storytelling study (2026-09-02) adds six laws, from the craft shelf:

10. **Need-to-know release.** Curiosity before exposition: no fact arrives
    before a beat has made the viewer want it (a fact delivered unasked is
    spoon-feeding). The one comprehension fact stays early; the film's
    secret — the answer, the observation, the identity, the opposer's
    history — lands last or nearly. Name a force early, show it whole late.
    The one legal way to give the ending away is hindsight: the outcome
    first, then the actors' recorded confidence played straight against it,
    the narrator never naming the gap.

11. **Never the middle rung.** Every movement is a documented particular
    (time, place, actor, action) or a stated claim anchored by one concrete
    — never a summary that is neither felt nor stated. Alternate them; never
    two scenic movements in a row. The workhorse between them is the half
    scene: a summary beat carrying one particular and one verbatim line.

12. **Suspense over surprise.** Uncertainty about how or why, or the viewer
    knowing what the actors did not, holds longer than a twist. A surprise
    needs a schema before it — a documented expectation held and then
    reversed by the record's own outcome; the confident wrong beat sits
    right before the turn. False mystery (withholding a fact the viewer
    needs to follow) and cheap surprise (a turn nothing prepared) are both
    banned. Prepare the turn with an omen or a plant — a recorded detail
    moved earlier — never a line that announces it.

13. **The people in the car.** A loss or shock before anyone is on screen
    is information, not feeling. When the hook is an event, the beat before
    it belongs to the person it lands on, as the source names them; where
    the source names no one, open on the paradox or the figure and film the
    catastrophe as a claim, not a drama.

14. **Never take the viewer where they want to go.** Every movement ends on
    something unresolved; the next movement re-enters elsewhere or quietly —
    the calm after a turn raises tension. A movement that answers its own
    question in its last beat, followed by a movement that continues in
    place, is a gratified boundary and the cut where the viewer leaves. After
    a reveal, one beat of reported reaction or decision before the next.

15. **Feeling before argument.** The viewer feels first, imagines second,
    and understands as an aftereffect. Plan the feeling per movement before
    the claim; the first minute carries an emotion the documented events
    can produce, or the film is an explainer. Never name the feeling — give
    the documented object or action that produces it, and let a calm picture
    under a grave line, or music against meaning, do the pulling.
