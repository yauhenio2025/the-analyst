# The storytelling lessons for a 45-second-to-3-minute grounded narrated video

> Synthesis of the operator's reading notes (11 notebooks, 196 pages, ~25 books) for Wirecut's actual form: one voice, generated pictures, subtitles, a source text that may not be added to. Written 2026-09-02 from a full read of the transcripts. Per-topic detail and page citations live in `lessons/`.

## 0. The one-line diagnosis

Jack Hart, writing about narrative non-fiction, puts the default news form on the middle rungs of the ladder of abstraction: too general to picture, too specific to mean — "not particularly meaningful *or* dramatic". Everything else in the shelf is a way off those rungs: down into a documented moment, up into a stated meaning, and back. The books agree on how a story holds someone who did not ask to watch:

- it is **one question**, posed early, tested in the middle, answered at the end;
- information arrives **when it is wanted**, not when it is convenient;
- every unit **turns** something, and the turns get bigger;
- there is a **face on the stake**;
- the ending **decides the meaning**, and nobody explains it.

Wirecut's doctrine already holds several of these (the hook, the promise, held-question debts, the motif, "every beat changes state", "end before the summary"). What the shelf adds is the part between the hook and the close: how the middle of a film is built so the viewer stays through it.

## 1. What the shelf is

Twenty-odd books, most about feature screenplays: McKee (*Story*, *Character*), Truby, Yorke, Vogler, Gulino (*The Sequence Approach*; *The Science of Screenwriting* with Shears), Myers, Edson, Chamberlain, Bell, Weiland, Iglesias, Price, Bork, Schechter, Booker, Pelican, Trottier, Hauge, Mercurio, Howard & Mabley, Williams. Two are about non-fiction and matter most here: Jon Franklin's *Writing for Story* and Jack Hart's *Storycraft*. One is an audio-storytelling handbook on the narrator's voice.

The note-taker's own headings and highlights are the selection criterion. Where they cluster, the lesson is theirs; where a book said something they did not flag, it is not in this document.

## 2. The twelve laws

Each law states what the books hold, what it changes for a Wirecut film, and the question a script editor (human or model) would ask. Citations point to the lesson documents.

### Law 1 — The film is one question, posed, deliberated, answered
*Structure L2, L10; Theme L4; Emotions L3.*

Frank Daniel's definition, via Gulino: somebody wants something badly and is having difficulty getting it. That implies a question; a question has three parts and no more. Act one poses it (the complicating event, Franklin), act two deliberates it (attempts, discards, swings), act three answers it. Schechter adds scale: a good central question has a part that matters to many, a part that matters to a few, and a part that matters to one.

**For Wirecut:** the spine has a `promise` (setup/payoff) and the script has held-question debts, but nothing requires the *middle* to test the question. A film whose middle lists evidence is a report with a hook. The question should be stated in one sentence from the source, at three scales where the source supports them: the systemic stake, the human stake (a named person or community), and the viewer's own (what they can no longer believe, or what changes for them).

**Check:** *State the question. Point to the beat that poses it, the beats that test candidate answers, and the beat that answers it.*

### Law 2 — Release on need-to-know: make them want it, then withhold as long as the story allows
*Anticipation L1, L2, L7; Expositions L5.*

Price: never give information until the story requires it; never give important information until you have created a desire for it; then dangle. McKee: exposition is paced like everything else — least important first, critical last, and the critical facts are secrets. Gulino: curiosity first, then exposition. Bell: act first, explain later; give the ten percent above the surface.

This is the shelf's sharpest challenge to Wirecut's habits. The storyboard doctrine orders "context → problem → evidence"; the verdict telling "states the claim early, then proves it". Both hand the viewer neutral atoms before they have asked anything. The correction is not to hide the claim but to make the viewer *want* it: open on the paradox, the unexplained outcome, the number that does not add up; give the one fact needed for comprehension; release the rest as each beat makes the next necessary. Telegraph the *destination* (the vote, the date, the verdict is coming); withhold the *answer*.

**Check:** *For the film's three most important facts, which beat made the viewer want each one before it arrived? Which fact is the film's "secret", and is it last or nearly?*

### Law 3 — Oscillate on the ladder: no movement may sit on the middle rungs
*Expositions L1–L4, L8.*

Hart: emotion lives on the lower rungs (a named person, a dated moment, an object, an action), meaning on the upper (a claim about a class of things); good narrative "constantly ascends and descends". Franklin: five threads (time, place, character, subject, mood) break at every zoom and must be re-established at once. Hart's "turn" is a *sudden* climb from a particular to the macrocosm it represents, then a return.

**For Wirecut:** the approach contracts declare a `zoom` per movement (in/out/hold), which is the right instrument aimed at the wrong target. The law is not "hold high" or "hold low" but "never hold *middle*": each movement is either a documented particular the picture can stage or a stated generalisation the narration can carry. Scenic narrative (present tense, process, one place, slow cut) is legal only where the source describes a moment with time, place and actors; elsewhere the film stays summary and moves fast. Explanations belong *inside* documented action — pause the event where something hangs, explain, return — and the pause may be as long as the suspense earns.

**Check:** *For each movement, name the rung: a particular thing in the source, or a claim about a class of things. If neither, rewrite. After each zoom, which threads broke and does the next line restore them?*

### Law 4 — Every movement turns a value, and the turns build
*Scene L1, L2, L4; Structure L5; Emotions L1, L2.*

McKee: a scene turns the charge of a value at stake (justice/injustice, known/hidden, solvent/ruined) or it is exposition and should be dissolved into the scenes that turn. Turns happen only two ways: on action or on revelation. Truby: revelations are the gears of plot and each must be stronger than the last. Trottier and Truby: the last line of a unit is its strongest; the unit is an inverted triangle ending on its key word. Iglesias: the two kinds of change are discoveries (knowledge) and decisions (action).

**For Wirecut:** the retention law "every beat changes state" is stated as knowledge; McKee's version is stated as value and is the stronger test at movement scale. Non-fiction turns mostly on revelation, so the *order of disclosures is the structure*: list the source's reveals, rank them by force, release them ascending, spend the biggest last but one. The screenwriter's "cadence" law (an act may end on a question) generalises: every movement ends on its strongest line, whatever kind.

**Check:** *Which value does each movement move, from what charge to what? Write the reveals in screen order — does each outrank the last? Is each movement's strongest sentence its last?*

### Law 5 — The felt turns: complication, lock, midpoint mirror, low point, decision, aftertaste
*Structure L4, L6, L10, L12, L13; Scene L5.*

The models disagree on counts (five acts, seven plot points, eight sequences, twelve stages, twenty-three goal sequences) and agree on the turns the audience feels. The one Wirecut lacks a home for is the **midpoint**: Gulino's culmination that shows the ending or its mirror opposite; Edson's sequence whose "mood and storytelling style differ from anything else"; Yorke's "key knowledge" followed by doubt and regression. Truby and Chamberlain add the shape of the first turn: the inciting event that feels like escape and is the trap; the Catch — what did they get that they didn't want. Mercurio: zig before you zag — the wrong expectation gets its most confident beat right before the reversal. Price: the second-act ending has a few shapes (dark moment then spark; nail in the coffin; false victory) and the story, not the payoff, decides which.

**For Wirecut:** the pattern-interrupt law wants one beat that breaks the grammar; the books say where — at the centre, on the moment the outcome looked to be going the other way. Even a 90-second film has a middle, and sources usually contain the mirror (the year the numbers looked best; the reform that seemed to work). The record decides the low-point shape; the desk may not bend it.

**Check:** *Is there a beat near the centre that shows the outcome's opposite or glimpses it early, and does the ending refer back to it? Is the beat before the film's turn the one where the wrong expectation is strongest? What actually followed the lowest point in the source — does the film say the true shape?*

### Law 6 — Suspense over surprise; hindsight is non-fiction's dramatic irony
*Anticipation L3, L4, L9; Emotions L5, L10.*

Price: mystery aims at the past (curiosity), surprise at the present (shock), suspense at the future (fearful anticipation) — and suspense is strongest and lasts longest. Iglesias: suspense = empathy + likelihood of threat + uncertainty of outcome; curiosity is not knowing the goal, suspense is knowing it. Hitchcock: fifteen minutes of tension beats ten seconds of surprise. Gulino: dramatic irony — the audience knows more than the actors — pushes attention into the future and gives dull lines double meaning; it is bracketed by a revelation and a recognition.

**For Wirecut:** a news video knows its outcome, which seems to kill uncertainty. Two honest moves restore it. First, shift the uncertainty to *why* or *how* (the mechanism, the cause) rather than *whether*. Second, use hindsight as heightened suspense: tell the outcome early, then follow the actors who did not know — the record says what they believed at the time — and land the recognition. Surprise should be rare and only where the source itself springs it; a surprise twist needs a schema first, so the plausible wrong version deserves screen time even in sixty seconds.

**Check:** *Where does the film's uncertainty live — outcome, cause, or mechanism? Which of the three engines does each act run, and is it deliberate? Before each reveal, has the viewer been given enough to predict the wrong thing?*

### Law 7 — Antagonism at honest full strength; the counter-idea runs through the film; the ending decides the theme
*Conflict L1–L3, L6; Theme L2; Values L2, L4.*

McKee: a story is only as compelling as its forces of antagonism. Truby: give the opponent a strong but flawed moral argument and similarities to the hero; both sides want the same thing by different values. Schechter: the hero asks the thematic question, the villain states the thematic argument. Price: the anti-theme needs an equal weight of material that seems to prove it; the climax decides — change the ending and *Chinatown* becomes a story of integrity winning. McKee: progressions alternate between the value's positive and negative charge until the crisis.

**For Wirecut:** the verdict telling requires one counterargument beat "stated flatly, answered"; the books say that is a strawman by construction. The other side should be given its honest best as it presents itself, and the opposing material should be distributed across the film as swings — evidence surfaces, the inquiry stalls, a witness speaks, the file closes — so the outcome stays open until the film decides it. False balance is not the risk here: the swings must be the source's own reported reversals, weighted as the source weights them. The ending contract should say which value wins and by whose account (Price's four endings: the right value wins; the wrong value fails; the right value loses — social criticism; the wrong value wins — the cynical world).

**Check:** *Could a supporter of the opposing side recognise their argument in the film's version? Chart the value's charge beat by beat — does it swing or slope? Which of the four endings is this, and does the source say so?*

### Law 8 — A face on the stake
*Emotions L7, L8; Conflict L4, L8; Character L9.*

Schechter's stakes character: the person whose life will be most shattered by failure gives the goal a human face — saving the universe is impersonal, saving Leia is not. McKee: conflict at three levels (inner, personal, extra-personal), and complexity is all three at once. Edson: the adversary should be one individual; natural disasters cannot serve — which non-fiction must break, but the underlying law is *legibility*: the force must be seen acting.

**For Wirecut:** most tellings can run a whole film with no person in it. The books say every telling — helicopter, numbers, timeline, verdict — should carry one named person or community *from the source* whose fate the outcome decides, introduced before the midpoint and returned to at the close. This is not the portrait (the person does not carry the film); it is the face on the stake. Likewise the opposing force should act through the agent the source names, or at least be shown acting in a beat rather than summarised in an adjective. Grounding is the wall: the person must be named or specifically described in the source; a composite is fabrication, and where the source has no one, the film says so and stays systemic.

**Check:** *Who, in the source, loses if the answer is the bad one? Are they on screen before the midpoint and again at the end? Does the opposing force act through a named agent, or at least act on screen?*

### Law 9 — Character is choice under pressure; dimension is consistent contradiction; want and need diverge
*Character L1–L4, L6; Values L5.*

McKee: true character is revealed only by choice in dilemma; characterization is the mask. A dimension is a consistent, unresolved contradiction between traits or between a trait and a deeper self, and one defining dimension leads. Myers and McKee: the conscious want drives the plot, the unconscious need drives the change, and they often oppose. Truby: begin at the self-revelation and work back; ask what the hero is wrong about at the start. Myers: character = function; five functions suffice.

**For Wirecut:** these transfer to institutions as well as people, with one wall — the desk cannot invent an inner life, only report a documented one. So: characterise every actor by reported decisions under pressure, never by title or adjective; find the actor's documented contradiction (the bank that preaches restraint and prints) and state it as a pair the narrator never resolves; where the source gives an actor's stated aim and the thing it actually needed, let them diverge on screen. Cap the cast at three or four named actors, each with one function the viewer can hold (the one who wanted, the one who opposed, the one whose fate is the stake, the one who explains). Never assert a motive the source does not.

**Check:** *Is every characterisation a reported choice? What is the central actor's consistent contradiction, stated as a pair and left unresolved? Does the narration assert any motive the source does not?*

### Law 10 — Never explain; dramatise; state the meaning once, late, in the source's words
*Theme L1, L3; Values L1, L3; Emotions L9.*

McKee: master storytellers never explain; ideas are authenticated only by the consequences of choice and action; explanations "seriously diminish a film". The controlling idea is one sentence, value plus cause: "X ends [positive/negative] because Y" — and the cause tells you which scenes belong. Iglesias: state the theme as a question and wrap it in an emotion. McKee's declension: the contrary, the contradictory, the negation of the negation — the institution meant to prevent the harm committing it — is where depth lives.

**For Wirecut:** the SYNTHESIZE and CONCLUDE functions are where editorialising leaks. The film's meaning should be carried by the order and outcome of documented events; the narration may state it at most once, late, in the source's words. The controlling idea is the strictest grounding test at the level of meaning: if the source does not assert the cause, the film cannot. And when the source reports the negation of the negation, that is the deepest beat and belongs near the end, not in the hook.

**Check:** *Write the film's controlling idea as value + cause and quote the source sentence that asserts the cause. Strike every sentence that tells the viewer what events mean — does the film still mean it?*

### Law 11 — Orientation always, meaning later: threads, invisible transitions, sparing signposts, cause before effect
*Expositions L9, L12, L13; Scene L8; Character L9.*

Franklin: establish time, place, character, subject and mood in the first lines and re-establish each after every break; the best transition is unnoticed ("Meanwhile") or absent (an image that leaps). Weiland: readers may never have to ask "what's going on here?" — orientation questions close at once so dramatic questions can stay open; cause before effect in every line, and within a reaction, feeling then action then speech. Abumrad via the audio handbook: tell listeners what to attend to, never how to feel — a signpost or two at the true turns. Mercurio: end a unit on the question and cut to the *image* that answers it.

**For Wirecut:** the connective-tissue law covers the character and subject threads; the timeline's "date aloud" covers time. Generalise: after every change of altitude, era or place, restate the broken thread in the same breath; a colour-script phase change is a mood break and needs re-anchoring. Allow one or two spoken arrows ("then one detail changed everything") at the film's hardest turns — not meta, not a summary. Let a held question be answered by the next beat's picture where the picture can carry it.

**Check:** *At each movement boundary, which threads break and does the first line of the new movement restore them? Does any line state an effect before its cause? Does the film mark its hardest turn with one spoken arrow, and no more than two in total?*

### Law 12 — Density: the shorter version, the seconds on the decisive moment, every element doing two jobs
*Scene L6, L7; Expositions L8, L14; General L2; Synergies L4.*

Mercurio: story density is story per second; if the shorter version loses nothing, take it; a longer unit must carry a proportionally bigger change. Tom French via Hart: speed up the boring, slow down the exciting — the decisive moment gets the space, the shortest sentences, and a held pause before it. Howard & Mabley: separate the necessary from the unnecessary by asking what *caused* each effect, not what preceded it. The note-taker's own summary of the Synergies notebook: double or triple duty for elements = synergy.

**For Wirecut:** the clock and CUT passes shorten by arithmetic; density says *what* to cut — the beats whose change is smallest per second, and the single-job beats first. The pacing pass gains a second law beside breather economy: the film's longest beat should be its decisive one. Trace the beats backward from the close by the source's stated causes and keep only the chain.

**Check:** *Which beat is the longest, and is it the decisive moment? For each beat, how many structural jobs does it do (turn, reveal, plant, restore a thread, carry the motif)? Which do only one? Is each beat the source's stated cause of the next?*

## 3. The grounding boundary — what fiction craft cannot do here, and what it can

The books assume an author who can invent. Wirecut's law is that the film may not add facts, scenes, quotes, motives or causal claims the source does not contain. Almost every lesson above survives that law because it is about **order, selection, and position of knowledge**, none of which adds anything to the record:

| Fiction craft | Legal transfer | Illegal transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Invent a scene | Descend to a moment the source documents with time, place and actors | Stage a "typical" scene from vibes |
| Give the hero a flaw or motive | Report the actor's documented decision under pressure and stated contradiction | Assert a motive ("out of greed") the source does not |
| Create a stakes character | Choose the named person or community the source says bears the cost | Compose a representative victim |
| Withhold the answer | Reorder disclosures; open on effect before cause | Omit a fact the viewer needs for comprehension; imply an outcome the source denies |
| Dramatic irony | Use hindsight: what the record says actors believed at the time | Attribute beliefs the source does not report |
| Beef up the anti-theme | Give the source's opposing evidence its honest best, weighted as the source weights it | Manufacture symmetry the source does not contain |
| The second-act shape (dark/spark, nail, false victory) | Read which shape the record has | Bend the chronology toward the bigger payoff |
| Hero brings about the finale | Report who actually acted | Manufacture agency; make drift into decision |
| Sympathy triggers | Show the reported unfair injury, competence, kindness | Cast a real person as a victim beyond what is reported |

The books also offer the one honest response to a thin source: **make a shorter, plainer film and say so** (Hauge's outer-motivation cut; Franklin's economy; Bork's "punishing" test). A source with no turn is an explanation; a source with no person is systemic; a source with no answer leaves the question open only if the source itself does.

## 5. The consolidated script-editor's checklist

Twenty-four questions, in the order a bench would ask them. Each is LLM-judged against the plan and the source, never regex.

**Question and stake**
1. State the film's question in one sentence from the source. Which beat poses it, which test it, which answers it?
2. At which scales does the stake exist in the source: systemic, human, the viewer's own? Does the ending answer at least two?
3. Who, in the source, loses if the answer is the bad one? On screen before the midpoint and again at the end?

**Release**
4. For the three most important facts, which beat made the viewer want each before it arrived?
5. Which fact is the film's secret — the thing the actors did not want known or the reader would not guess — and is it last or nearly?
6. Is the destination (date, vote, verdict) telegraphed early while the answer is withheld?
7. Is there any beat after which no question is open? (That is the exit door.) Are more than two ever open at once?

**Ladder and threads**
8. For each movement: a particular thing in the source, or a claim about a class of things? Any movement on neither rung?
9. Does the film contain scenic narrative, and is it grounded in a source passage that describes process?
10. Where does the film stop the action to explain — at a moment where something hangs — and does it return?
11. At each movement boundary, which threads break (time, place, character, subject, mood) and does the next line restore them?

**Turns**
12. For each movement: which value, which charge at the top, which at the close?
13. Write the reveals in screen order. Does each outrank the last? Is the strongest last but one?
14. Is there a midpoint beat that shows the outcome's opposite or glimpses it early, echoed by the ending?
15. Is the beat before the film's turn the one where the wrong expectation is strongest?
16. What followed the lowest point in the source — reversal or further fall — and does the film say the true shape?
17. Is each movement's strongest sentence its last?

**Antagonism and meaning**
18. Could a supporter of the opposing side recognise their argument in the film's version of it?
19. Does the value's charge swing across the film or slope? Are the swings the source's own?
20. Write the controlling idea as value + cause; quote the source sentence that asserts the cause. Which of the four endings is this?
21. Strike every sentence that tells the viewer what events mean. Does the film still mean it?

**Character and cast**
22. Is every characterisation a reported choice under pressure? Does the narration assert any motive the source does not?
23. Name each actor's single function. Is anyone without one? Is there a documented contradiction at the centre, stated as a pair and unresolved?

**Density**
24. Which beat is longest — is it the decisive moment? Which beats do only one structural job?

## 6. Three ideas that deserve their own line

**Plot is the ordering of emotions** (Blacker via Iglesias). The "How should the story be told?" menu is a menu of *plots* — vehicles for the source's story, each producing a different sequence of feelings. The right criterion for ranking tellings is therefore not only "does the source contain the material" but "what sequence of emotions would this vehicle produce from this source, and is it the sequence the story deserves". See `APPROACH_AUDIT.md`.

**Hindsight is a gift.** Fiction has to manufacture dramatic irony. Non-fiction has it for free: the outcome is known, the actors did not know it. Told honestly — the record says what they believed — it is the strongest suspense engine available to a news video and the menu does not name it.

**The one-beat normal.** The books want a glimpse of the flow of life before the disruption *and* no throat-clearing. Both hold: one concrete beat of how things stood, drawn from the source, broken by the third beat. Wirecut's cold-open law should admit this device explicitly so it stops being smuggled in as "context".
