# On-screen type — the settled practice (read by the spine, the text planner and the judge)

On-screen text in a short narrated film exists for two things: **what the ear
cannot hold** (a name, a figure, a date, a place, a document) and **what the
eye must own** (the one claim the viewer screenshots). Everything else stays
off. Reference: `communications/ON_SCREEN_TEXT_PRACTICE_2026-09-02.md`.

## Featuring — what earns the screen

1. **Text where the ear loses, silence otherwise.** A name on first
   appearance, a figure the argument turns on, a verbatim quote that IS the
   evidence, a locator. A beat whose words the viewer can hold from the
   narration alone carries no card. Long textless stretches read as
   confidence, not as an unfinished film; two textless beats need no excuse.
2. **One idea per card, one figure per card.** Never two numbers on one
   screen; a figure arrives with its unit and what it counts, and its
   comparison beside it if it needs scale. A card is at most two lines.
3. **Names: first sighting, once.** The chip appears the first time a person
   or institution matters, name over role, holds at least three seconds, and
   does not return unless the film has left them for a long time. One chip
   at a time.
4. **Never duplicate a channel.** The card must not say what the narration is
   saying in the same words at that moment, and must not restate the burned
   subtitle line. Same meaning twice halves both. The title appears once, at
   the open; a kicker under it only ADDS (a dateline, a place, a series) —
   never the title's own words again.
5. **No decorative words.** Nothing the story does not earn ("BREAKING",
   "EXCLUSIVE", a slogan). Quiet furniture is set small.
6. **Adjacent cards differ.** Not two STATs in a row, not two CLAIMs in a
   row, unless the film's structure is the count (a numbers film).

## Style — how it is set

7. **Hierarchy: two sizes, two weights per screen.** The hero (figure or
   claim), the referent under it, the furniture. If everything is loud,
   nothing is. The size register moves the whole ladder, never one rung.
8. **Faces: one display face, one text face, different in class.** A
   condensed black beside a plain sans; a serif display beside a sans text.
   The faces stay constant through the film and change BETWEEN films with
   the film's register — a serif for the historical or literary piece, a
   condensed black for urgency, a rounded face for warmth. Sentence case for
   chips and claims; tracked capitals only for furniture; never letter-spaced
   lowercase; no all-caps beyond three or four words; no thin weights over
   busy footage.
9. **Colour: off-white ink, one accent, the counter-note.** The accent is
   chosen FROM the phase's world but AGAINST the footage's dominant hue at
   that moment — the paler, cooler or brighter member the picture does not
   saturate. Warm type on a warm scene is legible by its outline and
   invisible as a design: amber figures over an amber neon refinery, coral
   emphasis beside a coral neon shape, are the echo, not the counter-note.
   Avoid saturated pure red or pure blue for small type (video bleeds them).
   The outline and the plate deliver the contrast over moving footage; the
   accent must also read at 3:1 against the outline (checked).
10. **Placement is composition.** Negative space only — sky, wall, unbroken
    tone — never over a face or the focal action; the visual prompt keeps
    the chosen zone quiet. Lower thirds on one consistent side through a
    film; placement varies only for a reason. The bottom band is the
    subtitles'.
11. **Backing by register.** A shadow or thin outline for most films; a
    plate when the footage is busy or the register is institutional; never a
    plate that hides the picture the beat exists for.
12. **Motion: one grammar, short, still during the read.** The same enter
    and exit for every card, under a third of a second, no builds, no
    movement while the viewer reads; the card enters on the word it serves
    (or a beat before) and leaves before the cut.

## Who decides what

The spine declares the film's type mood (faces, register, backing) and the
accent per colour phase; the text planner decides per clip what is featured
and in which role and template, never a colour, a face or a size; the
compositor resolves and burns; the judge audits the finished frames against
these rules and names the fault — the echo accent, the duplicated channel,
the crowded hierarchy, the card that straddled a cut — with a one-click cure.
Arithmetic refuses only what arithmetic can see: the reading floor, the
bounds, the kicker that repeats the title, the accent below 3:1.
